Nvidia Microsoft and Meta urge US to avoid overregulating open-weight AI models

News time: 2026-07-25T04:25:14.756507+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a rare joint policy statement released in July 2026, Nvidia, Microsoft, and Meta warned U.S. policymakers against imposing sweeping regulations on open-weight AI models. The three tech giants argued that such models — whose trained parameters are publicly shared but not necessarily accompanied by full source code, training data, or usage licenses — are essential infrastructure for American AI leadership, academic research, startup innovation, and global competitiveness. Their letter, titled 'Open Weights and American AI Leadership', stresses that overregulation would disproportionately harm smaller developers and researchers while failing to meaningfully address security risks — which they say are better managed through targeted, risk-based approaches.

The companies emphasized that open-weight models differ fundamentally from fully open-source software: they make model weights available for inspection, fine-tuning, and deployment, but often retain proprietary training methodologies, data curation pipelines, and commercial licensing terms. This middle-ground approach has enabled rapid iteration across academia and industry — from university labs adapting Llama-derived models to startups building vertical AI tools on top of Qwen or Phi-3 weights. The letter explicitly rejects proposals that would require pre-deployment audits, export controls, or usage bans on models above certain size thresholds.

The timing reflects growing legislative momentum: several draft bills introduced in early 2026 propose mandatory safety certifications and usage restrictions for models with more than 10 billion parameters — criteria that would cover widely used open-weight models like Mixtral 8x22B and DeepSeek-V2. The coalition contends these measures conflate openness with risk, ignoring how transparency actually enables third-party red-teaming, bias auditing, and localized adaptation — capabilities closed models inherently lack.

Critics, however, point to real concerns: malicious actors have already repurposed open-weight models for disinformation campaigns and cyberattacks, and some jurisdictions (including the EU) are moving toward stricter accountability frameworks. Still, the letter insists that regulatory design must distinguish between model distribution and downstream misuse — just as laws govern firearms or encryption without banning public access to cryptographic algorithms. As one signatory noted, 'Open weights don’t hand someone a weapon; they hand them a blueprint — and society has long trusted engineers, educators, and auditors to use blueprints responsibly.'
