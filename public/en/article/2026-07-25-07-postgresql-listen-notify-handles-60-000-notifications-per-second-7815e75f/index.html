PostgreSQL LISTEN/NOTIFY handles 60,000 notifications per second

News time: 2026-07-25T04:25:14.756396+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new technical blog post from DBOS challenges a long-standing myth in the PostgreSQL community: that the built-in LISTEN/NOTIFY mechanism doesn’t scale well for high-throughput real-time workloads. By running rigorous, production-inspired benchmarks — including concurrent listeners, variable payload sizes, and sustained load over time — the authors demonstrate that PostgreSQL can reliably deliver over 60,000 notifications per second on modest hardware. This isn’t a synthetic microbenchmark; it reflects realistic usage patterns like fan-out to multiple services or event-driven coordination within a durable workflow engine.

The test setup used a single PostgreSQL 16 instance with standard configuration (no exotic tuning), connected via persistent libpq sessions. Notifications were sent using NOTIFY channel, 'payload', and received via LISTEN in separate client processes. Crucially, the benchmark measured end-to-end latency and throughput under load, not just peak burst capacity — and found consistent sub-10ms p95 latency even at 50K+ notifications/sec.

This result directly contradicts widely cited assumptions — including a recent, highly upvoted Hacker News thread titled “Postgres LISTEN/NOTIFY does not scale” — that treat LISTEN/NOTIFY as inherently unsuitable beyond toy-scale applications. The DBOS team argues that many perceived limitations stem not from PostgreSQL itself, but from misconfigured clients (e.g., unbounded connection pools), poor application-level batching, or conflating durability guarantees with throughput.

The implications extend beyond academic interest. Teams building durable workflows — like those powering email automation, multi-step approvals, or cross-service state synchronization — can now confidently lean on LISTEN/NOTIFY as a zero-dependency, ACID-aligned messaging layer. It eliminates the operational overhead of managing separate message brokers like RabbitMQ or Kafka, while preserving transactional consistency: notifications fire only after committed transactions, ensuring exactly-once semantics when paired with proper application logic.

That said, the post cautions against treating scalability as binary. As one commenter noted, “scale isn’t a yes/no question — it’s a continuum.” What works for 60K/sec may fall short for 600K/sec or global fan-out across regions. But for many mid-scale SaaS applications, embedded systems, or internal platforms, this performance ceiling is more than sufficient — and far simpler to operate.
