ML conferences’ fixed paper limits disadvantage theoretical work

News time: 2026-07-26T04:37:42.565736+00:00 · Source: reddit

A seasoned theoretical machine learning researcher has raised a pointed critique of longstanding publication norms at top-tier ML conferences like NeurIPS and ICML. In a widely shared Reddit post, they argue that rigid page limits — typically eight to nine pages for the main body — combined with unstated expectations about background knowledge, systematically disadvantage rigorous theoretical contributions. While appendices are officially ‘unlimited’, reviewers are explicitly instructed not to rely on them, creating a paradox: theory papers require careful setup of definitions, notation, and prior results, yet cannot fit that scaffolding into the constrained main text without triggering rejections for being ‘too difficult’ or ‘insufficiently self-contained’.

The author notes a striking shift in reviewer feedback over the past decade. Earlier rejections centered on substantive concerns — e.g., novelty gaps or missing baselines — whereas recent ones increasingly cite subjective readability issues: ‘the math is hard to follow’, ‘terminology isn’t explained’, or demands to fully re-derive cited work in the main paper. These critiques often ignore field-specific conventions: just as real analysis courses assume calculus fluency, ML theory assumes familiarity with measure theory, convex optimization, or concentration inequalities — knowledge no reasonable reviewer would expect a systems paper to re-teach. Yet reviewers rarely disclose their own knowledge gaps; instead, they penalize authors for not bridging them within arbitrary page constraints.

This tension reflects deeper structural mismatches. Unlike empirical work — where experiments, ablations, and visualizations can be tightly compressed — theoretical proofs scale nonlinearly with rigor. A clean lemma may need three pages of assumptions, counterexamples, and edge-case handling to avoid subtle errors. The current policy treats all papers as if they share the same pedagogical burden, despite vastly different epistemic starting points. The author isn’t asking for longer main papers — they’re advocating for an explicit, enforceable norm: reviewers should acknowledge their own prerequisites and refrain from demanding expository labor that belongs in textbooks or graduate syllabi.

Ultimately, the post is less a complaint than a call for intellectual honesty in peer review. It asks the community to confront an unspoken trade-off: enforcing uniform brevity may streamline reviewing, but at the cost of marginalizing work whose value lies in depth, not digestibility. If ML aims to sustain foundational progress — not just incremental engineering — its publication infrastructure must evolve beyond print-era constraints and citation-count mentalities.
