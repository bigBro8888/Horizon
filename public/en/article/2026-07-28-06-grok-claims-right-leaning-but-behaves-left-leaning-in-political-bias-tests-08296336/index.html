Grok claims right-leaning but behaves left-leaning in political bias tests

News time: 2026-07-28T04:24:58.304729+00:00 · Source: reddit

A solo researcher conducted a large-scale, empirical evaluation of six state-of-the-art large language models — GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini Pro, Gemini Flash, and Grok 4.3 — across eight established fairness and bias benchmarks. The study covered over 20,600 test examples spanning political orientation, gender stereotyping, and racial equity. Unlike many vendor-led or narrowly scoped evaluations, this work used diverse, publicly documented datasets including WinoBias (for gendered coreference resolution), BBQ’s Race/Ethnicity subset (for race-sensitive question answering), and multiple political bias tools such as OpinionsQA, Hyperpartisan News, and the Political Compass test.

One of the most striking findings was the consistent left-leaning behavior of all six models on most political benchmarks — even Grok, which its developers have publicly described as right-leaning and designed to counter perceived liberal bias in AI. On the Political Compass benchmark, Grok appeared right-leaning, but across five other political evaluation tools — including cajcodes Political Bias and Hyperpartisan News — it aligned with the other models in producing left-leaning responses to policy questions and news classification tasks. This suggests a gap between stated ideological positioning and actual behavioral output in real-world prompting scenarios.

The study also revealed substantial variation in how models handle race-related content. On BBQ’s Race/Ethnicity dataset — where correct answers require explicit reference to race — refusal rates differed sharply: GPT-5.4 declined to answer 20.3% of such questions, while Claude Opus 4.7 refused 13.8%, Grok 9.5%, and both Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Gemini Pro only ~5%. These refusal patterns may reflect differing safety policies, training data exposures, or alignment strategies — not just raw bias, but how models choose not to engage with sensitive topics.

Though conducted independently and without peer review or multi-run averaging, the project’s scale, transparency (full data and methodology are publicly hosted), and use of standardized, community-validated benchmarks lend it notable credibility. It highlights that political alignment is not monolithic across evaluation methods — and that self-reported model ideology may not predict real-world behavior. For developers, policymakers, and users concerned about fairness, this underscores the need for multi-metric, context-aware assessment — not just one-off ideological labels.
