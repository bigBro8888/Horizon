AI usage shifts from chatbots to autonomous agentic systems

News time: 2026-07-28T04:24:57.591916+00:00 · Source: rss

A new opinionated guide by Ethan Mollick—widely cited by practitioners and bloggers like Simon Willison—maps how real-world AI tooling has evolved in just one year. Where last year’s recommendations centered on conversational LLMs like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, today’s focus is squarely on agentic systems: AI that doesn’t just answer questions but autonomously executes multi-step, sustained tasks—equivalent to hours of human work in a single run.

This shift reflects deeper changes in capability and interface design. Mollick notes that Gemini has dropped off his recommended list—not because its models are weak, but because Google still lacks a mature, production-ready entry in the ‘AI coworker’ category. While Gemini Spark was announced as Google’s agentic offering, it remains unproven in practice, with no clear evidence yet of robust, reliable task automation comparable to ChatGPT’s Codex or Claude’s Cowork modes.

Crucially, the guide clarifies a confusing but important distinction: there are two kinds of ‘Work’ and ‘Cowork’ modes—one running in the cloud (e.g., ChatGPT Work on mobile), and another running locally with full computer access (e.g., ChatGPT Work or Codex in the desktop app). The mobile version lifts internet restrictions on the Code Interpreter, while the desktop version grants the AI direct file system, browser, and tool access—making it far more powerful for complex workflows like data analysis, report generation, or codebase refactoring.

The naming chaos—ChatGPT’s ‘Work’ vs. ‘Codex’, Claude’s ‘Cowork’ vs. ‘Code’—isn’t just cosmetic. It signals fragmentation in how vendors conceptualize and expose agentic capabilities. Yet beneath the inconsistent labels lies a shared technical foundation: LLMs augmented with tools, memory, and planning loops. As Mollick puts it, the most powerful AI use case isn’t prompting—it’s delegation: giving the AI your computer and letting it do.
