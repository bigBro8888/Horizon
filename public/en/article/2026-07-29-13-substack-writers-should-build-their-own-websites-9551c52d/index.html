Substack writers should build their own websites

News time: 2026-07-29T04:27:46.549150+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A growing number of independent writers are rethinking their reliance on Substack—not to abandon it, but to reclaim foundational control over their work. The core argument isn’t anti-Substack; it’s pro-ownership. Writers who publish exclusively on Substack hand over critical assets: their domain names, URL structures, SEO history, and long-term data portability. If Substack changes its terms, raises fees, or shuts down—or even just shifts its algorithm—those writers have little recourse. A personal website acts as the canonical source of truth: the original home for every article, with full control over design, metadata, archives, and future migration paths.

This isn’t about technical idealism—it’s pragmatic infrastructure. As one creator notes, hosting a newsletter on a subdomain like newsletter.yoursite.com lets them keep all URLs intact if they ever move off Substack. Others adopt hybrid workflows: publishing first on their own blog (where they retain full ownership), then syndicating weekly to Substack for email delivery—leveraging its robust subscriber management and monetization tools without surrendering editorial sovereignty.

The trade-offs are real and openly debated. Distribution remains the biggest hurdle: no self-hosted site automatically delivers content to readers’ inboxes. Substack solves that—and handles payments, analytics, and community features that would cost hundreds per month to replicate elsewhere. Yet critics warn that relying solely on platform-owned channels risks invisibility: organic traffic to standalone sites is scarce, and social media feeds rarely surface thoughtful long-form writing reliably.

Emerging alternatives reflect this tension. Projects like Leaflet.pub and Standard.site aim to bridge the gap—offering Substack-like ease while anchoring content in open protocols like AT Protocol. These tools signal a broader shift: creators increasingly demand both convenience and control. The takeaway isn’t ‘go fully independent’ or ‘stay on Substack forever.’ It’s ‘own your foundation, borrow distribution wisely.’
