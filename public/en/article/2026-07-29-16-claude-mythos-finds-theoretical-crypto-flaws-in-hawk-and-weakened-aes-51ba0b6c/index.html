Claude Mythos finds theoretical crypto flaws in HAWK and weakened AES

News time: 2026-07-29T04:27:46.158405+00:00 · Source: rss

Anthropic researchers have demonstrated a novel use of large language models in formal security analysis: using Claude Mythos Preview — a highly capable, restricted-access LLM — to uncover theoretical cryptographic weaknesses in two cipher designs. The work did not break real-world systems but identified subtle mathematical flaws in HAWK, a blockchain-oriented cryptographic protocol, and a deliberately weakened variant of AES-128. Crucially, the breakthrough wasn’t automatic — it required sustained, iterative prompt engineering over 60 hours of model runtime (estimated at $100,000 in API costs), with human researchers repeatedly steering the model away from premature surrender or trivial solutions.

The prompts shared publicly reveal the real challenge: getting an LLM to behave like a tenacious cryptanalyst. Phrases like 'the models tend to think it is impossible to solve so they don't try' and 'we want proper research to find genuinely hard findings' underscore how much effort went into coaxing rigorous reasoning rather than plausible-sounding guesses. Researchers emphasized publishing-worthy novelty — rejecting low-hanging fruit and insisting on attacks that advance formal understanding, not just exploit known shortcuts.

This effort also produced CryptanalysisBench, a new evaluation framework co-developed with ETH Zurich, Tel Aviv University, and the University of Haifa. Designed to measure LLMs’ ability to perform step-by-step cryptanalysis — from identifying algebraic structure to constructing distinguishers — the benchmark fills a gap in AI safety and formal methods testing. It treats cryptanalysis not as a black-box task but as a reasoning pipeline requiring precise mathematical intuition, domain knowledge, and persistence.

While neither flaw threatens deployed systems — the paper explicitly states 'neither of these results has a practical impact on today’s computer systems' — the methodology matters deeply. It shows that with careful prompting and expert supervision, frontier LLMs can augment human researchers in exploring complex, underconstrained mathematical spaces. This isn’t about replacing cryptographers; it’s about extending their reach into corners of design space where manual exploration is prohibitively expensive or time-consuming.
