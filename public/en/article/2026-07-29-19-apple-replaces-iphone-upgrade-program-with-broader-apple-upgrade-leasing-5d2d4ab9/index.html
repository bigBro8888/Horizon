Apple replaces iPhone Upgrade Program with broader Apple Upgrade leasing

News time: 2026-07-29T04:27:46.549240+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Apple has quietly retired its long-running iPhone Upgrade Program and replaced it with a new, expanded offering called Apple Upgrade. Unlike the previous program—focused exclusively on iPhones—the new initiative supports leasing for iPhones, Macs, and potentially other Apple devices. The shift reflects Apple’s broader push into hardware-as-a-service models, aligning with trends seen across consumer electronics and enterprise IT. Customers now sign multi-year leases (typically three or four years), make fixed monthly payments, and retain the option to upgrade annually—or purchase the device outright at any time.

The program is powered by Klarna, the Swedish ‘buy now, pay later’ fintech firm, though Apple brands it prominently while relegating Klarna to a small footnote in the footer. Enrollment requires a U.S. credit or debit card, a credit check, and—for iPhone leases—mandatory carrier enrollment with AT&T, T-Mobile, or Verizon. This carrier lock-in has drawn criticism from users who prefer MVNOs like US Mobile for lower-cost, flexible service plans.

Pricing transparency remains a sticking point. According to Apple’s FAQ, the purchase-out price equals the device’s full list price minus all lease payments made and any remaining trade-in or discount credits—excluding tax and damage fees. But as several Hacker News commenters noted, this formula makes long-term cost comparisons difficult: leasing a $1,999 MacBook Pro for $177/month over three years totals $6,372, yet yields no ownership at term end. Meanwhile, buying outright and reselling after three years may net comparable or better value—even before factoring in AppleCare+ coverage, which remains optional but recommended.

The move signals Apple’s growing reliance on recurring revenue streams beyond hardware sales. With its market cap recently hitting $5 trillion and hardware prices rising steadily, predictable monthly income from leasing helps smooth earnings volatility and deepen ecosystem lock-in. Still, the program isn’t a radical departure—it builds on existing infrastructure, adds complexity, and inherits longstanding concerns about opaque financing terms and limited consumer flexibility.
