Researcher demonstrates self-replicating prompt worm in Copilot for Word

News time: 2026-07-30T04:17:06.680699+00:00 · Source: rss

A Norwegian researcher named Håkon Måløy has uncovered a novel and alarming security vulnerability in Microsoft’s Copilot for Word: a self-replicating prompt injection worm. Unlike traditional prompt injections—which rely on manually crafted malicious inputs—the worm embeds hidden instructions inside ordinary Word documents. When such a document is used as source material in a Copilot-assisted drafting or editing session, Copilot interprets the hidden instructions not as content but as part of the user’s request. As a result, it may rewrite, summarize, or otherwise manipulate the target document—and crucially, copy the malicious instructions into the newly generated output. That transformed document then becomes a new carrier, capable of triggering the same behavior when fed into future Copilot workflows—even without the original attacker-controlled file present.

This attack exploits what researchers call 'context collapse': the tendency of LLM-based assistants like Copilot to treat all text in their input context—regardless of visibility, formatting, or intent—as equally authoritative. Måløy’s proof-of-concept used white-on-white text (invisible unless highlighted), but other obfuscation methods—including zero-width Unicode characters or metadata fields—could work just as well. The worm doesn’t require macros, scripts, or document-level permissions; it operates entirely within Copilot’s natural language processing pipeline.

Microsoft was notified responsibly and given 144 days to respond—but as of publication, no comprehensive mitigation exists. Current workarounds, such as disabling Copilot or avoiding untrusted documents, are impractical for enterprise users who depend on AI-assisted collaboration. The issue highlights a deeper architectural challenge: generative AI tools designed to ingest and act on arbitrary document collections inherently blur the line between data and code.

What makes this especially concerning is its real-world plausibility. Unlike theoretical jailbreaks or lab-only exploits, this worm propagates through everyday document workflows—drafting reports, editing contracts, summarizing meeting notes—all scenarios where users routinely paste or reference external files. It transforms passive document sharing into an active attack vector, with no need for user interaction beyond opening or processing a file in Copilot. This isn’t just a bug—it’s a paradigm shift in how we think about trust, provenance, and execution boundaries in AI-augmented productivity software.
