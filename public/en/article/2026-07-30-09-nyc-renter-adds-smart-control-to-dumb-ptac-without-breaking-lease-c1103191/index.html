NYC renter adds smart control to dumb PTAC without breaking lease

News time: 2026-07-30T04:17:07.064125+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a classic New York rental dilemma — stuck with a clunky, non-smart PTAC (packaged terminal air conditioner) but forbidden from modifying permanent fixtures — one developer found a clever workaround. Rather than replace the unit or risk violating lease terms, they built a low-intrusion automation system using a stepper motor, microcontroller, and 3D-printed bracket to physically rotate the unit’s mechanical temperature dial. The setup doesn’t touch wiring, plumbing, or internal electronics; it simply mimics manual knob-turning with precision and repeatability.

PTACs are ubiquitous in NYC apartments and hotels: self-contained, wall-mounted units that both heat and cool, often controlled by analog dials or basic push-button interfaces. Because they’re landlord-owned and hardwired into building infrastructure, renters rarely get permission to install smart thermostats or retrofit internal circuitry. This project sidesteps those constraints entirely — no drilling, no rewiring, no voided security deposit.

The hardware is deliberately minimal: an off-the-shelf stepper motor mounted via custom-printed brackets, driven by an ESP32 microcontroller running custom firmware. Temperature commands come via Wi-Fi — either through a simple web interface or integrated into Home Assistant. Crucially, the system avoids any software-level integration with the AC’s proprietary logic; instead, it treats the dial like a physical API — turning it left or right to set target temperature, then letting the unit’s built-in thermostat do the rest.

Commenters on the original post praised the elegance of this ‘mechanical API’ approach. One noted how HVAC systems already standardize on simple, open interfaces like thermostat terminals — suggesting similar analog/digital headers should exist on other appliances. Others pointed out that while PTACs feel outdated, their persistence reflects deeper urban realities: NYC building codes, energy infrastructure legacy, and economic incentives favoring durable, centrally maintained units over consumer-upgradable gear. A few even recommended switching to ESPHome for faster firmware deployment — highlighting how accessible the toolchain has become.

This isn’t just a hack for comfort. It’s a quiet act of tenant agency — reclaiming control over indoor climate without confrontation or compromise. And as IoT security concerns mount (with over 820,000 daily hacking attempts targeting smart devices in 2025), its air-gapped, purely mechanical interaction stands out as refreshingly secure — no cloud dependency, no firmware vulnerabilities, no remote exploit surface.
