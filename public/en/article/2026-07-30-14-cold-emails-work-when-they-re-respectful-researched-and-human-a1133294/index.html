Cold emails work when they’re respectful, researched, and human

News time: 2026-07-30T04:17:07.063994+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a digital world saturated with automated outreach and algorithm-driven networking, Zach Holman’s essay The Cold Email makes a quiet but compelling case: cold emailing—when done thoughtfully—is not dead. It’s underused, misunderstood, and often unfairly dismissed as spammy or desperate. Holman draws from years of personal experience sending and receiving such messages—not as a growth-hacking tactic, but as a sincere attempt to connect, ask questions, or open doors. His core argument is simple: most people respond not to clever subject lines or templates, but to evidence that you’ve taken time to understand who they are and why your message matters to them.

He emphasizes three non-negotiable principles: do your research (read their blog, study their work), keep it short (no more than four sentences), and make the ask specific and low-friction (e.g., “Could I ask one follow-up question?” rather than “Can we meet for coffee?”). Crucially, he treats cold email not as a transactional tool but as an act of professional courtesy—one that mirrors how people used to write cover letters or send handwritten notes before LinkedIn and ATS systems reshaped hiring norms.

The essay resonates because it reframes cold outreach as relational rather than extractive. Holman shares examples where recipients—like Erlang co-creator Joe Armstrong—responded with surprising generosity, not because the sender was impressive, but because the email signaled genuine curiosity and respect. This stands in stark contrast to today’s default: mass-sent, AI-generated messages that prioritize volume over voice.

What makes the piece especially timely is its quiet pushback against platform dependency. As job seekers increasingly rely on black-box algorithms and opaque networks, Holman reminds readers that direct, human-to-human contact remains accessible—and often more effective—than waiting for a profile view or connection request to land. The takeaway isn’t that cold email guarantees results, but that treating people like people still works—even in 2024.
