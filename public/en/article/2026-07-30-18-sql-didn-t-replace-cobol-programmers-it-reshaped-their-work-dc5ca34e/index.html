SQL didn’t replace COBOL programmers—it reshaped their work

News time: 2026-07-30T04:17:06.680652+00:00 · Source: rss

D. Richard Hipp—creator of SQLite and a veteran database architect—offers a quietly powerful historical analogy that cuts through today’s AI-driven anxiety about developer obsolescence. In a recent talk, he recalls how SQL transformed data access in the 1970s and 1980s: before SQL, querying large datasets required writing verbose, error-prone COBOL programs by hand—often by specialists paid premium rates. These programmers weren’t just coders; they were domain experts who understood file structures, record layouts, and batch processing logic.

Then came SQL: a declarative language that let users describe what data they needed—not how to fetch it. A single SELECT statement could replace hundreds of lines of COBOL. Crucially, Hipp stresses this didn’t eliminate programming jobs. Instead, it shifted focus—from low-level data navigation to higher-level concerns like schema design, query optimization, application integration, and business logic.

His point isn’t nostalgia—it’s a lens for today’s moment. As LLMs begin generating boilerplate code, API integrations, and even test suites, many fear automation will displace developers. But Hipp’s analogy suggests a different trajectory: abstraction layers don’t erase roles—they redefine them. The COBOL programmer didn’t vanish; they became database designers, SQL tuners, or systems analysts. Similarly, today’s frontend or backend engineer may spend less time wiring REST calls and more time refining prompts, auditing AI outputs, or architecting resilient hybrid workflows.

This reframing matters because it redirects energy from existential dread toward skill evolution. Rather than asking ‘Will AI take my job?’, Hipp invites us to ask ‘What new responsibilities emerge when this layer becomes invisible?’ The answer lies not in resisting abstraction—but in learning to operate one level up.
