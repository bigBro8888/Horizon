NeurIPS authors report reviewers ignoring rebuttals

News time: 2026-07-30T04:17:07.285224+00:00 · Source: reddit

A growing number of NeurIPS authors are voicing frustration over a recurring problem: reviewers failing to read or respond to author rebuttals during the conference’s discussion phase. In a widely discussed Reddit post, one author described how reviewers often go silent after initial reviews—leaving authors unable to clarify misunderstandings, correct factual errors, or address methodological concerns. This lack of engagement undermines the core purpose of the rebuttal period: not as a formality, but as a live, two-way dialogue meant to improve paper quality and review fairness.

The issue isn’t new—but its persistence is alarming. NeurIPS, like other top ML conferences, relies heavily on open peer review via OpenReview, where authors and reviewers exchange comments publicly. Yet many reviewers treat the rebuttal window as optional rather than integral. Some authors report submitting detailed, point-by-point responses only to receive no follow-up—even when those responses directly correct misinterpretations of their methodology or results.

Proposed solutions range from gentle nudges to structural accountability. The original poster suggested explicitly prompting reviewers with polite, public comments like “We’re happy to clarify any points—please let us know.” Others have floated stronger measures: tying reviewer performance to consequences for their own submissions. Notably, NeurIPS has already implemented such logic for Area Chairs (ACs)—in 2026, ACs who missed meta-review deadlines had their access suspended until tasks were completed, and in 2025, late meta-reviews led to withheld scores for AC-submitted papers.

While enforcement remains inconsistent, the conversation reflects deeper tensions in academic publishing: rising submission volumes, reviewer fatigue, and misaligned incentives. Without meaningful feedback loops, rebuttals risk becoming performative rituals rather than substantive exchanges. For early-career researchers especially, opaque or unresponsive reviews can delay publication, distort career trajectories, and erode trust in the system. As NeurIPS continues refining its review cycle—including pilots like pre-response meta-reviews—the community is pushing for accountability that goes beyond goodwill.
