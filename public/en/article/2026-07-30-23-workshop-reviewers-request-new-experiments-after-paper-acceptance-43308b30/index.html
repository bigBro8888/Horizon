Workshop reviewers request new experiments after paper acceptance

News time: 2026-07-30T04:17:07.285096+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher recently shared a puzzling situation on Reddit’s r/MachineLearning: their paper was accepted to a workshop co-located with a top-tier machine learning conference, yet reviewers later asked for additional experiments—despite there being no formal revision or second review phase. The only remaining step is submitting the camera-ready version, meaning these requests won’t be re-evaluated by reviewers or factored into the acceptance decision. This creates a procedural gray zone: reviewers are offering suggestions that carry weight but lack accountability or follow-up, raising questions about fairness, transparency, and workload expectations.

Workshops at major conferences like NeurIPS, ICML, or KDD often operate under looser timelines and lighter review processes than main conferences. Many use single-round review—acceptance is final, and camera-ready revisions are typically limited to minor fixes (typos, formatting, citation updates). Unlike journals or venues like TMLR—which support multi-round revision histories and explicit revision tracking—most ML workshops don’t build in mechanisms to assess post-acceptance changes. As one KDD 2026 workshop page notes, camera-ready revision is open, but it’s framed as an opportunity for polishing, not substantive scientific expansion.

The core tension lies in reviewer intent versus process design. Some reviewers may genuinely want to strengthen the work before presentation; others may treat the camera-ready stage as a de facto revision round—blurring lines between feedback and obligation. For authors, complying risks unpaid labor and timeline pressure; ignoring requests risks perceived uncooperativeness or future reviewer bias. There’s no universal policy, so outcomes depend heavily on workshop chairs’ guidance and norms within specific communities.

This isn’t about rejecting feedback—it’s about aligning expectations with process. Ethical peer review requires clarity: if reviewers expect changes, the venue must provide either a formal revision round or clear guidelines on what’s optional versus expected. Without that, authors face asymmetrical responsibility: they bear the cost of extra work, while reviewers face no consequence for unreviewed suggestions. As machine learning research accelerates, maintaining trust in workshop evaluation hinges on making such informal practices visible, discussable, and equitable.
