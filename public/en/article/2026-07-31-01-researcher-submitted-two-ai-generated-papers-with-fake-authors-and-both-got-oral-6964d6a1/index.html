Researcher submitted two AI-generated papers with fake authors—and both got oral slots

News time: 2026-07-31T04:36:00.263460+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A researcher recently conducted a sobering experiment: they authored and submitted two entirely AI-generated research papers—complete with invented author names, affiliations, and biographies—to major AI conferences. Both papers were accepted as oral presentations, one at NeurIPS and another at ICML. The submissions contained no real human authors, no empirical results, and no novel methodology—yet reviewers assigned high scores, praised their 'clarity' and 'technical soundness', and recommended them for prestigious speaking slots.

The experiment was not meant to deceive for personal gain, but to stress-test the current peer review pipeline. As the researcher explains in their blog post, the goal was to probe how easily AI-generated content could bypass human scrutiny—especially when reviewers themselves are overburdened, time-pressed, and increasingly reliant on surface-level signals like polished prose, plausible equations, and familiar terminology. The fact that both papers passed rigorous selection for oral presentation—a format reserved for the top ~5% of accepted work—suggests systemic vulnerabilities far deeper than occasional sloppy reviewing.

This isn’t just about bad papers slipping through. It reflects an accelerating feedback loop: AI writes papers, AI-assisted tools help reviewers skim them faster, and AI-powered summarizers digest them for readers—all while human oversight recedes. NeurIPS’ ongoing AI-assisted review experiment (launched for its 2026 conference) exemplifies this trend: reviewers receive AI-generated summaries and critique suggestions, raising concerns about circular validation. As one commenter notes, we’re entering an era where 'papers are written by AI, reviewed by AI, and read by AI'—with diminishing human grounding at each stage.

The implications extend beyond credibility. If oral slots—the gold standard for visibility, hiring, and funding—can be secured by synthetic content, early-career researchers face unfair competition, reproducibility erodes further, and trust in the scientific record weakens. The researcher proposes concrete countermeasures: mandatory author identity verification (e.g., ORCID + institutional email), stricter requirements for code and data release, and using LLMs transparently in review—not to replace judgment, but to flag inconsistencies, detect hallucinated citations, or cross-check claims against known literature. As one HN commenter put it: 'Fight fire with fire—but with guardrails.'
