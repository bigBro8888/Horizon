AI professor loses three PhD candidates to conference review stress

News time: 2026-07-31T04:35:59.707634+00:00 · Source: reddit

An early-career assistant professor in AI research has publicly shared a sobering experience: over the past few years, they lost three undergraduate researchers — and nearly a fourth — as prospective PhD students, not due to lack of talent or interest in research, but because of deep disillusionment with the conference paper submission process. These students had worked closely with the professor on substantive parts of ongoing research projects — not superficial course assignments — producing results the professor describes as 'well above the bar' for top-tier venues. With over a decade of reviewing and publishing experience at the field’s most selective conferences — often referred to informally as the 'big three' — the professor judged the work as strong, and reviewers agreed: one paper received four unanimous 'weak accept' recommendations, yet was still rejected.

The frustration stems not from poor reviews, but from the perceived arbitrariness of successive rounds. After addressing every concern raised in one round, resubmissions triggered new, seemingly random critiques — not about core flaws, but about minor stylistic choices, framing decisions, or speculative weaknesses that weren’t flagged earlier. The professor likens this to a shifting target: when a paper has clear issues, fixes satisfy reviewers; when it’s solid, reviewers begin scrutinizing arbitrary details. This unpredictability eroded students’ faith in the system — one told them outright, 'I don’t like dealing with the paper reviewers,' and another simply refused to play 'this game' altogether.

What makes this especially consequential is that these were not marginal cases. The students were highly capable, deeply engaged, and already contributing meaningfully to real research. Their withdrawal reflects a broader pipeline problem: talented undergraduates who thrive in collaborative, hands-on research settings are turning away from PhD paths not because they dislike science, but because they see academic publishing as an opaque, high-stakes lottery disconnected from scientific merit. The professor admits they now struggle to ethically encourage students into a system they themselves find increasingly indefensible.

This isn’t just about rejection — it’s about how evaluation culture shapes career choices. When the gatekeeping mechanism feels less like peer assessment and more like ritualized gatekeeping, promising minds exit before they even enter. As AI research grows more competitive and publication pressure intensifies, such attrition risks narrowing the field’s future diversity of thought, background, and approach — unless structural reforms address transparency, consistency, and mentorship support around the review process.
