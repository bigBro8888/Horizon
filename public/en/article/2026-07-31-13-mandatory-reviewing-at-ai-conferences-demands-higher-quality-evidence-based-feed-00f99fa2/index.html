Mandatory reviewing at AI conferences demands higher-quality, evidence-based feedback

News time: 2026-07-31T04:35:59.707902+00:00 · Source: reddit

A growing number of top-tier AI and machine learning conferences—including NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR—have adopted mandatory reviewing policies: authors must complete a set number of peer reviews to submit their own papers. This shift transforms reviewing from voluntary service into a professional obligation rooted in reciprocity. As one researcher argues on Reddit, this change fundamentally alters the ethical expectations placed on reviewers: if researchers demand careful, thoughtful evaluation of their own work, they must provide the same rigor when assessing others’ submissions.

The core critique centers not on the existence of negative feedback—but on its vagueness. Phrases like “novelty is limited” or “comparison with prior work is insufficient” appear frequently in reviews, yet often lack concrete grounding. A meaningful review, the post insists, should cite specific papers (e.g., “Method A in [Author et al., 2023] shares this architecture”), identify precise gaps (e.g., “No ablation study isolates the effect of Module X”), or explain why a particular experiment is essential given the claimed contribution. Without such specificity, criticism becomes unactionable—and indistinguishable from superficial judgment by non-experts.

This isn’t about demanding exhaustive mentorship from every reviewer. Rather, it’s about minimum professional standards: when assigning a borderline or reject score, reviewers owe authors clear reasoning—not just a checklist of perceived omissions. A two-sentence review stating “lacks novelty” and “insufficient experiments” fails that standard. Worse, under mandatory systems, such low-effort reviews waste authors’ time, distort editorial decisions, and erode trust in the process—especially since reviewers are no longer unpaid volunteers but participants fulfilling a required exchange.

Conferences now face a practical challenge: how to enforce quality without overburdening reviewers or creating bureaucratic overhead. Some emerging tools—like Reciprocal Reviews—use token-based incentives to reward timely, substantive reviews. Others propose lightweight rubrics or post-submission review audits. Ultimately, the argument calls for systemic accountability: evaluating not just whether reviews were submitted, but how well they were written—because peer review, when institutionalized as obligation, must also be held to professional standards.
