Researcher trains LSTM-MDN to mimic human mouse movement

News time: 2026-07-31T04:35:59.708009+00:00 · Source: reddit

A developer has built a neural network that moves a computer mouse in ways indistinguishable from real human behavior — not for automation, but to probe the limits of modern bot detection. The model, shared on Reddit and hosted on GitHub, uses a two-layer Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network paired with a Mixture Density Network (MDN) output layer to generate smooth, variable, and contextually plausible cursor trajectories. Unlike deterministic models that output fixed coordinates, the MDN allows the system to express uncertainty and produce diverse, non-repetitive paths — a key trait of natural human motion.

The project was inspired by Precursor, Cloudflare’s newly launched behavioral validation engine that monitors full-session mouse dynamics to detect bots. Precursor goes beyond traditional browser fingerprinting or CAPTCHA challenges by analyzing timing, acceleration, hesitation, and micro-movements over time. Faced with such sophisticated detection, the researcher set out to see whether a relatively lightweight recurrent model could learn and reproduce those subtle patterns — and succeeded well enough to fool basic behavioral heuristics.

Training data came from real user sessions, likely captured via browser instrumentation or open mouse-tracking datasets. The LSTM processes sequences of (x, y, Δt) inputs — position deltas and time intervals — while the MDN outputs parameters for a mixture of Gaussian distributions, enabling probabilistic sampling of the next cursor position. This architecture avoids hard-coded rules and instead learns statistical regularities: how humans accelerate when targeting icons, pause before clicking, or make small corrections mid-motion. The resulting video demo shows fluid, organic-looking movement — no robotic straight lines or unnaturally precise arcs.

This isn’t about building better bots; it’s a stress test for behavioral biometrics. As services increasingly rely on mouse dynamics as a signal of humanness, models like this expose where those signals may be brittle or overfit. It also highlights the arms race between detection and evasion — one that hinges less on raw compute and more on modeling nuance. For developers working on bot management or accessibility tools, the work offers both a caution and a blueprint: human-like behavior is learnable, but only if you model its inherent randomness and intentionality together.
