Developer builds transformer model to predict blood sugar for diabetes management

News time: 2026-08-01T04:33:02.896700+00:00 · Source: reddit

A solo developer has built and open-sourced an encoder-only transformer model designed specifically to forecast future blood glucose levels in people with type 1 diabetes. Unlike generic time-series models, it ingests both historical data — including past glucose readings, insulin doses, and carbohydrate intake — and planned future inputs like announced meals and insulin boluses. This dual-context design allows the model to simulate how upcoming interventions will affect glycemic trajectories over the next two hours — a clinically critical window for avoiding hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia.

The architecture draws inspiration from BERT: it uses bidirectional self-attention but masks future glucose values during training, ensuring predictions remain causal. Notably, the model never consumes explicit timestamps — instead, it learns temporal structure implicitly from the sequence of events. Context length is flexible (8–24 hours), and the model can operate autoregressively to extend forecasts beyond two hours. Four model sizes — nano to large — were trained, with the largest containing ~17 million parameters across 16 layers and 16 attention heads.

What sets this work apart is its clinical grounding. All glucose values are transformed into Kovatchev risk space — a nonlinear scale that asymmetrically penalizes low glucose more heavily than high glucose, reflecting real-world clinical priorities. Training leverages three specialized loss functions: DILATE loss to preserve the shape and timing of glucose excursions (e.g., sharp drops after meals), pinball loss to calibrate prediction intervals, and Kendall-Gal uncertainty-aware mixing to balance these competing objectives without manual tuning. Pretraining on synthetic data took ~48 hours; fine-tuning on real-world datasets like OhioT1DM required under 10 minutes.

The model is already running on the developer’s phone using their personal data — a rare example of closed-loop personal health ML in practice. While it currently requires manually announced carbs and insulin (not yet automated detection), the MIT-licensed code, pretrained weights, and evaluation data are publicly available. This bridges a gap between academic time-series research and tangible, user-owned health tools — offering not just predictions, but a framework for building clinically meaningful AI in resource-constrained settings.
