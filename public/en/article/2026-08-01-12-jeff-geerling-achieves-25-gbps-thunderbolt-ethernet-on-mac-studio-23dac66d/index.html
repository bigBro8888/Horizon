Jeff Geerling achieves ~25 Gbps Thunderbolt Ethernet on Mac Studio

News time: 2026-08-01T04:33:02.670103+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a detailed hands-on guide, Jeff Geerling demonstrates how to achieve sustained ~25 Gbps Ethernet throughput on Apple’s Mac Studio using Sonnet’s Twin 25G Thunderbolt adapter — a rare feat given macOS’s historical limitations with high-speed Thunderbolt networking. Unlike standard USB-C or built-in Ethernet, this setup leverages Thunderbolt 4’s full bandwidth and Sonnet’s dual SFP28 ports to bypass the typical 10 Gbps ceiling imposed by Apple’s integrated controllers and drivers.

Geerling’s testing shows real-world bidirectional speeds peaking near 27 Gbps — slightly above the nominal 25 Gbps spec — using iperf3 over a direct fiber link to a Linux server with a compatible 25GbE NIC. However, he stresses that macOS itself remains the bottleneck: native support for RDMA (Remote Direct Memory Access) and SMB Direct — technologies critical for low-latency, high-throughput file sharing — is absent. As a result, even with raw link speed, file transfers via SMB or AFP top out well below line rate.

The hardware choice carries tradeoffs. Sonnet’s Twin 25G is bus-powered and compact, but delivers only 15W upstream power — insufficient to charge most modern MacBooks while simultaneously handling full-bandwidth Ethernet. Users with dual-port laptops (e.g., M-series MacBook Air or Pro) may find themselves sacrificing charging capability unless they add a separate power adapter. Still, Geerling notes the plug-and-play simplicity: no drivers, no kernel extensions, no eGPU enclosure — just Thunderbolt plug-in and immediate 25 Gbps interface detection.

Community feedback reinforces these engineering realities. One user confirms hitting 27 Gbps but laments the 15W power limit; another proposes cheaper alternatives like repurposing PCIe NICs in fan-cooled eGPU enclosures; and several point squarely at macOS’s lack of SMB Direct as the root cause of poor real-world file transfer performance. The consensus isn’t about whether 25 Gbps is possible — it is — but whether the cost, complexity, and OS-level constraints justify it for most workflows.
