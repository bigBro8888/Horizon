Practitioner seeks efficient text-existence detector for 2D art images

News time: 2026-08-01T04:33:02.896946+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning practitioner has posted on Reddit asking for architectural guidance on a deceptively simple but practically important computer vision task: binary classification of whether any text appears at all in an image. Unlike full OCR or text localization—which require bounding boxes or character-level recognition—this is purely a yes/no decision. The user’s target domain is high-resolution (1920×1080), stylistically diverse 2D artwork, where text may appear at wildly varying scales (e.g., tiny watermark vs. bold title banner) and visual styles (hand-drawn, pixel-art, photorealistic overlays). This makes robust detection challenging without expensive per-character supervision.

The post highlights a key gap: while object detection models like Faster R-CNN routinely use Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) to handle multi-scale objects, FPN is rarely adopted in pure classification pipelines—even though scale variation is equally critical here. The user considers adapting PaddleOCR v6’s lightweight LCNetv4 backbone (designed for edge and browser deployment) and fine-tuning it for their domain. They weigh three architectural strategies: (1) a grid-based approach—dividing the feature map into cells and aggregating per-cell predictions—but notes this fails when only image-level yes/no labels are available; (2) global average pooling; and (3) global max pooling, which may better preserve presence signals from sparse text regions.

Crucially, the user lacks bounding-box annotations—a common constraint in real-world industrial or artistic datasets. This weak supervision setting forces trade-offs: grid methods need cell-level labels, while global pooling works with image-level labels but risks losing spatial sensitivity. They’re also curious why FPN isn’t standard in classification—despite its proven ability to fuse multi-scale features—and whether top-down lateral connections could meaningfully boost recall for tiny or stylized text.

This question taps into broader trends in efficient vision modeling: as OCR systems mature, downstream tasks like text presence screening gain importance for preprocessing pipelines (e.g., filtering text-heavy assets before layout analysis or copyright checks). Lightweight, label-efficient architectures—especially those transferable from detection backbones—are increasingly valuable for embedded, real-time, or privacy-sensitive applications where full OCR is overkill. The community response, though not visible in the post, would likely explore hybrid pooling schemes, attention-guided pooling, or even distillation from stronger detectors—all grounded in practical constraints rather than theoretical novelty.
