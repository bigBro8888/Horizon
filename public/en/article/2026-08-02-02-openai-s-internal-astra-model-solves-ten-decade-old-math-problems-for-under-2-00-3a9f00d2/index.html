OpenAI’s internal Astra model solves ten decade-old math problems for under $2,000 each

News time: 2026-08-02T04:34:48.929137+00:00 · Source: rss

OpenAI has announced that an internal version of its upcoming Astra model solved ten long-standing open problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science—each with no major progress for at least ten years. The company reports spending less than $2,000 per problem using GPT-5.6 Sol token pricing, a dramatic cost reduction compared to Anthropic’s recent $100,000 effort to uncover cryptographic weaknesses with Claude. Unlike prior AI-assisted proofs that relied on human-guided scaffolding or narrow domain tools, OpenAI’s approach produced fully formalized Lean 4 proofs, published openly in the github.com/openai/ten-proofs repository.

The results span diverse areas: geometry, complexity theory, cryptography, and combinatorics—including a disproof of the Erdős unit distance conjecture, a problem open since the 1940s. Accompanying the code is a peer-accessible paper detailing each solution, plus an LLM-generated ‘reasoning walkthrough’ PDF reconstructing how the model arrived at key insights—based on unpublished internal reasoning traces. While OpenAI has not released the exact prompts or failure rates (e.g., how many unsolved attempts occurred before success), the transparency around formalization and cost metrics sets a new benchmark for reproducibility in AI-driven theorem proving.

This development echoes Terence Tao’s vision of ‘big mathematics’: a collaborative ecosystem where humans focus on high-level intuition and problem framing, while AI handles exhaustive search, verification, and technical execution. Mathematicians are reacting with both excitement and unease—Kirwin Hampshire’s recent essay ‘The Dark Night of Mathematics’ captures a growing sense of existential reckoning, likening the moment to Deep Blue’s 1997 chess victory over Kasparov. Yet unlike chess, mathematics prizes not just correctness but insight; whether AI-generated proofs can yield new conceptual frameworks—not just answers—remains an open question.

Still, the implications extend beyond academia. If formal reasoning at this scale becomes affordable and reliable, it could accelerate verification of cryptographic protocols, compiler correctness, and safety-critical system specifications. The $2,000-per-proof threshold suggests such capabilities may soon be accessible to research labs and even advanced students—potentially democratizing deep formal reasoning previously reserved for elite teams with massive compute budgets.
