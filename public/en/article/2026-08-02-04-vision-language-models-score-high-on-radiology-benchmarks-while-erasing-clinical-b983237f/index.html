Vision-language models score high on radiology benchmarks while erasing clinical terms

News time: 2026-08-02T04:34:49.685067+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new study exposes a dangerous gap in how vision-language models (VLMs) are evaluated for radiology report generation (RRG). Researchers found that widely used benchmark metrics — such as BLEU, METEOR, and CIDEr — consistently reward outputs that are generic, repetitive, and clinically bland. These models often generate reports that sound plausible but omit rare yet critical findings like 'pneumothorax', 'interstitial edema', or 'pleural effusion', replacing them with vague phrases like 'no acute cardiopulmonary abnormality' — even when the chest X-ray clearly shows pathology. Worse, the models sometimes introduce biased or stereotyped language, such as overusing gendered assumptions ('female patient presents with…') or defaulting to 'normal' descriptions regardless of image evidence.

This isn’t just stylistic drift — it’s a systemic failure of evaluation design. The paper demonstrates that current metrics treat clinical precision as optional: they prioritize lexical overlap with reference reports, which themselves often contain template-driven, low-variance language. As a result, models learn to 'game' the system by deleting uncommon but diagnostically essential terms — a phenomenon the authors term 'clinical silence'. This erasure goes undetected because standard metrics cannot distinguish between a truly normal report and one that simply avoids saying anything meaningful.

To address this, the researchers propose a new validation framework called Terminology Erasure & Bias Scoring (TEBS). TEBS quantifies how much clinically relevant terminology is dropped from generated reports compared to ground-truth references — using curated medical lexicons like RadLex and SNOMED CT — and separately measures the introduction of non-evidence-based, biased, or templated phrases. It also incorporates clinician-in-the-loop validation, where radiologists rate whether omissions meaningfully degrade diagnostic utility.

The implications extend beyond radiology. If benchmark scores mislead developers and regulators into believing models are clinically safe, real-world deployment could delay diagnoses, reinforce biases, or erode trust in AI-assisted documentation. The study urges rethinking not just what we measure, but why: validation metrics must reflect clinical impact, not just linguistic similarity. As AI moves toward integration in hospital workflows, measuring what models don’t say may be as vital as measuring what they do.
