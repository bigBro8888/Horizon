People reject AI assistants contacting them on behalf of coworkers

News time: 2026-08-02T04:34:48.929083+00:00 · Source: rss

At OpenAI, a quiet but telling workplace experiment has surfaced: many employees connect ChatGPT to Slack for automation—but when a coworker’s AI assistant messages someone directly to request help, the response is often negative. Greg Brockman, OpenAI’s President and Co-Founder, observed this firsthand and shared a concise yet powerful insight on social media: people consistently resist being contacted by another person’s AI, even for tasks they’d readily assist with if asked face-to-face or via direct message.

This isn’t about capability—it’s about context and connection. The same person who would happily review a document, debug a script, or clarify a project timeline when pinged by a colleague becomes hesitant or annoyed when that same request arrives from an AI agent labeled as “Alex’s ChatGPT.” The friction isn’t technical; it’s social. It reveals how deeply human interaction is woven into professional trust, reciprocity, and psychological safety.

Brockman’s observation cuts against a common assumption in AI product design: that automating communication is inherently efficient. In reality, inserting AI as an intermediary can erode relational cues—tone, timing, intent—that humans rely on to interpret goodwill and urgency. When AI acts on behalf of someone without their active, moment-to-moment oversight, it risks flattening nuance and making requests feel transactional, impersonal, or even invasive.

The implication is clear for developers, product managers, and enterprise IT teams: AI tools shouldn’t just mimic human workflows—they must respect the social architecture of work. Designing AI to augment rather than replace human contact means prioritizing transparency (e.g., clearly signaling when a human is actively guiding the AI), preserving opt-in consent, and avoiding autonomous outreach unless explicitly authorized. As Brockman puts it, people want AI to “give time back—or enhance time together—not become a layer separating people.” That distinction is ethical, practical, and profoundly human.
