AI financial advice works well—if you ask the right questions

News time: 2026-08-02T04:34:49.418303+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A recent MIT Sloan study reveals that large language models (LLMs) can deliver surprisingly competent financial advice—but only when users frame their questions thoughtfully. The research tested AI responses against benchmarks like the CFP Board’s standards and found strong alignment on core principles: diversification, low-cost index investing, tax-advantaged accounts, and avoiding emotional or speculative decisions. Yet performance dropped sharply when questions involved personal context—job loss, caregiving responsibilities, or multi-generational wealth goals—where human advisors still hold clear advantages.

This isn’t just about model capability; it’s about user readiness. As Hacker News commenters point out, widespread financial illiteracy means many people don’t know what to ask—or worse, ask dangerously misguided questions. One commenter recounted being urged to open savings accounts for toddlers “to earn compound interest over 18 years,” while others cited real-world examples of people treating meme coins or collectibles as legitimate long-term investments. These aren’t edge cases—they reflect a foundational gap between what AI can answer and what users actually understand.

The study also highlights structural limitations in how AI advice is evaluated. Most tests use one-shot, context-free prompts without memory or personalized history—unlike real-life financial planning, which evolves across years and life events. Some commenters suggest prompting techniques like assigning AI ‘skin in the game’ (e.g., “You’ll be held liable if this advice fails”) might improve risk awareness, though no empirical validation yet exists. Others note that finance, unlike software engineering, has broad consensus on best practices—making it unusually amenable to LLM assistance.

Still, adoption faces friction beyond accuracy. Financial planners routinely charge $2,000 for Statements of Advice (SoAs) that are largely boilerplate—a task AI already handles well. Yet trust, liability, and regulatory compliance remain unresolved. Tools like Fable—fed live Monarch Money data—are already helping users rebalance portfolios and evaluate major life purchases. The future isn’t AI replacing advisors, but reshaping roles: humans focusing on empathy, ethics, and complex trade-offs, while AI handles scalable, rules-based guidance.
