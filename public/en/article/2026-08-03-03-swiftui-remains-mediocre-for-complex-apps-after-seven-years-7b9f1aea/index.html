SwiftUI remains mediocre for complex apps after seven years

News time: 2026-08-03T04:44:33.720035+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Seven years after its 2019 debut at WWDC, SwiftUI has become Apple’s flagship UI framework—but not without growing pains. While it delivers elegant syntax and rapid prototyping for simple interfaces, a widely discussed retrospective argues that it still falls short in production-grade complexity. Developers report unpredictable rendering behavior, limited control over performance-critical paths, and opaque data flow that makes debugging and optimization difficult—especially in large-scale or latency-sensitive applications like games or enterprise tools.

The article highlights how many experienced teams have settled into pragmatic hybrid patterns: using SwiftUI for lightweight, static, or auxiliary UI (like settings toggles or help sheets), while falling back to UIKit for anything demanding precise layout control, fine-grained animation timing, or predictable lifecycle management. This isn’t failure—it’s adaptation. Apple’s official UIKit-SwiftUI interoperability APIs, introduced in 2020 and continuously refined, now serve as de facto scaffolding rather than temporary bridges.

Critics point to SwiftUI’s declarative model as both its strength and its weakness. Unlike UIKit’s explicit, imperative control over view creation and updates, SwiftUI relies on opaque diffing and reconciliation logic—hidden behind layers of opaque structs and opaque update triggers. Even with profiling tools like Xcode’s View Debugger and Timeline, developers often struggle to trace why a view re-renders or when state propagation occurs. As one commenter notes, you can be “dead long before you realize you’re dead”—a slow erosion of maintainability masked by initial velocity.

Still, SwiftUI isn’t stagnant. It’s gained features like async/await integration, improved concurrency support, and tighter Metal interop—but these additions haven’t resolved the core tension between abstraction and control. The broader debate echoes across platforms: Kotlin Compose faces similar critiques, suggesting this may be an inherent challenge for declarative-native frameworks—not just an Apple-specific misstep. For now, the most resilient iOS apps aren’t built in SwiftUI or in UIKit alone—they’re built across both.
