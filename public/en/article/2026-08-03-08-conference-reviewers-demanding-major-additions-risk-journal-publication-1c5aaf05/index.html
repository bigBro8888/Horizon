Conference reviewers demanding major additions risk journal publication

News time: 2026-08-03T04:44:33.975351+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning researcher recently raised a pressing dilemma on Reddit: what happens when top-tier conference reviewers ask authors to add substantial new content—like extended experiments or theoretical derivations—just before the camera-ready deadline, especially when the main paper is already at the strict page limit? The common workaround is to tuck those additions into supplemental materials or appendices. But that solution carries unintended consequences: such enriched versions may effectively constitute prior disclosure, jeopardizing future journal submission under standard double-publication policies.

This isn’t merely a formatting quirk—it’s a structural tension in how academic publishing operates across venues. Conferences like NeurIPS, ICML, and CVPR prioritize rapid dissemination and often treat supplements as unreviewed or lightly reviewed extensions. Journals, by contrast, require novelty and prohibit substantial overlap with previously published work—even if that earlier version appeared only in conference proceedings with supplementary material. One researcher reported withdrawing a paper entirely after realizing their revised conference version, complete with extensive new analysis in the appendix, would likely be flagged as duplicate or insufficiently novel by journal editors.

The issue cuts deeper than logistics. It exposes a misalignment between review expectations and publication rights. Reviewers may believe they’re improving rigor by requesting broader evaluation—but without clear guidance on how much can be added outside the core paper, they inadvertently push authors toward disclosures that violate journal norms. And because supplemental materials are increasingly discoverable (indexed, citable, and sometimes archived alongside the main paper), their status as ‘non-publication’ is eroding.

What’s needed isn’t less rigor—but clearer guardrails. Conference organizers could clarify supplement policies upfront: which additions count toward novelty assessment, whether appendix content undergoes review, and how much overlap journals will tolerate. Authors, meanwhile, should proactively consult target journal guidelines before revising for conferences—and consider pre-submission inquiries when major expansions are requested. Ultimately, this reflects a broader challenge: sustaining both speed and scholarly integrity in fast-moving fields like machine learning.
