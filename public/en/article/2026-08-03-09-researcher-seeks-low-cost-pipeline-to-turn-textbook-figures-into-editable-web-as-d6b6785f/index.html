Researcher seeks low-cost pipeline to turn textbook figures into editable web assets

News time: 2026-08-03T04:44:33.975584+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher on Reddit is asking the machine learning community for help building a practical, human-in-the-loop pipeline to convert static academic textbook figures—ranging from biology diagrams to engineering schematics—into structured, interactive digital assets. The goal isn’t full automation, but significant manual effort reduction: given a scanned page or image, the system should detect figure boundaries, identify embedded labels (letters, arrows, callouts), cleanly remove those labels while preserving underlying artwork, and output geometry data (e.g., masks or polygons) that lets a frontend dynamically render, hide, translate, or restyle annotations.

The biggest technical hurdle isn’t detection—it’s label removal. Standard OCR and contour-based methods can locate candidate regions, but erasing text and symbols without damaging delicate illustrations (like hand-drawn anatomical outlines or fine chemical structures) remains fragile. Diffusion-based inpainting shows promise but risks high compute cost and artifacts; the researcher explicitly prioritizes affordability and scalability over cutting-edge model size.

This sits at the intersection of document understanding, computer vision, and interactive visualization. It’s less about recognizing natural scenes and more about parsing semantically rich, domain-diverse educational figures—many of which contain layered elements (text + line art + shading) with inconsistent layouts. Recent work like DocSegTr offers instance-level segmentation for figures in documents, while biomedical figure segmentation frameworks demonstrate how layout cues (e.g., caption proximity) can improve robustness. Meanwhile, emerging concepts like 'LLM-native figures' emphasize structured, machine-interpretable representations—not just cleaned images, but linked geometry, metadata, and provenance.

What makes this request especially grounded is its pragmatic framing: it accepts human review as part of the workflow, focuses on cost efficiency, and avoids over-engineering. That realism resonates across open-source tooling communities—from layout parsers like LayoutParser to lightweight segmentation models trained on scientific figures. If solved well, such a pipeline could accelerate digital textbook development, accessibility tools, and AI-augmented STEM education—without requiring billion-parameter multimodal models.
