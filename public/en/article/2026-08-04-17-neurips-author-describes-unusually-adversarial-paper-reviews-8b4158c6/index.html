NeurIPS author describes unusually adversarial paper reviews

News time: 2026-08-04T04:28:36.366227+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning researcher shared a disheartening firsthand account of their NeurIPS submission experience — one marked by stark inconsistency, adversarial tone, and minimal administrative oversight. Though the author had conscientiously reviewed multiple NeurIPS papers themselves — even those they suspected were low-effort or AI-generated — they received two outright hostile reviews for their own work. Their scoring approach was notably measured: they reserved rejection only for papers with severe flaws, whereas one of their reviewers assigned a full set of 1s (the lowest possible score) across all evaluation criteria despite raising only minor concerns.

The handling by the Area Chair (AC) further deepened the frustration. The AC remained nearly silent until the final day of the review cycle, and almost none of the reviewers responded to follow-up requests — except one, who acknowledged that the authors’ rebuttal had addressed their concerns yet still upheld a reject decision. This disconnect between responsiveness and outcome highlights a structural weakness in how feedback is weighed and decisions are finalized.

What makes this case especially telling is not just its severity, but its familiarity. The author explicitly notes that while their batch of reviewers felt unusually adversarial, the broader unpredictability of NeurIPS outcomes is widely accepted — even expected — within the community. They describe the process as ‘a lottery’, underscoring how much hinges on reviewer assignment rather than objective merit. Yet calling it ‘toxic’ signals more than randomness: it points to eroded trust in fairness, transparency, and accountability.

This anecdote resonates because it reflects long-standing tensions in top-tier ML conferences. As submission volumes surge and reviewer fatigue grows, inconsistent standards, unresponsive chairs, and subjective scoring thresholds threaten the credibility of peer review. For early-career researchers — who rely heavily on NeurIPS acceptance for visibility, jobs, and funding — such experiences aren’t just disappointing; they risk discouraging participation altogether.
