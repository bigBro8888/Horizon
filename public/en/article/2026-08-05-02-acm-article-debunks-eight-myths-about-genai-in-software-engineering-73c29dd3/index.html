ACM article debunks eight myths about GenAI in software engineering

News time: 2026-08-05T04:26:41.154286+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new article published in ACM Queue challenges a wave of overoptimistic and oversimplified narratives about generative AI’s role in software development. Titled 'Eight Myths on Software Engineering and GenAI', the piece systematically examines widely repeated claims — such as 'developers spend most of their time writing code' or 'GenAI will soon replace junior engineers' — and shows how each misrepresents both empirical reality and the nature of engineering work.

The authors ground their analysis in decades of software engineering research, citing studies from Microsoft, GitHub, and academic labs that consistently find coding accounts for only about 11–14% of a developer’s typical workweek. The rest involves design trade-offs, debugging complex interactions, navigating legacy systems, collaborating across teams, and continuously learning new tools and domains. Far from automating these activities, current GenAI tools mostly assist with narrow, well-scoped tasks — like generating boilerplate, suggesting variable names, or summarizing documentation.

Crucially, the article argues that GenAI does not diminish the centrality of human judgment. For example, when an LLM proposes a fix for a bug, developers still must understand the system context, assess side effects, verify correctness across edge cases, and decide whether the change aligns with long-term architecture goals. These are not tasks that scale with model size — they demand experience, domain knowledge, and collaborative sensemaking.

The piece also pushes back against technological determinism: the idea that AI capabilities will inevitably reshape roles regardless of organizational needs or human workflows. Instead, it emphasizes that tool adoption succeeds only when it integrates thoughtfully into existing practices — not when it forces developers to adapt to the tool’s limitations. As one section notes, 'augmentation is not automation': the most effective AI-assisted teams treat models as collaborative partners, not autonomous agents. This distinction matters deeply for training, tool design, and management expectations.
