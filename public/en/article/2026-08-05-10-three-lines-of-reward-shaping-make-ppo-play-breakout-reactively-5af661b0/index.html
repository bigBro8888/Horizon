Three lines of reward shaping make PPO play Breakout reactively

News time: 2026-08-05T04:26:41.461381+00:00 · Source: reddit

After 124 failed attempts, a solo researcher cracked a long-standing puzzle in reinforcement learning: how to get a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent to react to the ball in Atari Breakout—not just replay memorized sequences. For months, every variant he tried—sticky actions, entropy regularization, adversarial brick layouts, dynamics randomization—produced agents that behaved like clockwork scripts: identical paddle movements regardless of where the ball actually went. The breakthrough wasn’t architectural or environmental; it was behavioral. He added just three lines of code to the reward function: a tiny +0.05 bonus per frame whenever the ball was descending and horizontally aligned with the paddle. Crucially, this bonus applied only during training—not evaluation—so the final policy operated on clean, unmodified Breakout. The result? A single agent that dynamically adjusts its paddle position in real time, succeeding even on custom brick configurations that break scripted trajectories.

This isn’t about higher scores—it’s about behavioral robustness. Prior approaches tried to break memorization by making environments harder to script, but PPO always adapted: developing timing-robust, layout-aware, or noise-tolerant scripts instead. Reward shaping flipped the optimization landscape: now, the highest-reward policy isn’t a fixed sequence—it’s continuous alignment. A static center-hold gets incidental bonuses; a reactive tracker earns them on every descent frame. The shift is fundamental: from exploiting environment regularity to sensing and responding to state.

To demonstrate this visually, the author built ‘Split-Watcher’—a side-by-side visualization tool showing two Breakout instances controlled by the same trained agent. Left: standard game. Right: nonstandard brick layouts. In all 123 prior runs, both sides mirrored identical paddle motions—even when the right-side ball bounced unpredictably. In run #124, the right-side paddle moved differently, tracking the ball’s actual path. This visual proof underscores how reward shaping reshapes what the agent learns to prioritize.

The project is fully open source, including all 123 failed experiments, detailed documentation, and a ready-to-run PPO implementation. While the mechanism behind why this specific reward term works so cleanly is still under investigation, the empirical lesson is clear: sometimes, the most powerful intervention isn’t more complexity—but sharper, human-aligned feedback.
