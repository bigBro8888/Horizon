NeurIPS 2026 Concept & Feasibility track faces early consistency concerns

News time: 2026-08-05T04:26:41.460995+00:00 · Source: reddit

A growing concern has emerged among machine learning researchers about the implementation of NeurIPS 2026’s newly introduced Concept & Feasibility (C&F) track. Launched as part of a broader effort to broaden the scope of acceptable contributions—especially for high-risk, high-reward ideas that cannot be fully validated in a single paper—the track explicitly welcomes proposals with only preliminary experimental support. Yet an anonymous author recently raised alarms on Reddit, reporting that their C&F submission received reviewer feedback sharply at odds with the official policy: reviewers praised the novelty and ambition of the idea but criticized the limited scale of experiments—despite the track’s clear statement that full empirical validation is not required.

This discrepancy points to a deeper tension in academic publishing: how to operationalize new review paradigms when reviewer expectations remain anchored in traditional norms. The NeurIPS 2026 blog posts emphasize that C&F papers should be judged primarily on conceptual originality, feasibility reasoning, and long-term potential—not completeness of evaluation. Still, early anecdotal evidence suggests some reviewers may be applying General Track standards by default, potentially undermining the track’s purpose.

The author’s post also highlights procedural gaps: no rebuttal responses were received from reviewers, and there appears to be little public discussion or shared experience around C&F submissions so far. With no comments yet on the Reddit thread—and no publicly available aggregate data on acceptance rates or review patterns—the community lacks visibility into whether this case reflects isolated misalignment or a systemic issue.

If unaddressed, inconsistent enforcement could discourage submissions to the C&F track, especially from junior researchers or those proposing truly speculative ideas. It also risks reinforcing longstanding critiques of ML conferences: that they reward incremental engineering over foundational insight, and that structural reforms often stall at the implementation level. For NeurIPS—a venue widely seen as setting de facto standards for the field—getting this right matters not just for fairness, but for shaping what kinds of science get recognized and funded.
