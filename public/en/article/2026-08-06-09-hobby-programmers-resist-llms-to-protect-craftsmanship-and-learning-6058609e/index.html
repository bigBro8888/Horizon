Hobby programmers resist LLMs to protect craftsmanship and learning

News time: 2026-08-06T04:28:17.718303+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A widely discussed essay titled Born Against argues that resistance to large language models (LLMs) in hobbyist programming communities isn’t rooted in technophobia or ignorance—but in a deep commitment to values like craftsmanship, deliberate learning, and authentic authorship. The author observes that many hobbyists treat programming not as a means to an end, but as an intrinsically rewarding practice: the joy lies in understanding how systems work, debugging step-by-step, and building competence through sustained effort. Introducing LLMs as default helpers—especially for core coding tasks—feels less like augmentation and more like bypassing the very activity that gives the hobby meaning.

This stance echoes norms in other skill-based hobbies. As one commenter notes, racing rules prohibit driver aids even when they’d improve lap times; grappling competitions ban striking to preserve the integrity of the discipline; chess players reject engine assistance to honor the mental labor of play. Similarly, hobby programmers see LLM-generated code not as ‘help,’ but as a substitution that erodes the developmental arc—from confusion to insight, from copy-paste to confident design.

The resistance also extends to ethical concerns beyond personal practice. Several commenters highlight real-world incidents where LLM-assisted projects repackaged AGPL-licensed code without proper attribution or license compliance—dubbed ‘copy-washing.’ This blurs provenance, undermines open-source reciprocity, and risks legal exposure. Others point to broader community effects: declining forum engagement, rising low-effort ‘AI-generated’ abandonware, and fewer meaningful peer interactions as developers shift from collaborative problem-solving to solo prompting.

Importantly, the critique isn’t anti-tool—it’s pro-intentionality. The essay and comments distinguish between using LLMs for documentation lookup, refactoring suggestions, or boilerplate generation (where human oversight remains central) versus delegating design decisions, architecture, or logic synthesis. The line isn’t technical—it’s cultural and pedagogical. As one reader puts it: ‘A hobby is something you enjoy the process of doing, not just the end result.’ For these programmers, the process is the point.
