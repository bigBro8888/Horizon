Nashville blocks data center near zoo using eminent domain

News time: 2026-08-06T04:28:17.718104+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a rare and consequential move, Nashville’s Metro Council voted to invoke eminent domain to block a proposed data center development adjacent to the Nashville Zoo — effectively seizing the land not for public construction, but to prevent private infrastructure from being built there. The decision, approved in early 2024, marks one of the first known instances in the U.S. where a city has used its condemnation power defensively: not to acquire land for a public project, but to deny it to a commercial developer.

The site in question is part of a mixed-use industrial corridor near the zoo’s southern boundary — surrounded by office parks, freight rail yards, and big-box retail. Developer DC BLOX had planned a large-scale colocation facility on the parcel, though no final design or power load specifications were publicly released. Local residents raised concerns about traffic, noise, utility strain, and the symbolic impact of placing high-density AI infrastructure next to a cherished civic institution that has expanded significantly over the past two decades.

Tennessee law permits eminent domain for ‘public use’ — a term historically interpreted broadly, including economic development. But Nashville’s action stretches that interpretation further: the city isn’t building a road or park on the land. Instead, it intends to hold the property as open space or repurpose it for community-serving uses — a strategy some legal observers call ‘preemptive condemnation.’ Critics argue this blurs the line between regulation and acquisition; supporters say it reflects legitimate municipal authority to shape growth in alignment with long-term quality-of-life goals.

This case arrives amid a national wave of friction over AI-driven data center expansion. From rural Iowa to suburban Virginia, communities are confronting sudden proposals for facilities drawing tens of megawatts — often without prior zoning clarity or environmental review. Nashville’s precedent doesn’t ban data centers outright, but signals that location, context, and cumulative infrastructure burden now carry legal weight alongside technical feasibility. As AI infrastructure scales, cities may increasingly treat land-use control not just as planning policy — but as a frontline tool of digital governance.
