New Mexico court orders Meta to pay $942 million for harming children on Instagram and Facebook

News time: 2026-08-07T04:02:59.122261+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a landmark ruling, a New Mexico state court has ordered Meta Platforms Inc. to pay $942 million to address documented harms to children’s mental health caused by its platforms Instagram and Facebook. The decision stems from a lawsuit filed by the state’s attorney general, which argued that Meta’s design choices—including algorithmic feeds, infinite scroll, and engagement-maximizing features—knowingly exacerbated anxiety, depression, body image issues, and sleep disruption among minors. Crucially, the court found Meta liable not under consumer protection or privacy statutes, but under New Mexico’s public-nuisance law—a legal theory rarely applied to tech companies and traditionally reserved for physical hazards like pollution or unsafe buildings.

The ruling hinges on Section 30-8-1 of the New Mexico Statutes, which defines a public nuisance as conduct that is ‘injurious to public health, safety, morals or welfare’ or that ‘interferes with the exercise and enjoyment of public rights.’ The court determined that Meta’s systemic manipulation of adolescent psychology through platform architecture met this threshold. Unlike typical settlements, this is a judicial finding of liability after trial—not a negotiated agreement—and carries precedential weight for future litigation across other states.

Legal experts note that while the amount is substantial, it represents less than 1% of Meta’s annual revenue, raising questions about whether such penalties will meaningfully alter corporate behavior. Critics—including commenters on Hacker News—wonder whether fines at this scale simply become ‘the cost of doing business’ rather than a deterrent. Others point out that the ruling opens the door for similar suits in jurisdictions with comparable public-nuisance statutes, potentially reshaping how courts view digital platforms’ societal responsibilities.

Importantly, the order does not mandate product changes or injunctions—only monetary compensation. That limitation underscores both the power and the limits of public-nuisance claims: they can impose financial accountability but cannot directly regulate platform design. Still, advocates see the decision as a watershed moment—one that reframes social media’s impact on youth not as an unfortunate side effect, but as a legally redressable harm rooted in corporate choice.
