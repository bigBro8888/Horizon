Taste remains the last uniquely human quality in software development

News time: 2026-08-07T04:02:59.122102+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In an era where large language models (LLMs) generate code, write documentation, and even draft architecture proposals, a growing number of developers argue that one quality still resists automation: taste. Not in the culinary sense—but as a holistic faculty encompassing aesthetic judgment, moral intuition, intellectual discernment, and design sensibility. The essay 'Taste Is All That's Left' contends that while LLMs excel at localized tasks—fixing a bug, suggesting a function signature, or paraphrasing documentation—they consistently fail when it comes to coherence across time, consistency across teams, and intentionality across systems.

This isn’t just about style. It’s about how decisions accumulate: choosing between abstraction layers, weighing trade-offs between readability and performance, deciding when not to build something, or recognizing when a seemingly elegant solution masks long-term technical debt. These judgments rely on context that no current model fully internalizes—organizational history, team dynamics, user empathy, and evolving ethical norms.

Commenters reinforce this view with lived experience. One veteran developer notes that decades of coding taught them taste through repeated failure—not via rules, but through pattern recognition honed over thousands of shipped features and abandoned prototypes. Another observes that AI-generated codebases often suffer from 'low signal': syntactically correct but semantically shallow, lacking narrative flow or architectural rhythm. Even writing—LLMs’ strongest domain—frequently produces text that reads like plausible noise rather than purposeful communication.

Yet not all agree. Some push back against romanticizing taste as a durable competitive advantage, pointing out how quickly UI patterns (like pull-to-refresh) spread across apps once introduced—suggesting that ‘taste’ often becomes commoditized faster than it can be cultivated. Still, the consensus emerging from the discussion is less about whether taste can be automated, and more about whether we’re willing to accept software built without it: functional, perhaps, but brittle, opaque, and ultimately alienating to both users and maintainers.
