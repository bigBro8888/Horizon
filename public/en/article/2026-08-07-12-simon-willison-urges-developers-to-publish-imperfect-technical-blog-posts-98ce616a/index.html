Simon Willison urges developers to publish imperfect technical blog posts

News time: 2026-08-07T04:02:59.276545+00:00 · Source: rss

In a newly surfaced interview originally conducted in January and republished on his personal site, veteran developer and open-source advocate Simon Willison offers candid, experience-driven advice on technical blogging. Known for building Datasette, contributing to Django, and maintaining one of the web’s most consistently insightful developer blogs since 2004, Willison reflects not on theory or metrics—but on the human habits that sustain long-term writing.

He frames blogging as a practice rooted in consistency, not polish. His central message is disarmingly simple: lower your standards enough to hit publish, even when you’re still dissatisfied with the draft. "The only alternative," he warns, "is a huge folder full of drafts and never publishing anything at all." This isn’t about sacrificing clarity or accuracy—it’s about recognizing that the version you judge harshly is often indistinguishable from a 'finished' piece to readers.

Willison walks through seven key questions—from why he started blogging (to clarify his own thinking) to which post he finds most difficult (a deeply researched piece on SQLite’s virtual tables), and which blogs he reads regularly (including Dan Luu’s and Julia Evans’s). His answers avoid prescriptive formulas; instead, they emphasize iterative learning, audience feedback as a compass, and treating each post as a step—not a statement.

What stands out is his rejection of perfectionism as a barrier to contribution. He notes that readers rarely notice the flaws authors obsess over—and that shipping work builds credibility, sharpens communication, and creates unexpected professional opportunities. For early-career engineers especially, this mindset shift—from waiting for readiness to embracing incremental sharing—can transform how they engage with the broader software community.

Ultimately, Willison’s advice lands not as a productivity hack but as a cultural corrective: technical writing isn’t about proving expertise, but about participating in a shared, evolving conversation. And participation begins the moment you click publish—flaws and all.
