Ex-NSA chief warns water system controllers shouldn’t be internet-connected

News time: 2026-08-08T02:59:47.556020+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a stark warning echoing growing alarm across critical infrastructure sectors, former NSA Director Michael Hayden has declared that water system controllers have no business being connected to the public internet — especially amid confirmed and suspected Iranian cyber operations targeting U.S. industrial control systems. His remarks, published in The Register, come after recent incidents involving compromised programmable logic controllers (PLCs) at municipal water facilities, where attackers reportedly attempted to alter chemical dosing levels. Hayden’s position isn’t theoretical: it reflects decades of operational experience and aligns with long-standing guidance from NIST and ICS security experts who stress air-gapping as a foundational defense for legacy OT environments.

The debate isn’t simply about connectivity — it’s about risk architecture. Many water utilities still rely on PLCs deployed in the 1990s and early 2000s, devices never designed with network security in mind. As one veteran PLC engineer noted in community comments, these systems often lack encryption, role-based access, or even basic firmware update mechanisms. Worse, some are now being retrofitted with wireless RF modules or Bluetooth interfaces — ostensibly for remote maintenance — yet these local links frequently use weak or no authentication, creating backdoors that bypass traditional firewalls.

At the same time, AI-powered offensive tools are accelerating threat velocity. Coding agents capable of autonomously scanning, fingerprinting, and exploiting ICS vulnerabilities are no longer hypothetical; they’re appearing in underground forums and red-team toolkits. One commenter warned that without urgent investment in defensive engineering — including thousands of specialized security engineers trained in both OT protocols and modern AI-assisted detection — the U.S. faces a potential ‘9/11-scale’ incident not from planes, but from poisoned reservoirs or failed pump stations.

That said, outright disconnection isn’t universally practical. Engineers point out that modernized PLCs with secure-by-design architectures — combined with properly configured firewalls, zero-trust VPNs, and strict network segmentation — can support safe remote monitoring and updates. But the consensus is clear: connecting legacy PLCs directly to the internet remains an unacceptable risk. As another commenter put it bluntly: 'Don’t put your PLCs directly on the internet. Most industrial gear was never built for that.'
