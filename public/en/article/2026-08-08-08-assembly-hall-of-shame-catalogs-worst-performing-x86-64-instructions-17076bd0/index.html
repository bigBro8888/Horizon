Assembly Hall of Shame catalogs worst-performing x86-64 instructions

News time: 2026-08-08T02:59:47.555840+00:00 · Source: hackernews

The Assembly Hall of Shame is not a satire site—it’s a meticulously curated, technically grounded repository of x86 and x86-64 assembly instructions that deliberately trigger pathological behavior on real hardware. Hosted on GitHub by developer xoreaxeaxeax, the project documents instructions that induce extreme latency, infinite bus waits, SMM (System Management Mode) abuse, or virtualization traps—not for malicious exploitation, but as stress tests for CPU microarchitecture, firmware robustness, and emulator fidelity.

Each entry includes precise opcodes, execution timing measurements (some exceeding 12 milliseconds), hardware context (e.g., specific Intel/AMD generations), and detailed commentary on why the instruction misbehaves. For example, certain I/O port writes to ACPI registers can trap into SMM and stall execution for milliseconds—time enough to expose timing side channels or break real-time guarantees. Others exploit legacy addressing modes or undefined opcodes that generate exceptions only under specific privilege or emulation conditions.

The project sits at the intersection of systems programming, security research, and hardware validation. It’s used by firmware engineers to audit SMI (System Management Interrupt) handlers, by hypervisor developers to test trap-and-emulate logic, and by educators to illustrate how abstract ISA specifications collide with concrete silicon realities. Unlike theoretical benchmarks, these examples are empirically verified on physical machines—including older Core i7s and modern Xeon Scalable processors.

What makes it especially valuable is its rigor: entries must be reproducible, measurable, and grounded in observable hardware behavior—not speculation. The author explicitly excludes hypothetical or untestable cases, and contributors annotate each instruction with microarchitectural notes (e.g., pipeline stalls, front-end bottlenecks, or cache-line locking effects). As one commenter observes, even NOP earns ironic praise—not for speed, but for its unmatched inefficiency per unit of work done.
