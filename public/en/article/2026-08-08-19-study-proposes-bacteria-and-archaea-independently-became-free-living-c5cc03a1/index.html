Study proposes bacteria and archaea independently became free-living

News time: 2026-08-08T02:59:47.556106+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new study challenges the standard narrative of life’s origin by proposing that bacteria and archaea did not diverge from a single free-living ancestor—but instead each evolved the capacity to live independently after being tethered to mineral surfaces in hydrothermal vent environments. Rather than emerging fully formed from a shared, membrane-enclosed, self-sustaining cell, the two domains may have separately crossed a critical threshold: shedding dependence on catalytic mineral interfaces to become metabolically autonomous, enclosed cells capable of replication and dispersal.

This reinterpretation does not claim two origins of genetics or biochemistry. All known life shares the same genetic code, core translation machinery, and central metabolic pathways—strong evidence for a universal common ancestor (LUCA) that likely existed as a population of loosely organized, surface-bound proto-cells. LUCA probably lacked full biosynthetic independence: it relied on geochemical energy and mineral scaffolds—especially metal-rich sulfide surfaces—to concentrate molecules, drive reactions, and stabilize early membranes. Only later did descendant lineages independently evolve complete, enzyme-driven versions of essential pathways like nucleotide synthesis and lipid biosynthesis.

The evidence comes from comparative biochemistry: researchers identified cases where bacteria and archaea use structurally unrelated enzymes to catalyze identical biochemical reactions—particularly in pathways absent from LUCA reconstructions, such as parts of cofactor and membrane lipid synthesis. These ‘non-homologous replacements’ suggest parallel innovation, not vertical inheritance. Crucially, the differences align with known biophysical constraints: bacterial membranes use fatty acid esters; archaeal membranes use isoprenoid ethers—chemically distinct solutions likely forged under different mineral-surface conditions.

Critics emphasize nuance: this is not ‘life arising twice’ in the sense of two separate abiogenesis events producing RNA, ribosomes, or coded translation. Instead, it’s a proposal that the transition from surface-confined proto-metabolism to free-living cellular autonomy occurred twice—once in the bacterial lineage, once in the archaeal. As one commenter noted, the headline oversimplifies; the real insight lies in reframing LUCA not as a cell, but as a dynamic, spatially constrained chemical system—one whose descendants solved the same survival problems in different ways.
