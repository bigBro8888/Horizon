NeurIPS reviewers used LLMs inconsistently and broke double-blind rules

News time: 2026-08-09T03:06:46.885335+00:00 · Source: reddit

A NeurIPS reviewer recently shared a candid reflection on the 2025 review cycle—revealing troubling inconsistencies in how large language models (LLMs) were used during peer review. The reviewer noted that while they provided detailed, constructive feedback—pointing to specific methodological gaps and suggesting concrete improvements—other reviewers submitted superficial comments focused on trivial formatting or notation issues. Even for papers where the reviewer deliberately avoided LLM assistance as a control, their peers’ reviews remained shallow and unengaged with core technical substance.

More seriously, the reviewer observed at least one instance where a fellow reviewer explicitly cited LLM-generated text during the discussion phase—describing what the model ‘said’ about the paper—and used it to justify rejection. Crucially, this reviewer had not disclosed LLM use in their original review nor engaged with the authors’ rebuttal, violating both transparency norms and NeurIPS’s double-blind policy. That breach was especially glaring because NeurIPS mandates strict anonymity: authors and reviewers must not identify each other, and reviewers are prohibited from seeking external information that could compromise blind evaluation.

The reviewer also raised a subtle but important point about epistemic fairness: several of their own papers received low clarity scores—not because of poor writing, but because reviewers unfamiliar with established notation in the subfield struggled to follow arguments. They suggested that if LLMs were being used anyway, reviewers could ethically consult them to clarify standard definitions or compare notation across prior work—without breaking anonymity. Instead, many appeared to treat LLMs as black-box validators rather than learning aids, deepening knowledge asymmetries.

This account isn’t an isolated complaint—it reflects growing unease across AI research communities about unregulated LLM adoption in scholarly evaluation. Recent studies confirm that field-specific prompting can significantly shift review outcomes, and ethical guidelines stress the need for disclosure, non-anthropomorphic framing, and alignment with human judgment. Without clear policies, inconsistent LLM use risks eroding trust in peer review—not just at NeurIPS, but across machine learning venues.
