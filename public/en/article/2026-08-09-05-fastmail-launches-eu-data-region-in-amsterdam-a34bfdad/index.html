Fastmail launches EU data region in Amsterdam

News time: 2026-08-09T03:06:46.646357+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Fastmail has launched a dedicated EU data region, enabling users to store the primary copy of their email and files on Fastmail-owned servers located in Amsterdam. This move gives customers a clear choice at signup—or via account settings—to route their data into this new infrastructure. The company emphasizes that no extra fee applies, and the change is immediately available to both new and existing subscribers. CEO Bron Gervais framed it as delivering an ‘EU home’ for email—responding directly to growing demand for data locality amid tightening GDPR enforcement and rising concerns about transatlantic data transfers.

However, Fastmail is unusually transparent about what this doesn’t guarantee. In its official blog post, the company explicitly states: ‘If what you need is a guarantee that your data remains only in the EU, we don’t have that.’ The limitation stems from Fastmail’s multinational corporate structure: headquartered in Melbourne (Australia), merged with U.S.-based Pobox, and now operating infrastructure across three jurisdictions. Even with EU-hosted primary storage, certain backend services—including authentication, billing, and some metadata processing—may still involve systems or vendors outside the EU.

This nuance matters because data residency (where data is physically stored) is not the same as data sovereignty (which jurisdiction’s laws govern that data). As IBM and Splunk explain, sovereignty hinges on legal control—not just geography. A server in Amsterdam doesn’t automatically shield data from U.S. legal reach if Fastmail remains subject to U.S. jurisdiction via ownership, contracts, or third-party dependencies (e.g., cloud providers or compliance tools under U.S. law).

User comments reflect sharp awareness of these distinctions. Several experienced observers caution that ‘EU data region’ is a marketing term—not a legal safeguard—and warn that U.S.-owned infrastructure layers anywhere in the stack could expose data to Five Eyes surveillance or compelled disclosure. Others point to fully EU-owned alternatives like Tuta as viable options for users requiring strict jurisdictional separation. Ultimately, Fastmail’s step is pragmatic and customer-facing—but it underscores how hard true data sovereignty remains for globally operated tech services.
