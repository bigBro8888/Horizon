New _for-sale DNS record lets domains publicly signal availability

News time: 2026-08-09T03:06:46.646385+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new, lightweight internet convention—using a standardized DNS TXT record named _for-sale—enables domain owners to publicly declare that their domain is available for purchase. Unlike relying on opaque third-party marketplaces or outdated WHOIS contact details, this approach embeds sale status directly into the domain’s DNS infrastructure, where it can be queried automatically by tools, registrars, or potential buyers. The record follows RFC-aligned syntax: a simple TXT entry beginning with v=FORSALE1, optionally followed by structured or free-text fields like price, contact email, or negotiation terms.

This proposal responds to long-standing friction in domain discovery. With widespread WHOIS privacy redaction and no universal signaling mechanism, buyers often resort to speculative outreach—sending cold emails to anonymized contacts or guessing administrative addresses. Meanwhile, legitimate owners struggle to attract serious offers without exposing personal data or listing on centralized platforms that charge commissions or lack transparency. The _for-sale record sidesteps these issues by leveraging existing, decentralized DNS infrastructure—no new protocols, servers, or permissions required.

Implementation is intentionally minimal: setting the record signals intent; removing it revokes the signal. There’s no official registry, no enforcement, and no ‘not for sale’ counterpart—the absence of the record carries no defined meaning, much like a house without a ‘For Sale’ sign isn’t necessarily off-market. Tools like the open-source MCP server mcp-forsale already support automated querying via DoH (DNS over HTTPS) or traditional resolvers, validating presence and parsing content.

The idea has sparked nuanced discussion across technical, legal, and economic dimensions. Commenters raised trademark-related risks—e.g., whether advertising a domain for sale could weaken a defensive registration if a later trademark holder files a UDRP complaint. Others pointed to hostmaster@ as a pre-existing, standards-based contact channel, questioning whether a new DNS record adds real value. A few proposed complementary economic models, such as Georgist-style annual fees tied to self-declared sale prices, to disincentivize speculative hoarding. Taken together, the proposal isn’t about replacing human judgment—it’s about adding a small, interoperable signal where none existed before.
