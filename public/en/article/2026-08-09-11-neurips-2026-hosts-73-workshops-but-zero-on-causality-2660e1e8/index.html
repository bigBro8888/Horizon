NeurIPS 2026 hosts 73 workshops but zero on causality

News time: 2026-08-09T03:06:46.885230+00:00 · Source: reddit

A striking observation has surfaced ahead of NeurIPS 2026: among its 73 officially accepted workshops, not a single one is dedicated to causal inference. The finding, posted by a user on Reddit’s r/MachineLearning forum, quickly resonated with researchers who see causality as foundational—not peripheral—to robust, trustworthy AI. Causal reasoning enables models to move beyond pattern recognition toward understanding why things happen, supporting decisions in healthcare, policy, and fairness-critical domains. Its absence from NeurIPS—the field’s most prestigious machine learning conference—has sparked quiet alarm about shifting research priorities.

This isn’t the first time causality has been sidelined at top-tier venues. While NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR increasingly spotlight large language models, agentic systems, and scaling laws, causality has found stronger institutional footing elsewhere. The Conference on Uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence (UAI), now in its 42nd year, continues to feature causality as a core pillar—its 2026 edition in Amsterdam includes dedicated tracks on causal fairness, identifiability, and statistical trade-offs. Similarly, the Causal Learning and Reasoning (CLeaR) conference—co-founded with support from Amazon and now in its third iteration—hosts rigorous peer-reviewed work on causal discovery, structural causal models, and causal reinforcement learning.

The trend reflects deeper tensions in AI research culture. Causal methods often demand careful assumptions, domain knowledge, and smaller-scale experiments—qualities at odds with the data-hungry, benchmark-driven momentum behind LLMs and foundation models. Yet real-world applications—from drug development to climate modeling—increasingly require causal understanding, not just predictive accuracy. As one researcher noted, ‘LLMs can hallucinate correlations; causality helps us avoid acting on them.’

That said, causality hasn’t vanished from NeurIPS entirely: it occasionally appears in main-track papers or co-located tutorials—but never as a standalone workshop. This signals not irrelevance, but a loss of visibility and community-building infrastructure at the flagship event. Without dedicated spaces for debate, mentorship, and shared benchmarks, early-career researchers may steer toward more ‘trendy’ subfields—even if causality remains essential for deploying AI safely and equitably.
