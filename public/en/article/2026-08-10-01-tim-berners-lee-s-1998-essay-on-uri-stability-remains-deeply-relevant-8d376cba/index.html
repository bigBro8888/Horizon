Tim Berners-Lee’s 1998 essay on URI stability remains deeply relevant

News time: 2026-08-10T03:17:51.867229+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Nearly three decades after its publication, Tim Berners-Lee’s short 1998 essay 'Cool URIs Don’t Change' continues to shape how engineers, librarians, and policy makers think about web identity and persistence. Written not as a technical specification but as a design philosophy, the essay insists that URIs should function like names—not addresses—meant to identify resources permanently, regardless of where or how they’re hosted. Berners-Lee argues that when URIs change, they break links, erode trust, and undermine the web’s core promise: that information can be reliably referenced over time.

The essay’s enduring influence is evident in real-world struggles with link rot—the phenomenon where hyperlinks stop working due to content migration, site rewrites, or domain abandonment. As one commenter notes, even recent government URLs like https://www.nsf.gov/pubs/1998/nsf9814/nsf9814.htm now return 404 errors—despite being only ~25 years old. Meanwhile, news sites routinely rewrite URLs during CMS upgrades, and corporate support pages vanish without redirects, leaving users stranded on generic landing pages.

Yet practical compromises abound. Modern platforms like WordPress automatically issue 301 redirects when slugs change, helping preserve SEO value and user access—but this relies on active maintenance, not architectural permanence. RFC 8820 (2020) echoes Berners-Lee’s principles, advising designers to treat URIs as stable identifiers owned by communities—not transient paths controlled by server configurations. Still, as commenters observe, conflating location (URL) with identity (URI) remains deeply embedded in tooling, workflows, and incentives—from search engine optimization to content management systems.

Ultimately, 'Cool URIs' is less a checklist than a north star: a reminder that every broken link represents lost knowledge, fractured citations, and diminished accountability. Its relevance isn’t nostalgic—it’s diagnostic. When governments, journals, and archives fail to uphold URI stability, they reveal systemic gaps in digital stewardship—not just technical limitations.
