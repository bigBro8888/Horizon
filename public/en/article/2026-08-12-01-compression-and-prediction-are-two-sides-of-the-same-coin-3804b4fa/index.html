Compression and prediction are two sides of the same coin

News time: 2026-08-12T03:28:47.092483+00:00 · Source: hackernews

At first glance, data compression—shrinking files to save space—and predictive modeling—forecasting future values from past data—seem like unrelated engineering tasks. But a growing body of theory argues they’re not just similar; they’re fundamentally equivalent. The core idea, crystallized in the ngrok blog post 'Compression is prediction', is that any system that predicts well can be used to compress data efficiently—and conversely, any optimal compressor implicitly learns a predictive model of its input.

This equivalence rests on information theory’s bedrock: entropy measures uncertainty, and reducing uncertainty is what both prediction and compression do. A good predictor minimizes surprise—i.e., it assigns high probability to likely outcomes—so the residual errors (what it got wrong) become highly compressible. Likewise, an optimal compressor must discover regularities and statistical structure in data—exactly what a predictor does when learning patterns. This insight bridges disciplines: in machine learning, the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle treats model selection as finding the shortest program that both describes the model and encodes the data given it. In neuroscience, brains may be viewed as biological compression engines, discarding redundant sensory input while retaining predictive summaries. Even physics enters the picture: Newton’s laws compress millennia of planetary motion into three concise equations—and enable precise predictions.

The equivalence isn’t perfectly symmetric in practice. As one commenter notes, some compression techniques (e.g., Fourier transforms or block sorting) exploit global structure without sequential prediction, suggesting compression can go beyond online forecasting. Still, algorithmic information theory formalizes the link via Kolmogorov complexity—the length of the shortest program generating a dataset—which serves as both an ideal measure of compressibility and a theoretical limit on predictability.

This unification matters because it reframes how we think about intelligence itself. Grant Sanderson’s video series frames compression as a proxy for intelligence: systems that better compress sensory streams likely understand them deeper. Similarly, modern large language models succeed not just by memorizing text, but by building internal predictors that make token sequences highly compressible. Ultimately, the principle reminds us that learning, understanding, and generalization all reduce to managing information efficiently—whether in silicon, synapses, or spacetime.
