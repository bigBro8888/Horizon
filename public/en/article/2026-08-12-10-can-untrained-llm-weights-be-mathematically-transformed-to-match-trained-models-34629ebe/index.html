Can untrained LLM weights be mathematically transformed to match trained models

News time: 2026-08-12T03:28:47.168352+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher on Reddit has proposed a bold new research direction: transforming the raw, untrained weights of a large language model (LLM) into an exact functional replica of a pre-trained LLM—using only direct mathematical operations, with no gradient-based training or knowledge distillation. The idea emerged from frustration with current efficiency bottlenecks: even state-of-the-art distillation methods require generating teacher responses, running backpropagation, and iteratively adjusting student weights—processes that still take hours or days. What if, instead, we could treat model weights not as optimization artifacts but as algebraic objects—manipulable via closed-form transformations to achieve identical input-output behavior?

This is not incremental compression or fine-tuning. It’s a foundational challenge: does a deterministic, non-iterative mapping exist between two neural network parameterizations such that their functions over all inputs are provably identical? The post explicitly invokes function equivalence—not just behavioral similarity—and asks whether such a mapping could be derived analytically, perhaps leveraging tools like neural tangent kernels (NTKs), linear mode connectivity, or weight-space geometry.

While the proposal lacks technical implementation details or formal proofs, it resonates with several active threads in modern ML theory. Recent work like DeltaLLM uses low-rank deltas between shared-weight layers for efficient post-training adaptation—hinting that structured weight differences may encode meaningful functional shifts. Similarly, studies on mode connectivity show that many high-performing models lie in connected regions of weight space, suggesting paths between them may be simpler than assumed. Yet none claim exact functional equivalence without training—only approximate or task-specific alignment.

The implications—if feasible—would be transformative: near-instant model deployment, zero-shot weight initialization for new architectures, and a deeper understanding of what ‘learning’ actually computes in weight space. But major hurdles remain: neural networks are highly nonlinear, overparameterized, and non-invertible; small weight changes often yield large functional shifts; and functional equivalence across infinite input domains is far stricter than typical evaluation metrics. Still, the question reframes training not as search, but as coordinate transformation—a perspective that could reshape how we think about representation, generalization, and model reuse.
