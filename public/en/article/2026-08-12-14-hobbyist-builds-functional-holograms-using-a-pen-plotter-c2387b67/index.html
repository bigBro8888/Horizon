Hobbyist builds functional holograms using a pen plotter

News time: 2026-08-12T03:28:47.092647+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A DIY electronics enthusiast has demonstrated how to create working analog holograms—not with lasers or digital displays, but with a humble pen plotter and transparent film. Jordan Matelsky, the creator behind the project, uses software to compute interference patterns—the optical 'blueprint' needed to reconstruct a 3D image—and then instructs a standard pen plotter to etch those patterns onto acetate or polyester film with micron-level precision. The resulting film, when illuminated with coherent light (like a laser pointer), projects a visible 3D holographic image—no screens, no code execution, just physics and careful mechanical motion.

This approach sits at the intersection of analog computing, optics, and accessible fabrication. Unlike digital holography—which relies on spatial light modulators or LCDs—this method is entirely mechanical and open-source. Matelsky’s workflow begins with Blender or custom Python scripts to simulate wavefront interference, exports vector paths, and feeds them to the plotter. The key insight is that a hologram doesn’t need photographic emulsion: fine scratches or refractive index changes induced by physical abrasion can diffract light just as effectively.

The project resonates with a broader revival of analog techniques in maker culture. Commenters quickly drew parallels to 1990s ‘abrasion holography’, where artists hand-scribed interference fringes with needles or fingernails on black polymer sheets. One commenter noted how Matelsky’s olive oil–fingerprint–phone screen demo elegantly illustrates diffraction: smearing oil on a fingerprint creates temporary micro-lenses that bend light like a hologram’s grooves. Others proposed hardware upgrades—like adding a piezoelectric disk scanner—to achieve sub-micron line spacing and higher resolution.

While not yet suitable for commercial displays, the technique lowers the barrier to holographic experimentation dramatically. It transforms holography from a lab-bound discipline requiring expensive optics into something teachable in a high school physics class—or replicable in a home workshop with $200 worth of gear. As one commenter put it, this is ‘old Internet’ fun: clever, tactile, and deeply rooted in first principles—not flashy AI, but light, math, and motion.
