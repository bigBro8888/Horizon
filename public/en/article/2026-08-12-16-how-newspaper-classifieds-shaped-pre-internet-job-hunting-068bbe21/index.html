How newspaper classifieds shaped pre-internet job hunting

News time: 2026-08-12T03:28:47.092703+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Before LinkedIn, ATS systems, and AI-powered resume screeners, job hunting was a slower, more tactile process—one rooted in physical newspapers, landline phones, and face-to-face initiative. The essay How we used to get jobs revisits this era not as a quaint relic, but as a window into hiring practices that prioritized human judgment over algorithmic filtering. Readers learn how job seekers scanned the ‘Help Wanted’ section of local dailies—often with narrow columns, cryptic abbreviations, and minimal details—and then acted: mailing resumes (sometimes hand-delivering them as a quiet ‘hack’), waiting days or weeks for a callback, and coordinating interviews around fixed office hours and answering machines.

The process demanded patience and persistence—but also created natural filters. As one commenter notes, the effort required to respond—printing, stamping, dialing, showing up—meant fewer applicants per role, giving employers time to assess candidates beyond bullet points. A high school graduate walked into an IBM office in the 1960s and landed a mainframe programming job after an aptitude test; another found early tech work via USENET postings, long before job boards existed. Even technical interviews were low-tech: candidates debugged C++ linked-list code printed on line printer paper—not on a live IDE.

This wasn’t just nostalgia—it was infrastructure shaped by constraint. Without digital reach, hiring leaned on local networks, reputation, and observable effort. Employers invested more in onboarding because turnover was costly and replacements hard to find. Employees, meanwhile, often stayed longer—not out of loyalty alone, but because switching jobs meant repeating a labor-intensive process from scratch.

Today’s hiring tools promise speed and scale, yet many struggle with signal-to-noise ratio, bias amplification, and candidate disengagement. The contrast isn’t about rejecting technology, but questioning what got lost when efficiency eclipsed intentionality: the weight of a stamped envelope, the voice on the other end of a landline, the quiet confidence of walking into an office and asking for a chance.
