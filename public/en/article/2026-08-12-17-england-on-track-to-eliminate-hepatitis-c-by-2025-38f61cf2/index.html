England on track to eliminate hepatitis C by 2025

News time: 2026-08-12T03:28:47.092719+00:00 · Source: hackernews

England is rapidly approaching a historic public health milestone: the elimination of hepatitis C as a major public health threat. According to recent assessments, the country is on course to achieve WHO-defined elimination—defined as reducing new chronic infections to fewer than 1 per 100,000 people per year and cutting hepatitis C-related deaths by 65%—by 2025. This progress stems from a coordinated national strategy that combines widespread community-based screening, rapid access to highly effective direct-acting antiviral (DAA) treatments, and targeted outreach to high-risk groups, including people who inject drugs and those born between 1950 and 1985.

The National Health Service (NHS) has played a central role, negotiating landmark 'subscription' or 'Netflix-style' payment agreements with pharmaceutical companies. Instead of paying per pill, the NHS pays an annual fee for unlimited access to curative DAA regimens—dramatically lowering cost barriers and enabling treatment at scale. Since 2019, over 40,000 people in England have been treated annually, with cure rates exceeding 95% after just 8–12 weeks of oral therapy.

Unlike many countries where hepatitis C remains underdiagnosed and undertreated, England’s approach prioritizes proactive case finding—not waiting for patients to present with symptoms or liver damage. Mobile testing units, prison-based screening, and integration with drug services have helped identify thousands of previously undiagnosed cases. Early diagnosis matters: most people with hepatitis C show no symptoms for decades, yet untreated infection can lead to cirrhosis, liver failure, or hepatocellular carcinoma.

While success is within reach, challenges remain—including reaching marginalized populations, sustaining funding, and ensuring equity across regions. Still, England’s experience offers a real-world blueprint for how health systems with universal coverage can translate scientific advances into population-level impact. If sustained, this effort could prevent tens of thousands of liver cancers and save countless lives—proving that elimination of a blood-borne viral disease is not just theoretical, but achievable.
