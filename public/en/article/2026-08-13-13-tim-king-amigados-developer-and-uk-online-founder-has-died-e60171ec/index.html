Tim King AmigaDOS developer and UK Online founder has died

News time: 2026-08-13T03:32:13.942530+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Tim King, the lead developer behind AmigaDOS—the command-line interface and file system core of the iconic AmigaOS—has passed away. His work shaped how a generation of users interacted with computers in the late 1980s and early 1990s, offering a remarkably flexible and scriptable CLI long before such features became mainstream on personal computers. Beyond the Amiga, King co-founded UK Online, one of the UK’s earliest commercial internet service providers, helping bring dial-up internet access to homes and small businesses during the formative years of the public web.

AmigaDOS was more than just a file manager: it introduced concepts like command redirection, pipelines, and hierarchical directory navigation to home users at a time when MS-DOS offered only rudimentary batch scripting and no built-in pipes. King’s contributions helped make the Amiga a favorite among developers, artists, and educators—not just for its graphics and sound, but for its underlying usability and programmability. Many users recall booting directly into AmigaDOS without the GUI desktop, treating it as a serious development environment rather than a secondary mode.

His later work with UK Online placed him at the forefront of Britain’s internet adoption wave in the mid-1990s. Though Pipex launched first in 1992, UK Online quickly gained traction by focusing on accessibility, support, and community—a philosophy echoed in user testimonials remembering King as approachable, generous with his time, and deeply committed to empowering non-experts. His dual legacy bridges two pivotal eras: the rise of expressive personal computing and the democratization of networked communication.

Tributes from the retrocomputing and CLI communities highlight how King’s influence extended far beyond code. Several commenters describe AmigaDOS as their first real exposure to command-line thinking—some even crediting it as the foundation for later Linux or Unix work. Others recall troubleshooting modems with UK Online’s early support teams, or learning to write scripts that automated backups and file transfers. In an era where interfaces are increasingly abstracted, King’s work reminds us how much clarity, control, and joy can live in a well-designed prompt.
