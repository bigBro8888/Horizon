Choose boring technology to conserve innovation tokens

News time: 2026-08-14T03:28:57.429620+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In 2015, software engineer Dan McKinley published 'Choose Boring Technology'—a concise, influential essay that challenged the tech industry’s obsession with novelty. At its core, the piece argues that engineering teams should default to mature, well-documented, widely adopted tools—even if they’re less flashy—because stability, predictability, and team familiarity dramatically reduce delivery risk and accelerate iteration. McKinley frames this not as a call for stagnation, but as a disciplined allocation strategy: every organization has only a few 'innovation tokens'—a metaphorical budget for technical risk—and spending them unwisely (e.g., adopting an unproven database just because it’s trending) jeopardizes reliability, onboarding, and long-term maintenance.

The essay gained traction precisely because it named a silent tension in engineering culture: the pressure to innovate versus the quiet cost of operational debt. It resonated during the peak of JavaScript framework churn, when developers cycled through Angular, Ember, React, and countless clones—often without clear functional advantages. McKinley’s advice wasn’t anti-innovation; it was pro-intentionality. He urged teams to spend tokens deliberately—on problems that truly differentiate the product—not on infrastructure plumbing or language syntax.

Over time, the 'innovation tokens' concept evolved beyond its original context. As commenters note, it’s now applied to emerging domains like AI agents: if your agent system is built on Rust, lean into Rust’s ecosystem rather than chasing Zig’s theoretical advantages—even if Zig scores higher on paper. The rationale remains the same: leverage what your team and tooling already understand. Novelty only pays off when it solves a concrete bottleneck—not when it satisfies curiosity or resumes.

Critics rightly point out that 'boring' is contextual and that token budgets can oversimplify tradeoffs. A new database may be essential for real-time analytics at scale, even if it’s unfamiliar. But the essay’s enduring value lies in shifting the default question from 'What’s cool?' to 'What do we need to invent—and what can we safely borrow?' That reframing continues to shape engineering roadmaps, hiring practices, and CTO-level strategy discussions across startups and enterprises alike.
