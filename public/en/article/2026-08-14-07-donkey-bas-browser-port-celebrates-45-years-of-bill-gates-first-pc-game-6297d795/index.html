Donkey.bas browser port celebrates 45 years of Bill Gates’ first PC game

News time: 2026-08-14T03:28:57.429685+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A faithful web-based recreation of DONKEY.BAS—the 131-line driving game co-written by Bill Gates in 1979 and shipped with the original IBM PC in 1981—has launched to mark its 45th anniversary. The browser port, accessible at donkeybas.com, runs entirely in modern web browsers without plugins, using JavaScript to emulate the original IBM PC hardware environment. It preserves the game’s minimalist charm: a top-down view where players steer a car left or right to avoid donkeys wandering across the road—a deceptively simple premise that introduced countless users to programming via its readable BASIC source code.

The timing is no coincidence: the release coincides with renewed interest in early PC history following the 45th anniversary of the IBM PC itself. Developer jkrauska, who built the port, describes it as both a technical homage and a personal reflection—recalling how the game sparked his childhood fascination with what could be achieved in under 150 lines of code. Unlike many retro ports that prioritize visual polish, this version prioritizes behavioral fidelity, replicating the original’s timing, keyboard input handling, and even the constraints of the PC speaker (though some commenters note the current audio implementation exceeds the hardware’s actual capabilities).

Beyond nostalgia, the project sits within a broader wave of browser-based retrocomputing. Tools like PCjs—an open-source, JavaScript-based IBM PC emulator—have made it possible to run not just DONKEY.BAS but full DOS environments, QBasic sessions, and vintage software stacks directly in the browser. This accessibility lowers the barrier for students, historians, and hobbyists to explore foundational computing artifacts without hunting down physical hardware or complex emulation setups.

What makes DONKEY.BAS historically unique isn’t just its authorship—it’s how transparently it exposed the machine’s inner workings. Its source code was bundled with IBM PC DOS, inviting users to read, modify, and learn. That ethos lives on in today’s port: the site includes the full annotated source, links to historical documentation, and even invites contributions. As one Hacker News commenter put it, it’s less a game and more a ‘first lesson in computational thinking’—a reminder that software’s cultural weight often lies not in complexity, but in accessibility and invitation.
