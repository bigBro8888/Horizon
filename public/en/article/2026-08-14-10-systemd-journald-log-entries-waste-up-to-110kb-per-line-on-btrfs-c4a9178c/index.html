systemd-journald log entries waste up to 110KB per line on btrfs

News time: 2026-08-14T03:28:57.429772+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A high-visibility GitHub issue has exposed a startling inefficiency in systemd-journald: a single log entry can consume over 49KB on ext4 and more than 110KB on btrfs filesystems. This isn’t theoretical — users report real-world cases where applications like KDE’s kio file picker or AMDGPU drivers generate thousands of low-signal messages daily, rapidly bloating journal files and exhausting disk space on embedded or resource-constrained systems.

The root cause lies in journald’s binary journal format, which stores metadata, variable-length fields, and padding in fixed-size objects. Unlike plain text logs — where each line maps roughly 1:1 to its content — journald wraps every message in headers, hashes, object alignment boundaries, and redundant field copies. On copy-on-write filesystems like btrfs, this amplifies overhead further due to internal fragmentation and block allocation patterns.

Critics point out that the problem isn’t just size — it’s lack of control. Filtering is coarse-grained (e.g., by priority only), and there’s no built-in way to rate-limit, truncate, or drop logs from specific units or identifiers. As one commenter notes, when amdgpu misbehaves after suspend/resume, flooding the journal with identical kernel messages, administrators must either disable persistence entirely or route logs externally via rsyslog — undermining journald’s stated purpose as a unified logging layer.

This issue has reignited long-standing architectural debates. While journald was designed for robustness and structured querying — inspired by git’s append-only model — many argue its indexing and storage trade-offs favor theoretical elegance over operational pragmatism. The community increasingly treats journald not as a primary log store, but as a transient relay: capturing logs briefly before forwarding them to more flexible, filterable, and space-efficient backends like Loki or Elasticsearch.

Ultimately, the 49KB–110KB anomaly is less about a bug and more about an unaddressed design consequence — one that forces sysadmins to choose between data fidelity and disk sustainability. Until upstream implements field-level compression, selective serialization, or configurable object sizing, workarounds remain the norm.
