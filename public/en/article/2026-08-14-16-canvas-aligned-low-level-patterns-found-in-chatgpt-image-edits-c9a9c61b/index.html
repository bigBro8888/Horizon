Canvas-aligned low-level patterns found in ChatGPT image edits

News time: 2026-08-14T03:28:57.937260+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher investigating persistent visual anomalies in ChatGPT’s generative image editing noticed faint, cloudy textures appearing repeatedly in smooth regions—like walls, skin, and uniform backgrounds—after multiple edit cycles. At first assumed to be ordinary diffusion noise, the pattern behaved unusually: identical edits sometimes improved or worsened the artifact; shifting the image by 20 pixels before editing altered its visibility; and removing a final ‘shift back’ instruction unexpectedly yielded cleaner results. These inconsistencies suggested the artifact wasn’t stochastic—but tied to fixed spatial coordinates on the output canvas.

Further experiments confirmed this hypothesis. When generating multiple ‘completely black’ images (640×640), pixel-level analysis revealed strong reproducibility: non-zero pixel masks overlapped with Jaccard similarity of 0.766 (vs. expected 0.071 for random noise), and cross-correlation peaked sharply at zero lag—meaning the same spatial structure appeared identically aligned across independent generations. Gaussian-blurred versions of these black images even revealed matching large-scale cloud-like patterns, reinforcing that the signal was deterministic and canvas-locked.

The researcher also observed region-dependent behavior during editing: faces and bodies often remained stable while backgrounds degraded, suggesting internal masking or segmentation logic preserves some areas while regenerating others. This selective regeneration may explain why artifacts accumulate unevenly—building up most where content is re-synthesized rather than retained. Crucially, the artifact intensified with iterative edits, implying compounding errors—not one-off noise.

This isn’t evidence of watermarking or intentional steganography, as the author explicitly cautions. Instead, it points to an underexamined side effect of how diffusion-based editing pipelines handle repeated latent-space round trips, spatial alignment constraints, or internal coordinate systems. Recent work like REED-VAE highlights how iterative editing degrades quality due to repeated pixel–latent conversions—a likely contributor here. The findings underscore that ‘editing in place’ isn’t neutral: every operation interacts with fixed architectural scaffolding, making reproducible artifacts a systemic feature—not just a bug.
