NeurIPS 2026 reviewers can update scores without final justifications

News time: 2026-08-14T03:28:57.937326+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent post on the r/MachineLearning subreddit has sparked quiet concern among researchers about transparency in NeurIPS 2026’s peer review process. The user noticed that many reviews display recent 'modified dates'—visible to authors and readers on OpenReview—but observed that high-scoring papers often lack edits after the author rebuttal phase. This stands in contrast to other top-tier conferences, where reviewers are typically required to submit a final justification after the area chair (AC) discussion, effectively locking in or revising their assessment based on collective deliberation.

According to the poster, they spoke with an active NeurIPS 2026 area chair who confirmed that submitting a formal final justification is not mandatory. Instead, reviewers who wish to add context or clarify positions often do so via private comments—visible only to ACs and other reviewers, not authors. That means a public 'modified date' may reflect anything from minor text edits to substantive score changes—yet there’s no visible signal distinguishing them.

This ambiguity raises practical questions: if a review was updated days before decisions are released, does that mean the reviewer changed their score? Did the AC request revisions? Or was it simply a typo fix? Without official documentation or public guidelines clarifying what triggers a modified timestamp, authors are left interpreting timestamps as proxy signals—despite NeurIPS’ stated commitment to fairness and openness.

The issue isn’t new. Past NeurIPS experiments—including replication of the famous 2014 inconsistency study in 2021—have shown that reviewer agreement remains modest, with substantial variation in scoring even among qualified experts. In that context, unexplained modifications risk amplifying perceptions of arbitrariness. While NeurIPS has invested heavily in improving review quality—through structured forms, reviewer training, and AC moderation—the visibility of timestamps without interpretive guardrails may unintentionally erode trust in the final outcome.
