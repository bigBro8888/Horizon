Firefox is now the only major browser fully supporting uBlock Origin

News time: 2026-08-15T02:18:21.278715+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Firefox has become the sole major browser that still fully supports uBlock Origin—the widely trusted, open-source ad and tracker blocker—after Chromium-based browsers like Chrome, Edge, Brave, and Opera have effectively disabled its core functionality. This shift stems from Google’s enforcement of Manifest V3, a new extension platform standard that removes or restricts powerful APIs uBlock Origin relies on, especially webRequestBlocking. While uBlock Origin remains installable in Chrome and others, its ability to block ads and trackers in real time is severely degraded—reducing effectiveness by up to 80% in some tests.

The change isn’t technical inevitability—it’s policy-driven. Google framed Manifest V3 as an improvement for privacy, security, and performance, but critics—including the Electronic Frontier Foundation—argue it primarily serves Google’s advertising business by weakening content blockers that interfere with ad delivery. Firefox, by contrast, continues to support Manifest V2 indefinitely and has even enhanced its review process for high-impact extensions like uBlock Origin: Mozilla staff manually audit its code on every update to ensure no malicious changes slip in.

This divergence highlights a deeper tension in web platform governance. As Chrome commands over 75% of the desktop browser market, its technical decisions effectively become de facto web standards—even when they erode user control. Users who rely on robust privacy tools now face a stark choice: switch to Firefox, accept diminished protection in Chromium browsers, or resort to workarounds like enterprise-sideloaded MV3 ports (e.g., r58Playz’s unofficial uBlock MV3 build), which require administrative privileges and aren’t available to average users.

The community response reflects both frustration and resolve. Commenters note how browsing feels broken without strong ad blocking—comparing modern web pages to ‘slot machines from hell’—and affirm deep resistance to surrendering control: ‘You can have my ad blocker when you take it from my cold dead fingers.’ Ultimately, this isn’t just about one extension; it’s about whether users retain the ability to shape their own web experience—or must conform to platforms optimized for surveillance capitalism.
