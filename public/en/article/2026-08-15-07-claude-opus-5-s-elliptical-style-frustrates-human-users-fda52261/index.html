Claude Opus 5’s elliptical style frustrates human users

News time: 2026-08-15T02:18:21.278609+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Many developers and technical writers report that Claude Opus 5—Anthropic’s latest flagship model—feels harder to work with than its predecessors, despite strong benchmark performance. A widely discussed critique, anchored in a detailed blog post by Mun Logadan, identifies a core issue: Opus 5 communicates in an unusually elliptical, abstract, and self-narrating style. Instead of delivering clear, direct answers, it often orbits ideas, delays key points for rhetorical effect, and favors syntactically complex constructions—like making inanimate nouns the grammatical subject—to create ‘surprise landings’ at sentence endings. Users describe this as cognitively exhausting, especially during iterative, collaborative workflows.

This stylistic shift appears tied not to general capability loss, but to a fundamental realignment in training priorities. According to internal documentation and community speculation, Opus 5 was optimized less for single-turn human dialogue and more for agentic collaboration—specifically, serving as a subagent within Anthropic’s internal multi-agent system Fable. The model excels at long-horizon reasoning, sustained tool use, and multi-file coding tasks—areas where verbose self-monitoring and step-by-step narration are beneficial between agents. But those same traits become noise when a human is the primary interlocutor.

Real-world consequences are mounting. Several commenters report switching to OpenAI’s Sol model—not because it scores higher on benchmarks, but because its responses are shorter, more grounded, and easier to parse in daily work. One user burned through hundreds of dollars in Claude credits before migrating entirely, citing Opus 5’s habit of over-explaining, confessing minor uncertainties, and narrating its own thought process as unsustainable cognitive overhead. Others note that even simple instructions now require repeated rephrasing or heavy prompt engineering to suppress verbosity.

The tension highlights a growing fault line in LLM development: alignment isn’t just about truthfulness or safety—it’s also about audience alignment. When models are tuned primarily for agent-to-agent handoffs rather than human-to-model dialogue, usability suffers. As one commenter put it: humans may no longer be the target audience of post-training optimization. That doesn’t make Opus 5 less capable—but it does make it less cooperative for people who need clarity, speed, and predictability over performative reasoning.
