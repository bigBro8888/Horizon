RISC-V’s pragmatic design trades elegance for openness and implementability

News time: 2026-08-15T02:18:21.278753+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Dmitry Grinberg’s essay 'RISC-V: They should have known better' offers a rare blend of technical rigor and candid reflection from someone who has both designed CPUs professionally and built them as a hobbyist. Rather than dismissing RISC-V outright or praising it uncritically, he zeroes in on two defining—and contested—features: the compressed instruction set (C extension) and the architecture’s open extensibility model. His critique isn’t about whether RISC-V works, but whether its design choices reflect deep architectural foresight—or merely expedient compromises.

He highlights how the C extension, intended to improve code density, ends up severely constrained: a 16-bit compressed store instruction can only encode byte offsets from 0 to 3. That makes it nearly useless for common structure accesses—even modest structs with more than four fields quickly outgrow its reach. Similarly, he notes that while RISC-V’s modular extension system empowers hardware designers to add custom instructions, it also fragments software toolchains and complicates portability unless tightly coordinated across vendors and compilers.

Yet the essay doesn’t conclude with rejection. Instead, it acknowledges RISC-V’s real-world appeal: it’s licensable without legal risk, supported by mature open-source toolchains (GCC, LLVM), and small enough to implement on an FPGA in a weekend. As one HN commenter put it, RISC-V satisfies the two non-negotiable needs of a hobbyist CPU designer—compiler support and freedom from IP litigation—while leaving room to ‘fix in post’ through microarchitectural tuning or custom extensions.

The broader significance lies beyond silicon. China’s heavy investment in RISC-V isn’t driven by its instruction encoding elegance—it’s a strategic bet on sovereignty over compute infrastructure. In an era where licensing control over ARM or x86 can be weaponized geopolitically, RISC-V’s open specification is itself the feature. Its technical imperfections are tolerated because its political and economic utility is unmatched among viable ISAs. As another commenter observed, RISC-V proves that a globally adopted, royalty-free, public ISA is not just possible—it’s already happening.
