Developer builds RSS-to-e-ink newspaper system to cut phone use

News time: 2026-08-15T02:18:21.278772+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A developer named Jonny has built a personal automation system that transforms curated RSS feeds into daily, newspaper-style PDFs optimized for e-ink readers—like the Kindle or reMarkable. Each morning, his script fetches articles from selected sources (e.g., long-form blogs and newsletters), formats them with clean typography, section headers, and pagination, then generates a single PDF resembling a traditional broadsheet. He then transfers it wirelessly to his e-ink device and reads it offline—no notifications, no scrolling, no screen glare.

The project isn’t about replacing news apps—it’s about reintroducing intentionality. Jonny designed it after noticing how smartphone-based reading fragmented his attention: endless swiping, algorithmic recommendations, and constant context-switching eroded deep focus. By compressing a day’s worth of reading into one static, tactile-feeling document, he recreates the ritual of sitting down with a physical paper—minus the ink smudges and delivery logistics.

Technically, the pipeline combines open-source tools: RSS feed parsing via Python’s feedparser, HTML-to-PDF conversion using WeasyPrint (for precise layout control), and optional Calibre integration for metadata and device syncing. It runs on a local machine or Raspberry Pi, triggered by cron—no cloud dependency or third-party service. The output is deliberately minimal: no images (except embedded SVG icons), no JavaScript, no tracking pixels—just text, headlines, and whitespace.

Critics rightly point out real-world friction: unreliable full-text RSS feeds, missing images in gallery-heavy content, and the manual effort required to maintain feed lists and formatting rules. As one commenter noted, the system shines for consistent, long-form publishers—but stumbles with blogs that only syndicate excerpts. Still, its value lies not in perfection but in provocation: it asks whether our tools should serve attention, not engagement metrics. For readers tired of digital overload, it offers a working blueprint—not just for better reading, but for reclaiming time.
