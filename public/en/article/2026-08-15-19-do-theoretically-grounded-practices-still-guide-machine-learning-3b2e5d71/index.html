Do theoretically grounded practices still guide machine learning?

News time: 2026-08-15T02:18:21.768598+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent Reddit post titled 'Are there any theoretically-guided practices left in machine learning nowadays?' has reignited a long-simmering debate: whether modern ML development is still meaningfully shaped by mathematical theory—or whether it has become, in practice, an empirical craft dominated by large-scale experimentation and heuristic tuning.

The post traces how foundational ideas once taught as gospel—like the bias-variance tradeoff, overfitting from excessive model capacity, strict test-set isolation to avoid data leakage, and optimizer selection based on convergence guarantees—were distilled from early statistical learning theory. These principles appeared in textbooks, shaped interview questions, and guided curriculum design for years. Yet many have been quietly undermined by real-world deep learning successes: models with billions of parameters generalize well despite vanishingly small sample-to-parameter ratios; Adam optimizer is used ubiquitously even though its theoretical convergence guarantees are weaker than SGD under standard assumptions; and practitioners routinely peek at validation sets, tune hyperparameters aggressively, and stack models without invoking ensemble theory.

This isn’t just about outdated maxims—it’s about epistemic shift. As one commenter noted, theoretical generalization bounds (e.g., Rademacher complexity or PAC-Bayes) remain mathematically sound but often produce vacuous numerical bounds for modern architectures—so large they offer no actionable guidance. Meanwhile, empirical phenomena like double descent and implicit regularization lack clean theoretical accounts yet inform daily engineering decisions. The gap isn’t between theory and practice per se, but between which theories survive translation into usable heuristics—and which get abandoned when experiments outpace explanation.

Still, theory hasn’t vanished. Researchers continue to use tools like stability analysis to justify differential privacy, or spectral norms to constrain adversarial robustness. Some optimization choices—e.g., using SGD with momentum for convex problems—retain theoretical grounding. But these are exceptions rather than defaults. Most practitioners select components not because of provable guarantees, but because benchmarks say they work—often across diverse tasks, datasets, and hardware. That pragmatic consensus, built on shared experience rather than shared theorems, now forms the de facto foundation of ML engineering.
