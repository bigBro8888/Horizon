Unicode contains real code points for fictional characters

News time: 2026-08-16T02:33:38.585470+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A haunting anomaly lives inside Unicode: dozens of code points assigned to characters that do not exist in any known language, historical script, or attested corpus. These so-called 'ghost characters' — like the mysterious U+5F41 (彁) — appear in official Unicode charts and are fully supported by fonts, operating systems, and programming languages, yet no living or documented language uses them. The phenomenon traces back to errors in source standards, especially Japanese Industrial Standards (JIS) character sets, where mis-scanned glyphs, typographical artifacts, or artistic inventions were mistakenly encoded as legitimate characters and later inherited by Unicode during CJK unification.

The problem is not merely academic. Once a code point enters Unicode, it is effectively frozen under the Unicode Stability Policy: removal or reassignment would break backward compatibility across billions of devices and trillions of documents. So even when a character is confirmed to be a phantom — such as 彁, widely believed to stem from a corrupted newspaper scan — it remains permanently embedded in digital infrastructure. This creates subtle but real challenges for natural language processing, font rendering, and text normalization, particularly in East Asian contexts where character identity is tightly coupled with meaning and usage.

Ghost characters also reveal deeper tensions between standardization and reality. Unicode aims to encode all characters used in human writing — but who decides what counts as 'used'? A single mislabeled glyph in a 1970s JIS document can become an immutable part of global computing. Artists have exploited this: Xu Bing’s 1988 installation A Book from the Sky featured 4,000 hand-crafted Chinese-like characters, none of which meant anything — yet over a dozen accidentally matched real Unicode code points. Such overlaps blur the line between error, art, and encoding authority.

What makes ghost characters especially intriguing is their quiet persistence. Unlike emoji or private-use area symbols, they occupy the same semantic space as real logographs — they look authentic, sort correctly, and even appear in dictionaries. They are not placeholders or design experiments; they are ghosts with passports, legally recognized yet ontologically vacant. As one commenter wryly noted, we could now use 彊 to name 'a concept so alien it has no name' — turning bureaucratic accident into poetic utility.
