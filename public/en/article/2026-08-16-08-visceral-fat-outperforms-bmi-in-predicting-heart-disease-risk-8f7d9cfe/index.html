Visceral fat outperforms BMI in predicting heart disease risk

News time: 2026-08-16T02:33:38.585394+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A major new analysis from the American College of Cardiology (ACC) confirms that visceral abdominal fat — the deep fat surrounding internal organs — is a more accurate predictor of future heart disease than body mass index (BMI). The findings, based on long-term follow-up of over 260,000 individuals across multiple cohorts, challenge decades of clinical reliance on BMI as a primary screening tool for cardiovascular risk. While BMI remains widely used because it’s simple and inexpensive to calculate, this study underscores its limitations: it cannot distinguish between muscle mass and fat, nor does it reflect where fat is stored — a critical factor in metabolic health.

The research highlights that excess visceral fat drives inflammation, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia — all key pathways leading to atherosclerosis and cardiac events. Unlike subcutaneous fat (the kind just under the skin), visceral fat is metabolically active and releases fatty acids and cytokines directly into the portal circulation, affecting liver function and systemic metabolism. This biological distinction explains why two people with identical BMIs can have vastly different cardiovascular outcomes — one may carry most fat subcutaneously and remain metabolically healthy, while the other harbors dangerous visceral deposits despite appearing only moderately overweight.

Clinicians are increasingly turning to more precise metrics like waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, and imaging-based assessments (e.g., CT or MRI) to estimate visceral fat. However, these tools face barriers: waist measurements lack standardization, and imaging is costly and impractical for population-level screening. That’s why researchers are also exploring accessible proxies — such as blood biomarkers, ECG-derived indicators, and even gut microbiota profiles — to identify high-risk individuals earlier and more affordably.

The implications extend beyond diagnostics. Community discussion around the finding reflects growing interest in interventions targeting visceral fat specifically — including dietary strategies like resistant starch consumption (found in cooled potatoes, green bananas, and legumes), which may reshape gut flora and support fat loss. Still, experts caution that no single metric replaces comprehensive risk assessment: age, blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking status, and family history remain essential. Ultimately, this work doesn’t discard BMI — it refines how we interpret it, urging clinicians to look deeper when BMI signals potential risk.
