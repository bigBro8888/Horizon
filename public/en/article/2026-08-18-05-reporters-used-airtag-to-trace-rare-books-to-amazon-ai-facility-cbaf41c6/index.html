Reporters used AirTag to trace rare books to Amazon AI facility

News time: 2026-08-18T02:20:11.395464+00:00 · Source: rss

In a striking feat of digital-age investigative journalism, reporters from 404 Media tracked a shipment of approximately 1,000 rare and out-of-print books — purchased anonymously on the online marketplace Biblio — all the way to an Amazon logistics facility in Las Vegas. The team collaborated with a cooperating bookseller who concealed an Apple AirTag inside one of the volumes before shipping. Over several days, the tracker’s location data, relayed via Apple’s Find My network, led investigators to the LAS8 fulfillment center — specifically to its VGT3 section, a known hub for large-scale book processing.

What made this discovery especially telling was not just the destination, but the signage: at the VGT3 entrance stood a bold logo featuring a red Tyrannosaurus rex gripping a book with claws — an image widely interpreted as darkly symbolic rather than celebratory. Further corroboration came from internal discussions among Amazon employees on public forums, who confirmed that VGT3 conducts destructive scanning: books are physically disassembled, pages fed into high-speed scanners, and then discarded. This process yields high-fidelity digital text for training large language models — but leaves no physical copy intact.

The investigation adds concrete evidence to mounting concerns about how AI companies source training data. For over a year, booksellers have reported unusual bulk orders — often for obscure academic titles, pre-2022 imprints, or fragile antiquarian editions — placed by buyers who showed little interest in price or condition. Similar patterns were previously documented in reporting on Anthropic’s book acquisition spree in early 2024, where court filings revealed millions of books were bought and destroyed for scanning. But unlike prior reports based on invoices or worker testimony, this case offers direct, real-time physical tracking — a methodological first in AI data provenance investigations.

Critics argue that while purchasing books legally may satisfy narrow copyright formalities, it sidesteps ethical questions about consent, cultural preservation, and the long-term devaluation of physical knowledge infrastructure. Meanwhile, publishers and authors’ groups continue to press for transparency and opt-out mechanisms — demands increasingly echoed by lawmakers drafting AI accountability legislation in the EU and U.S. Congress. This AirTag-led trace doesn’t prove illegality, but it does make the opaque supply chain of AI training data suddenly, undeniably visible.
