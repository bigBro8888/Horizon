Quake’s 1996 shareware CD used trivial file-header encryption

News time: 2026-08-18T02:20:11.644264+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In August 1996, id Software released a physical shareware CD-ROM for Quake — not as a premium product, but as an experiment in direct distribution. Packaged with the NIN soundtrack and bonus id titles like Doom II and Wolfenstein 3D, the disc was meant to let players try the first episode, then call an 800 number to order the full game with a password unlock. But within just 39 days, the hacker group GNOMON released Quakecrk.zip — a tool that decrypted every game on the disc. The ‘encryption’ turned out to be little more than XOR obfuscation applied only to the first 12 bytes of each file header, easily reversed with basic hex inspection and a few minutes of reverse engineering.

This wasn’t a failure of technical ambition — it was deliberate minimalism. As Fabien Sanglard’s deep-dive analysis reveals, id Software had long rejected traditional DRM: no serial numbers, no activation servers, no copy protection beyond this token gesture. John Carmack even publicly criticized DRM as counterproductive, arguing that ease of copying helped games spread virally — especially among students and modders who would later become developers themselves. The Quake shareware CD wasn’t supposed to hold up; it was designed to be copied, shared, and tinkered with.

The disc also reflects a transitional moment in PC gaming hardware. CD-ROM drives were still relatively new and expensive in 1996 — Wing Commander III (1994) and Phantasmagoria (1995) had already pushed their limits with full-motion video — yet Quake’s assets barely filled the disc. Its 650 MB capacity dwarfed the ~100 MB needed for the shareware episode, leaving room for extras but also exposing how early CD distribution often prioritized novelty over density.

Today, the disc is a time capsule: a pragmatic artifact from an era when openness fueled innovation. Its cracked files live on in countless Quake mods, speedrun tools, and source ports — all built on the foundation of freely available, easily understood data. As one veteran recalls, he’s been shuffling those same decrypted ID1 directory files across computers for thirty years — proof that id’s permissive approach didn’t just survive cracking; it enabled longevity.
