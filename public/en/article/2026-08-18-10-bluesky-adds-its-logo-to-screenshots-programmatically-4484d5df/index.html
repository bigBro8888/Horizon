Bluesky adds its logo to screenshots programmatically

News time: 2026-08-18T02:20:11.644219+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Bluesky, the decentralized social platform built on the AT Protocol, has implemented a subtle but consequential UI behavior: when users take screenshots of its iOS app, a small, semi-transparent Bluesky logo appears in the bottom-right corner of the resulting image — even though it was not visible on screen at the time of capture. This isn’t a static watermark baked into the app’s interface; instead, it’s injected programmatically using iOS’s screenshot detection and overlay APIs. The feature activates only after the screenshot is taken, leveraging system-level hooks that let apps respond to the screenshot event — a capability originally intended for accessibility or debugging tools, not branding.

The technical mechanism relies on iOS’s UIApplication.userDidTakeScreenshotNotification, which fires when a screenshot occurs. Bluesky listens for this notification and then overlays a branded view just before the image is saved to Photos. Crucially, the logo does not appear on-screen during normal use — it materializes only in the saved screenshot file. This distinction blurs the line between user-controlled output and platform-controlled artifact, raising questions about consent, transparency, and digital provenance.

Critics argue that screenshots are meant to be faithful representations of what appeared on screen — a fundamental expectation of digital literacy and trust. As one commenter put it: “It’s not a picture of your app; it’s a picture of my screen.” Others draw parallels to Apple’s early “Sent from my iPhone” email signature — a viral marketing tactic disguised as convenience. Supporters counter that the overlay is unobtrusive, non-intrusive to content, and preferable to permanent on-screen branding — especially since Bluesky intentionally mimics familiar microblogging UI patterns and needs distinguishable identity.

What makes this case notable isn’t just the technique, but how it surfaces deeper tensions in mobile design ethics: who owns the output of a user action? When an OS grants apps the ability to alter the result of a system-level operation like screenshotting, does that power require opt-in, disclosure, or even regulatory scrutiny? For developers, it’s a reminder that API access doesn’t imply ethical neutrality — and for users, it’s a quiet prompt to reconsider what “capturing” really means on today’s tightly controlled platforms.
