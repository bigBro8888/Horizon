Sun Clock web app visualizes real-time solar position with precision

News time: 2026-08-18T02:20:11.644568+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Sun Clock is a sleek, interactive web application that shows exactly where the sun is in the sky—right now—for any location on Earth. Built as a clean, responsive interface, it displays not just the sun’s current azimuth and altitude, but also key daylight events: sunrise, sunset, solar noon, twilight phases, and the much-photographed golden hour. The app uses geolocation by default, but users can manually enter coordinates or click anywhere on its embedded world map to explore solar behavior across time zones and latitudes.

What sets Sun Clock apart is its reliance on rigorous astronomical computation—not approximations or simplified models. It leverages the open-source suncalc library, a widely trusted JavaScript tool for calculating solar and lunar positions with high fidelity. As noted by suncalc’s author Mourner in community comments, the underlying library recently underwent a major precision overhaul—improving accuracy for edge cases like polar regions and extreme seasonal variations.

The app’s design invites both casual curiosity and technical scrutiny. Users quickly notice subtle UI decisions: how sunrise/sunset times shift when crossing midnight, whether ‘golden hour’ is defined by fixed clock hours or dynamic solar angles, and how the interface handles locations where the sun never rises or sets (e.g., northern Norway in winter). These aren’t bugs—they’re deliberate confrontations with real-world astronomical complexity. One commenter points out that near the Arctic Circle, golden hour can stretch for many hours—or even all day—depending on season, challenging simplistic time-based definitions.

Beyond visualization, Sun Clock sparks thoughtful discussion about time semantics, geographic nuance, and user expectations. Suggestions include comparing solar times across multiple locations simultaneously, adding historical or projected sun paths, and enhancing calendar views with static solar diagrams. It joins other tools like Sun Path—which traces centuries-long solar drift—to form a growing ecosystem of accessible, accurate solar literacy tools for educators, photographers, architects, and outdoor enthusiasts.
