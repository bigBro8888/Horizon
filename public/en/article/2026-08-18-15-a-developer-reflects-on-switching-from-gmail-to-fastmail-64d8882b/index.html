A developer reflects on switching from Gmail to Fastmail

News time: 2026-08-18T02:20:11.644588+00:00 · Source: hackernews

After years of relying on Gmail, a software developer decided to make the switch to Fastmail—not as a protest or privacy stunt, but as a deliberate recalibration of personal infrastructure. Their blog post isn’t a technical migration guide, nor a feature-by-feature comparison. Instead, it’s a candid reflection on how email needs evolve: from wanting convenience and AI-powered sorting in early career, to valuing control, predictability, and quiet reliability later on.

The author highlights that Fastmail doesn’t try to interpret your inbox—no automatic tabs for Primary, Social, or Promotions; no algorithmic filtering based on inferred intent. Instead, it offers powerful but explicit tools: robust filtering rules, custom folders, and full IMAP support. This shifts responsibility back to the user—but for someone who’s built habits around intentional organization, that’s not a drawback. It’s alignment. The interface is lean, fast, and ad-free, with no data harvesting baked into the service model.

Privacy emerges as a quiet but consistent theme—not as a headline-grabbing claim, but as a lived experience. Unlike Gmail, where email content fuels ad targeting across Google’s ecosystem, Fastmail treats messages as private by default. Its business model is simple: users pay $3–$9/month, and in return get storage, custom domains, masked emails, and responsive support. There are no ads, no third-party data sharing, and no machine learning trained on your correspondence.

Still, the transition wasn’t purely utopian. The author acknowledges trade-offs: losing Gmail’s seamless integration with Calendar, Docs, and Meet; missing smart replies and package-tracking summaries; and accepting that some conveniences—like one-click unsubscribe or AI-generated summaries—are simply absent. Yet those gaps feel intentional, not accidental. As one commenter put it, ‘What I want now is just the big box at the top telling me my package is arriving today’—a reminder that email’s role in our lives keeps shifting, and so should our tools.
