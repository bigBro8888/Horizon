Amazon charges brands a hidden tax through ad-dominated search

News time: 2026-08-19T02:23:11.372716+00:00 · Source: hackernews

When you type “Seth Godin The Knot” into Amazon’s search bar, you might expect to see the official book — especially since it’s a trademarked title. Instead, you’re likely served a paid ad for a competing product, ranked above the actual result. Seth Godin’s 2026 essay ‘The Amazon Tax’ frames this not as a quirk of e-commerce, but as a systemic extraction: a toll levied on both brands and shoppers by a platform that controls the gateway to purchase intent.

Amazon processes nearly 6 billion searches per month. Research shows the top three results capture 72% of all clicks — and increasingly, those top spots are reserved for advertisers, not organic or trademark-protected listings. Even when users search for a specific brand or product name, Amazon prioritizes sponsored placements over the genuine item — sometimes burying it beyond the first page. This isn’t incidental; it’s engineered. As one commenter notes, sorting by ‘Best Sellers’ instead of the default ‘Featured Items’ removes ads entirely from results — revealing how deeply advertising is baked into the interface.

The critique goes beyond convenience or pricing. Godin and legal commentators raise serious concerns about trademark infringement and consumer deception. If someone searches for a registered product name — say, ‘Dyson Airwrap’ — and Amazon surfaces a cheaper knockoff at the top while demoting Dyson’s own listing, does that violate trademark law? Does it constitute fraud when the platform knows the user’s intent but monetizes it against their interest? These aren’t hypotheticals: 85% of Amazon ads are search-targeted, and their conversion rates rival top organic results — meaning advertisers pay premium prices to hijack intent.

What makes this especially potent is Amazon’s dual role: it’s both marketplace operator and competitor. When a brand’s own product is outranked by Amazon’s private-label version — promoted via paid placement — the conflict of interest becomes structural. Users pay more (via inflated ad costs passed on in prices), brands lose control over their customer relationships, and competition erodes. The only consistent winners are Amazon’s ad revenue streams — which grew to $53.4 billion in 2025, now surpassing its cloud division in profitability.

This isn’t just about Amazon. As commenters point out, Google, Meta, and others replicate similar models — turning search and discovery into toll roads. But Amazon stands apart because its search doesn’t just lead to commerce; it is commerce. That blurring of gatekeeper and merchant intensifies the stakes — and makes regulatory scrutiny, legal challenges, and user-level workarounds like sorting adjustments more urgent than ever.
