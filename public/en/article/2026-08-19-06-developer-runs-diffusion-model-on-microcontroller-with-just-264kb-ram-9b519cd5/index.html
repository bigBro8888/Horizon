Developer runs diffusion model on microcontroller with just 264KB RAM

News time: 2026-08-19T02:23:11.509806+00:00 · Source: reddit

A hobbyist developer has successfully trained and deployed a diffusion model capable of generating 32×32 pixel images on the Shrike Lite — a low-cost, open-source microcontroller board featuring only 264KB of SRAM. What makes this project remarkable isn’t just the model’s tiny footprint, but the empirical lesson it delivers about hardware acceleration trade-offs at the extreme edge: despite adding two parallel INT8 multiply–accumulate (MAC) engines on the onboard FPGA to speed up inference, the system performed slower than the MCU-only version.

The Shrike Lite combines an RP2040 microcontroller — familiar from Raspberry Pi Pico boards — with a small 1120-LUT FPGA, making it ideal for embedded ML experiments that demand both programmability and custom logic. The developer trained a lightweight diffusion architecture from scratch, heavily quantized to INT8 and optimized for memory locality. Due to tight SRAM constraints, every tensor had to be carefully staged, reused, or recomputed — eliminating standard caching strategies used in larger systems.

When the FPGA accelerators were enabled, throughput expectations were high: each MAC engine used 16-bit accumulation and was pipelined for efficient fixed-point arithmetic. But real-world performance revealed a bottleneck not in computation, but in data movement. Frequent reads and writes between the MCU’s SRAM and the FPGA’s local registers created severe I/O contention — slowing down the overall pipeline. As a result, inference took ~220 seconds per image with FPGA acceleration versus just ~70 seconds using the RP2040 alone.

The generated images reflect the harsh realities of ultra-constrained deployment: many appear noisy or distorted due to aggressive quantization and limited model capacity. Yet some outputs show coherent structure — proof that meaningful generative behavior can survive extreme compression and hardware limitation. This isn’t production-ready AI, but it’s a valuable stress test for embedded ML toolchains, compiler optimizations, and co-design intuition. It reminds engineers that faster hardware blocks don’t always yield faster systems — especially when memory bandwidth and interface latency dominate the critical path.
