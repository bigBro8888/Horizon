A satirical slideshow exposes management consulting theater

News time: 2026-08-19T02:23:11.372868+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A deliberately jarring, low-fidelity web slideshow titled Beware Management Consultants—hosted on Iceland’s quirky corporate website—has gone viral among tech and organizational design circles. Rather than presenting data or case studies, it uses absurd navigation, broken interactions, and cryptic narration to mimic the experience of enduring a high-priced consulting engagement: endless workshops, shifting deliverables, and change initiatives that vanish as quickly as they’re announced. Each slide feels like a parody of real consulting artifacts—Gantt charts with no dates, org charts with floating titles, and mission statements that dissolve before you finish reading.

The piece doesn’t name specific firms, but its targets are unmistakable: global strategy consultancies whose business models rely on recurring engagements, perceived expertise, and the illusion of transformation. It highlights how consultants often inherit problems they didn’t create—and then repackage them as new challenges requiring more consulting. The satire lands because it mirrors real pain points: misaligned incentives (consultants rewarded for billable hours, not outcomes), the outsourcing of accountability, and the substitution of process for progress.

What makes the work especially resonant is its medium: the intentionally terrible UX isn’t just a gag—it’s a rhetorical device. By forcing users to click through confusing menus, wait for nonsensical animations, and decipher fragmented text, it replicates the cognitive load and disorientation many employees feel during multi-month consulting rollouts. As one commenter noted, the bad UX actually improves attention retention in an age of distraction—a darkly ironic twist on usability principles.

Beyond laughter, the piece sparks sober reflection about who really owns organizational knowledge and change. Internal teams often understand context, constraints, and history—but lack authority or budget. Consultants bring authority and budget—but rarely context. The result is a cycle where ‘transformation’ becomes synonymous with churn: new acronyms, new dashboards, new roles—none of which alter underlying power structures or decision-making rhythms. In that light, the slideshow isn’t just satire; it’s a diagnostic tool disguised as a glitch.
