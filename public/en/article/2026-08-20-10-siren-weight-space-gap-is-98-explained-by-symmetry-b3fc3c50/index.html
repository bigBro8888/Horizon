SIREN weight-space gap is 98% explained by symmetry

News time: 2026-08-20T02:21:37.165446+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new empirical study quantifies how much of the notorious 'weight-space perception gap' in SIREN networks—where models trained from the same initialization yield interpretable weights, but independently trained ones do not—is actually due to parameter symmetries. The author doesn’t just assume symmetry explains the gap; instead, they dissect the claim into three distinct, testable hypotheses: (1) that the parameterization admits a symmetry group, (2) that correcting for that group improves weight-space prediction, and (3) that the group alone suffices to reproduce the full performance drop seen between shared-init and random-init settings. Using ~1.8 million fitted SIREN-based implicit neural representations (INRs) across MNIST, FashionMNIST, and CIFAR-10, the work isolates symmetry effects via controlled perturbations—randomly applying only function-preserving transformations while holding the represented function fixed.

The symmetry structure turns out to be richer than standard permutation-and-sign flips. For a single hidden layer, the relevant group is the wreath product D_inf ≀ S_n, where D_inf = ℤ ⋊ ℤ₂ captures infinite dihedral actions—including integer-multiple-of-π phase shifts—which are affine, not linear, and thus missed by conventional monomial matrix symmetry models. At depth two, cross-layer coupling complicates things, so the author constructs exact invariants using second-layer Gram matrices rather than treating neurons in isolation.

Empirically, applying only these symmetry transformations to shared-init weights—leaving functions unchanged—reduces MNIST weight-space classification accuracy from 95.3% (shared-init) to 14.9%, nearly matching the observed 14.7% accuracy of fully independent training. That is, symmetry scatter alone accounts for 79.1 of the 80.4-point gap—98% of the total degradation. Breaking it down: sign flips cause ~63 points of loss, neuron relabeling ~15, and integer phase shifts ~1.

Yet the most revealing finding is conceptual: even a perfect symmetry quotient—mapping weights to their orbit under D_inf ≀ S_n—achieves only 91.7% accuracy, far below querying the network as a function (95.3% at lower FLOP cost). This suggests weight-space learning’s main advantage may not be information—it’s computation. If orbit equivalence is informationally equivalent to function access, then the real motivation for weight-space methods must lie in efficiency, scalability, or integration with weight-generation pipelines—not representational fidelity.
