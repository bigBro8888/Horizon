Simon Willison defends lines of code as a meaningful AI coding metric

News time: 2026-08-20T02:21:36.402168+00:00 · Source: rss

In a recent episode of the Talking Postgres podcast, software engineer and technologist Simon Willison challenged a long-standing dogma in software engineering: that lines of code (LoC) are a useless — even harmful — measure of productivity. His argument isn’t a throwback to waterfall-era metrics, but a carefully qualified claim rooted in the new realities of AI-assisted development. Willison contends that while LoC is indeed meaningless for human engineers working under open-ended design constraints, it does become a surprisingly useful proxy when evaluating AI coding agents — precisely because those agents operate under hard, observable limits on output quality and conceptual coherence.

He points out that before AI, a truly exceptional day for a senior engineer might yield 200 lines of production-ready, tested, and maintainable code — and most days delivered far less. With AI agents, hitting 1,000 lines of debugged, working, production-level code in a day is now possible. That leap isn’t trivial; it reflects real acceleration — if the resulting code meets the same standards of correctness, test coverage, and architectural clarity. The catch? Achieving that consistency demands deep expertise: prompt engineering, rigorous validation, architectural oversight, and iterative refinement — the very skills that define senior engineering practice.

Willison then pivots to a deeper concern: conceptual integrity. Borrowing from Fred Brooks’ The Mythical Man-Month, he describes it as the unifying logic that makes a system feel coherent — where every component fits, decisions align, and surprises are rare. AI agents, he warns, erode this integrity by making feature expansion dangerously cheap. A prompt can generate a new module in minutes — but without disciplined design governance, the system accumulates ‘weird bumps,’ like rooms added haphazardly to the Winchester Mystery House. The low cost of creation removes the natural friction that once forced teams to prioritize and unify their vision.

Ultimately, Willison’s thesis is not about reverting to naive metrics, but about recognizing that AI changes what constrains software development. Speed is no longer the bottleneck — cognitive load and design discipline are. So while one engineer empowered by AI may write code 100× faster, they still need teammates to share the burden of understanding, reviewing, and maintaining that expanded codebase. Productivity gains, he reminds us, don’t eliminate the need for collaboration — they just shift its focus.
