EU confirms AI-generated works lack copyright protection

News time: 2026-08-21T02:36:17.709865+00:00 · Source: hackernews

The European Union has reaffirmed that AI-generated content—whether text, code, images, or translations—cannot be protected by copyright under current law. This position stems not from new legislation, but from a consistent interpretation of existing EU copyright frameworks: only works resulting from the intellectual creation of a natural person qualify for protection. The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) and national courts across member states have repeatedly held that authorship requires human creative input, originality, and personal expression—none of which an AI system can legally supply.

This clarification carries immediate weight for developers, open-source contributors, and creative professionals relying on generative AI tools. For instance, software written entirely by an AI model—without meaningful human intervention in structure, logic, or design—falls outside copyright’s scope. That means no enforceable license (e.g., MIT or GPL) can attach to such code, because those licenses presuppose a valid underlying copyright. Similarly, AI-assisted translations, summaries, or data analyses may lack independent copyright unless a human demonstrably shaped their expressive form—not just prompted or edited superficially.

Legal experts point out that the threshold isn’t about using AI, but about how much a human contributed. As one commentator noted, hiring a human translator yields a new copyrighted work; using an AI for the same task does not—even if the human later reviews or refines the output. Courts look for evidence of creative choices: selection, arrangement, stylistic decisions, or structural innovation. Mere prompting, filtering, or light editing typically fails to meet that bar.

The implications extend beyond ownership. Without copyright, AI-generated outputs cannot be licensed, assigned, or enforced against infringement—raising concerns for commercial platforms, academic publishers, and public repositories. A recent European Parliament study recommends developing objective criteria to assess human contribution in AI-assisted works, warning that legal uncertainty could stifle investment and innovation. Meanwhile, mandatory AI-content labelling rules—set to become binding in August 2026—will coexist with this copyright void, highlighting a growing regulatory split between transparency and rights.
