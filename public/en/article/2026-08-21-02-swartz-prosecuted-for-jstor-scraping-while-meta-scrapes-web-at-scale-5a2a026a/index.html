Swartz prosecuted for JSTOR scraping while Meta scrapes web at scale

News time: 2026-08-21T02:36:17.709952+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new blog post reignites a painful contrast in digital justice: Aaron Swartz, the 26-year-old co-creator of RSS and open-access advocate, faced federal prosecution—and ultimately died by suicide under immense legal pressure—for downloading nearly 5 million academic articles from JSTOR via MIT’s network. Meanwhile, Meta routinely scrapes vast swaths of the public web—including news sites, blogs, and forums—to train its AI models, with no criminal charges, minimal regulatory scrutiny, and little public accountability.

The disparity isn’t just about scale or intent—it’s about power. Swartz acted alone, without corporate backing, and targeted paywalled scholarly knowledge he believed should be free. His actions involved bypassing IP-based access controls and rotating MAC addresses after being blocked—technically distinct from simple HTTP requests to publicly accessible pages. Yet prosecutors charged him under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) with 13 felony counts, threatening up to 35 years in prison (though sentencing guidelines would have likely capped it far lower). JSTOR itself declined to pursue civil action and later made its archive freely available to libraries; MIT refused to support a plea deal that would have avoided jail time.

By contrast, Meta’s Llama training data includes content scraped from millions of domains without explicit consent, often ignoring robots.txt directives and copyright notices. Courts have recently upheld that scraping publicly available websites is generally lawful in the U.S.—but only when done by entities with legal resources, political influence, and economic weight. As one commenter notes, prosecuting Meta at scale could chill AI investment, a priority the U.S. government actively protects. That asymmetry reveals how law enforcement discretion, institutional alignment, and corporate immunity shape what ‘illegal’ even means online.

This isn’t nostalgia for Swartz as a martyr—it’s a sober reckoning with how legal tools like the CFAA are selectively weaponized. The same conduct, reframed as ‘AI training data acquisition,’ becomes not just permissible but celebrated. Until laws, enforcement priorities, and platform accountability mechanisms evolve in tandem, the web remains governed less by neutral rules than by who holds the leverage.
