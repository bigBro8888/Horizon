Biology education kills wonder — and how to bring it back

News time: 2026-08-21T02:36:17.709982+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In his 2020 essay 'I Should Have Loved Biology', programmer and writer James Somers reflects on a profound personal regret: not falling in love with biology earlier — not because the subject lacked beauty or depth, but because his formal education reduced it to memorization, taxonomy, and disconnected facts. He recalls staring at textbook diagrams of mitochondria and chloroplasts, sensing their elegance but never being invited to ask how or why they evolved that way — or what it means that life builds itself from scratch, every second, inside every cell.

Somers argues that biology is uniquely positioned among the sciences to inspire awe: it’s tangible (we are biology), historically rich (Darwin’s notebooks read like detective fiction), and increasingly computational (genomes are digital, cells run code). Yet standard curricula often strip away narrative, context, and agency — turning students into passive recipients of facts rather than active explorers. He contrasts this with how he learned programming: by breaking things, building small tools, and seeing immediate feedback — a process rooted in discovery, not recitation.

The essay resonates beyond nostalgia. It aligns with growing critiques of STEM pedagogy — especially in light of rising interest in computational biology, where DNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and AI-driven protein folding demand interdisciplinary fluency. As one commenter notes, shifting from software engineering to cancer epigenetics felt like unlocking a new universe — not because the data was prettier, but because the questions were deeper and more human. Yet that pivot required self-directed learning; formal biology training rarely prepared him for it.

Somers doesn’t propose a syllabus — he proposes a mindset shift. What if high school biology began not with the Linnaean classification system, but with CRISPR-edited zebrafish embryos? What if students simulated natural selection using simple Python scripts — watching virtual populations adapt in real time? The goal isn’t to replace rigor with entertainment, but to restore the question as the engine of learning. As another commenter — a working biologist — puts it: even after decades, diving into a new molecular pathway still evokes disbelief: How the hell can this even work? And yet it does — perfectly, relentlessly, beautifully.
