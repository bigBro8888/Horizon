New entropy-based method estimates true intrinsic rank in tabular data

News time: 2026-08-21T02:36:17.215466+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher has introduced the Entropic Scree—a non-parametric, model-agnostic framework that redefines how we estimate intrinsic dimensionality in complex tabular datasets. Unlike PCA or kernel-based methods, which rely on linear covariance or Euclidean geometry, the Entropic Scree operates entirely in information space: it uses Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) and a variant of the Jaccard similarity based on Variation of Information to quantify pairwise dependencies between variables—regardless of data type, scale, or distribution. This makes it uniquely suited for real-world tabular data, where mixed feature types (binary, categorical, continuous), non-linear interactions, and high feature-to-sample ratios (m > N) routinely break conventional estimators.

The core insight is that standard PCA doesn’t just underestimate—it overestimates rank by splitting non-linear relationships (e.g., X₁ × X₂) into artificial orthogonal dimensions. Kernel PCA fails too: its infinite-dimensional Hilbert space lacks finite-sample stability, causing structural collapse when generative factors are entangled or sparse. Meanwhile, topological estimators like TWO-NN suffer from distance concentration in high-dimensional, asymmetric settings—rendering local neighborhoods meaningless. The Entropic Scree bypasses all these pitfalls by measuring shared probability mass instead of variance or distance.

At its heart, the method constructs an ‘entropic scree plot’—a curve mapping how much mutual information is retained as progressively more variable pairs are aggregated. It identifies the intrinsic generative rank not as a hard cutoff, but as the point where added redundancy plateaus. Crucially, it also quantifies ‘informational gravity’: a stability metric reflecting how consistently each latent root appears across resampled subsets—helping users distinguish robust signals from fragile artifacts. Further, it computes the ratio of shared signal to idiosyncratic noise, and reveals decoupled variable sub-networks—making it both a diagnostic tool and an exploratory map.

The open-source implementation (v1.0.0, released July 2026) includes utilities for variable clustering, bottleneck sizing for autoencoders, and integration with common Python data stacks. While still a preprint—not yet peer-reviewed—the approach directly addresses long-standing pain points in unsupervised learning, especially for domains like healthcare analytics, financial risk modeling, and industrial IoT, where tabular data dominates and interpretability matters. As one practitioner noted in early testing: ‘It finally gave me confidence that the 7 components I kept extracting weren’t just noise masquerading as structure.’
