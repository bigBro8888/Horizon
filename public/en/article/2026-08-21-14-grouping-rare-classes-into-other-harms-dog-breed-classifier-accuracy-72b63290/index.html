Grouping rare classes into 'Other' harms dog breed classifier accuracy

News time: 2026-08-21T02:36:17.214878+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning practitioner recently raised a subtle but consequential question on Reddit: what happens when you collapse dozens of rare dog breeds—each with only a handful of training images—into a single catch-all 'Other breed' class? The motivation is practical: real-world image datasets often follow a long-tail distribution, where a few popular classes (e.g., Golden Retriever, German Shepherd) dominate, while hundreds of others suffer from severe data scarcity. Rather than discard those rare breeds entirely, many practitioners default to grouping them under one umbrella label—a strategy that seems pragmatic but may introduce hidden modeling costs.

The core concern is semantic incoherence. A Chihuahua and a Great Dane look nothing alike—not just in pixel space, but in learned feature representations. Forcing a model to treat both as members of the same class means it must learn decision boundaries that accommodate wildly divergent visual patterns within a single label. This can distort latent space geometry, degrade calibration (e.g., overconfident predictions for 'Other'), and weaken generalization—especially for downstream tasks like fairness-aware deployment or open-set recognition.

Several commenters noted that this isn’t merely a statistical issue—it’s a label design problem. Unlike binary classification, multiclass problems assume mutually exclusive, semantically meaningful categories. Merging dissimilar classes violates that assumption and turns classification into an ill-posed task. One suggestion was to reframe the problem entirely: instead of training an 'Other' class, treat rare breeds as out-of-distribution (OOD) samples and build a dedicated OOD detector alongside the main classifier. Another proposed using semi-supervised or few-shot techniques—like prototypical networks or prompt-based fine-tuning—for low-resource breeds, rather than discarding or conflating them.

Ultimately, the thread highlights a quiet tension in applied ML: the gap between dataset convenience and modeling integrity. There’s no universal threshold (e.g., ‘N = 10 samples’) that makes grouping safe—impact depends on intra-class variance, feature separability, and task requirements. As imbalanced learning remains a persistent challenge in vision and NLP, thoughtful label engineering matters just as much as algorithm choice.
