Scientists unveil largest 2D map of the universe to date

News time: 2026-08-22T02:20:08.619267+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Astronomers have released the largest two-dimensional map of the universe ever assembled — a staggering mosaic built from over 263,000 telescope exposures across visible and near-infrared light. Covering roughly three-quarters of the sky (not one-third, as earlier reports misstated), the map contains nearly 4 billion celestial objects: stars, galaxies, quasars, asteroids, and even distant black holes. It is the product of the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys — a collaborative effort using data from three major surveys: the Mayall z-band Legacy Survey (MzLS), the Dark Energy Camera Legacy Survey (DECaLS), and the Beijing-Arizona Sky Survey (BASS). The final image spans 5.6 trillion pixels, making it not only the most extensive but also among the highest-resolution wide-field astronomical maps publicly available.

Unlike traditional star charts, this map isn’t hand-drawn or schematic — it’s a direct, calibrated photometric representation of real sky data. Every pixel corresponds to actual light collected by ground-based telescopes, primarily the 4-meter Mayall Telescope in Arizona and the 4-meter Blanco Telescope in Chile. The team processed petabytes of raw imaging data using advanced pipelines that correct for atmospheric distortion, instrument noise, and cosmic rays. Crucially, the map serves as the foundational reference for the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) project, which is now building the largest 3D map of the universe by measuring redshifts of tens of millions of galaxies.

The map is fully interactive and freely accessible via the Legacy Survey Sky Viewer — a web-based tool that lets users pan, zoom, overlay catalogs, and cross-match with other datasets like WISE and SDSS. Researchers can download full images and object catalogs from NERSC or query them through NOIRLab’s Astro Data Lab. While it’s labeled a ‘2D map’, astronomers emphasize this is a projection: each object’s position on the sky (right ascension and declination) is precise, but distance information is absent — that requires spectroscopic follow-up. As one commenter noted, it’s less a flat wall and more a ‘fisheye snapshot’ of a curved, expanding 4D spacetime.

What makes this milestone especially valuable is its role as infrastructure — not just a pretty picture, but a shared coordinate system for future discovery. From identifying rare high-redshift galaxies to calibrating machine-learning models for transient detection, the map lowers the barrier for both professional and citizen scientists. Its public release underscores a broader shift toward open, reproducible astronomy — where data, tools, and visualizations are treated as communal resources rather than proprietary outputs.
