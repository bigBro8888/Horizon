US citizen charged with felony for deleting phone data at border

News time: 2026-08-22T02:20:08.619337+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Samuel Tunick, a U.S. citizen returning from Canada in early 2026, faced an unexpected legal consequence after deleting personal data from his smartphone during a routine U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) inspection. According to court documents cited by The New York Times, CBP agents observed Tunick rapidly wiping files—including photos and messages—while his device was temporarily in their custody. Though he was allowed to re-enter the country, federal prosecutors later filed felony charges under 18 U.S.C. § 1519, a post-Enron statute criminalizing the destruction of records to impede federal investigations.

This case marks the first known prosecution of a U.S. citizen under this statute for on-the-spot digital deletion during a border search. It sits at the volatile intersection of two long-standing legal doctrines: the border search exception—which permits warrantless, suspicionless searches of persons and property entering the U.S.—and the Fourth Amendment’s protection against unreasonable seizures. While courts have repeatedly upheld CBP’s authority to inspect phones, they have not ruled on whether actively erasing data during such a search constitutes obstruction, especially when no formal investigation or seizure order has yet been issued.

Technically, the incident highlights how quickly forensic workflows at ports of entry have evolved. As reported by Medium and UltraLocked, CBP routinely conducts ‘Level Two’ forensic extractions using tools like Cellebrite and GrayKey—capable of recovering recently deleted files, accessing encrypted partitions, and even bypassing passcodes in some cases. Yet Tunick’s action raises a practical paradox: if deletion is reversible, does it meaningfully obstruct? And if agents can recover the data anyway, why charge the traveler—not the agency—for attempting to assert control over their own device?

The broader implications extend beyond one defendant. Privacy engineers and civil liberties advocates warn that prosecuting self-defense measures—like decoy partitions, automated wipe triggers, or bootable privacy OSes—could chill legitimate digital hygiene. As Hacker News commenters noted, solutions like Tasker-based factory resets or flash-drive-booted Tails-like environments are not theoretical; they’re increasingly viable countermeasures. But if deleting data becomes legally perilous, travelers may be forced into binary choices: surrender full access or risk felony conviction. That pressure reshapes the very meaning of consent—and security—in America’s most surveilled public spaces.
