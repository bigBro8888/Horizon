Researcher accidentally logged hundreds of thousands of military base calls via ENUM

News time: 2026-08-22T02:20:08.619311+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a striking demonstration of how forgotten internet infrastructure can quietly persist—and leak sensitive data—a security researcher named Lina recently discovered she had unintentionally logged over 200,000 phone calls to U.S. military bases. The discovery came not from hacking or scanning, but from routine DNS queries to e164.arpa, the official ENUM (Electronic Number Mapping) domain designed to map E.164 telephone numbers into DNS records. While ENUM was standardized in the early 2000s to bridge telephony and IP networks, it never gained mainstream adoption. Yet as Lina’s blog post reveals, large portions of the system remain fully operational—especially for high-assurance entities like the Department of Defense.

The mechanism is deceptively simple: any E.164 number (e.g., +1-555-123-4567) can be transformed into a DNS name by reversing its digits, inserting dots between each digit, and appending ‘e164.arpa’. A query for ‘7.6.5.4.3.2.1.5.5.5.1.e164.arpa’ may return NAPTR records pointing to SIP URIs, email addresses, or other contact methods. Lina wasn’t targeting military numbers—she was testing bulk ENUM resolution at scale—and found that many DoD numbers resolved successfully, often with full caller ID, location, and routing metadata.

What makes this especially notable is not just the volume, but the invisibility of the exposure. These records aren’t indexed, advertised, or documented in public APIs; they live in DNS zones managed by national registries and legacy telecom operators. Some responses even included internal extensions and VoIP endpoints tied directly to secure facilities. Crucially, no authentication or rate limiting was enforced—making enumeration trivial and scalable.

The incident underscores a broader tension in internet governance: protocols deemed obsolete on paper often linger in production for decades, maintained only by inertia or niche compliance requirements. ENUM’s continued operation for defense communications suggests it serves a quiet, unacknowledged role—perhaps for interoperability with legacy systems or regulatory reporting. But without active security review, such infrastructure becomes an accidental surveillance vector. As one commenter observed, the real story isn’t that ENUM is dead—it’s that it’s unseen, unmonitored, and still answering queries.
