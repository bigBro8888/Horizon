Kagi adds toggle to hide paywalled links from search results

News time: 2026-08-22T02:20:08.619289+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Kagi, the ad-free, privacy-focused search engine based in Palo Alto, has rolled out a new user-controlled setting that lets subscribers exclude paywalled websites—such as those behind subscription barriers or metered access—from their search results. The feature, confirmed in Kagi’s official changelog on May 29, 2024, gives users immediate control over result accessibility without requiring workarounds like browser extensions or cache tricks. It reflects a growing demand for frictionless, verifiable research—especially among academics, journalists, and developers who rely on quick, open access to primary sources.

Unlike mainstream search engines that surface content regardless of access restrictions, Kagi’s approach aligns with its core philosophy: prioritizing quality, transparency, and user agency. The setting is opt-in and appears in the search preferences panel, allowing users to enable or disable it with one click. Importantly, it doesn’t alter how Kagi indexes the web—it filters results after retrieval, meaning the underlying index remains unchanged. This preserves Kagi’s hybrid architecture: a curated, high-quality index supplemented by trusted external sources.

The timing matters. As more publishers tighten paywalls—and as AI assistants increasingly summarize or hallucinate from inaccessible content—the ability to see only freely readable material becomes a practical advantage. Users report using this filter to avoid dead ends during technical troubleshooting, academic literature reviews, or news verification. One long-time subscriber noted how useful it is now that Reddit blocks unauthenticated access to older posts—a problem Kagi’s filtering helps sidestep.

Still, the feature sparks nuanced debate. Some users praise its simplicity and alignment with Kagi’s values; others question whether excluding paywalled content risks underrepresenting high-quality journalism, especially outlets funded by subscriptions (e.g., The New York Times or BBC). Yet most agree the choice belongs to the user—not the algorithm. In an era where search is increasingly mediated by LLMs trained on opaque data, Kagi’s move reaffirms human-centered design: giving people tools to shape what they see, not just what’s profitable to show.
