Coding agents succeed through intent and verification, not line-by-line review

News time: 2026-08-23T02:36:19.354818+00:00 · Source: rss

Simon Willison’s August 2026 post challenges a widespread assumption in AI-assisted development: that effective use of coding agents depends on meticulously reviewing every line of generated code. Instead, he argues the real skill lies in clearly articulating what needs to change — the intent — and then applying confident, higher-level validation strategies to confirm correctness. This shift reframes the human role from gatekeeper of syntax to orchestrator of outcomes.

Willison points out that exhaustive line-by-line scrutiny has never been the gold standard for software validation — even before AI. Unit tests, integration checks, behavioral assertions, and manual end-to-end verification have long served as more reliable indicators of correctness than visual scanning. With coding agents, those same techniques become even more valuable: a well-designed test suite or a precise prompt specifying expected inputs and outputs can verify intent far more efficiently than reading hundreds of lines of generated code.

This perspective aligns with the emerging discipline of agentic engineering, where humans set goals, define constraints, and interpret results — while agents handle planning, implementation, and iteration. It reflects a maturing understanding of human-AI collaboration: trust is earned not through surveillance, but through clear interfaces, observable behavior, and repeatable validation. As AWS Prescriptive Guidance notes, coding agents are designed to interpret goals and analyze context — meaning their output should be judged against those goals, not against arbitrary stylistic or syntactic expectations.

The implication is practical and empowering. Developers don’t need to become LLM interpreters or reverse-engineer agent reasoning. They do need to sharpen skills in specification writing, test design, and outcome-oriented review. In short: spend less time reading code, more time defining what ‘done’ looks like — and how you’ll know it when you see it.
