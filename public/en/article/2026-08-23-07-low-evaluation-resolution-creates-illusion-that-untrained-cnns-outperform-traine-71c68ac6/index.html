Low evaluation resolution creates illusion that untrained CNNs outperform trained ones at V1

News time: 2026-08-23T02:36:20.195554+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new preprint challenges a widely cited finding in computational neuroscience: that untrained convolutional neural networks (CNNs) can match or even surpass backpropagation-trained models in predicting neural responses in the primary visual cortex (V1). The study shows this apparent superiority is not due to intrinsic brain-likeness, but rather an artifact of using low-resolution images—specifically 32×32 pixels—during model evaluation. When the same models were tested across six image resolutions (from 32px to 224px), the gap between untrained and backprop-trained CNNs shrank dramatically and even reversed: at 32px, untrained models slightly outperformed trained ones (−0.001), but at 224px, trained models pulled ahead (+0.044).

The researchers used a controlled experimental setup: a small CNN trained on a CIFAR-10 subset, five distinct learning rules (random initialization, backpropagation, feedback alignment, predictive coding, and STDP), fixed weights and normalization, and evaluation on the THINGS-fMRI dataset—a large-scale, multimodal fMRI benchmark with thousands of object images. Crucially, they ruled out several alternative explanations: mismatched train/eval resolution wasn’t the culprit (since off-the-shelf ResNet-50 and Swin-Tiny also peaked at low resolution); batch-norm calibration issues were addressed; and low-level pixel or Gabor-like structure alone couldn’t explain the effect. A content-vs-pooling control further confirmed that image content—not just spatial pooling—drove the resolution dependence.

One robust finding survived all resolutions: backprop-trained models consistently outperformed untrained ones in the lateral occipital complex (LOC), a higher visual area. This suggests learning does shape neural representational geometry—but not where prior work focused. The paper also uncovered and corrected a batch-norm evaluation mode bug present in three earlier preprints, highlighting how subtle implementation details can skew neuro-AI comparisons.

This work reframes how we benchmark AI models against brain data. It warns against drawing strong conclusions from single-resolution evaluations—especially when comparing untrained versus trained architectures—and calls for standardized, multi-resolution protocols in neural encoding studies. The open code and preprint invite replication and extension, making it a timely methodological contribution to both machine learning and systems neuroscience.
