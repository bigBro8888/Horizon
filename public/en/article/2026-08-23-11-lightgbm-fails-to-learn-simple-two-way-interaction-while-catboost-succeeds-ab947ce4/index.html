LightGBM fails to learn simple two-way interaction while CatBoost succeeds

News time: 2026-08-23T02:36:20.195399+00:00 · Source: reddit

A Reddit user posted a revealing toy experiment showing that LightGBM—despite its speed and popularity—fails to fit a minimal synthetic dataset where the target depends solely on a two-way interaction between binary features A and B. The dataset contains just eight samples: for each combination of A ∈ {0,1} and B ∈ {0,1}, there are two identical rows with y = 0 or 1 such that the marginal means of y across A or B alone are both exactly 0.5. This makes the interaction AB (encoded as a distinct integer ID per combination) the only predictive signal.

When trained only on A and B, LightGBM predicts a constant 0.5 everywhere—correctly reflecting the lack of main effects—but cannot recover the true pattern (y = 1 iff A == B). Even when given the precomputed interaction variable AB as a single numeric feature, LightGBM still outputs near-zero predictions, failing to split cleanly at AB = 2 or AB = 3. Surprisingly, CatBoost fits the same A–B data perfectly without any explicit interaction term—producing [0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0], which matches the ground truth.

The root cause lies in fundamental algorithmic differences. LightGBM uses histogram-based splitting and leaf-wise tree growth: it approximates continuous feature distributions by binning values, then greedily expands the leaf with highest loss reduction. In this tiny dataset, coarse binning (e.g., default 255 bins over just 4 unique AB values) and early stopping heuristics can prevent fine-grained splits needed to separate AB=3 from others. CatBoost, by contrast, employs ordered boosting and handles categorical-like patterns more natively—even with numeric AB, its oblivious (symmetric) trees and built-in interaction-awareness during split evaluation let it discover the exact partition.

This isn’t a bug but a consequence of design trade-offs: LightGBM prioritizes scalability and speed on large tabular data, sacrificing precision on small, structured problems. CatBoost trades some raw speed for stronger bias toward capturing interactions out-of-the-box—especially useful in low-data or interpretability-critical settings. For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: algorithm choice matters beyond accuracy metrics; understanding how a model searches the hypothesis space is essential for debugging, feature engineering, and trust.
