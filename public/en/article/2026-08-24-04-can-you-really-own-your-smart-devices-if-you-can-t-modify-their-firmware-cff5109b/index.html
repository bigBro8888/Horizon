Can you really own your smart devices if you can’t modify their firmware

News time: 2026-08-24T02:36:12.388984+00:00 · Source: hackernews

What does it mean to own a piece of hardware in 2025? Not just legally — but technically, practically, and ethically? A recent reflective essay titled Everything I Own, Owned tackles this question head-on by documenting one developer’s hands-on attempts to reverse engineer and modify firmware on everyday devices: an ASUS ROG Swift PG42UQ OLED monitor, a Wi-Fi smart outlet, and other embedded systems. The author didn’t set out to build something new — instead, they wanted to remove an intrusive pixel-cleaning pop-up, turn a plug into a WiFi-controlled lava lamp, or simply understand what code was running beneath the surface. Each attempt revealed how deeply manufacturers lock down even basic functionality — not through encryption alone, but via signed bootloaders, undocumented protocols, missing debug interfaces, and the ever-present risk of bricking.

The post quickly became a focal point for a nuanced technical conversation. Commenters shared real-world successes — like using AI-assisted tooling to flash custom firmware onto a Wi-Fi relay in under 20 minutes — and sobering failures, such as accidentally disabling a $1,500 OLED monitor or permanently bricking a router while experimenting with TFTP boot paths. One contributor reverse-engineered the Supernote file format in hours using prompt-engineered agents and sample files — a task previously deemed too niche and labor-intensive for manual effort. These stories highlight a growing tension: the tools for firmware modification are becoming more accessible, yet the stakes remain high due to fragile update mechanisms and scarce documentation.

Underlying all this is the principle of firmware freedom: the idea that true ownership includes the right to inspect, modify, and replace the software baked into hardware. This isn’t just about convenience or customization — it’s tied to security transparency, longevity, and repairability. When vendors push opaque, non-upgradable firmware, users lose control over device behavior, privacy, and lifespan. And unlike desktop software, firmware bugs or backdoors can persist across reboots, survive OS reinstalls, and evade traditional scanning tools.

Still, the discussion avoids dogma. Contributors acknowledge trade-offs: safety-critical devices (like medical hardware) demand stricter controls; hobbyist tinkering carries real risk; and not every user wants — or should need — to become a firmware engineer. What emerges is a call not for universal root access, but for design transparency, standardized debugging interfaces, and vendor-supported modding pathways — especially for consumer electronics marketed as 'yours'.
