How staff engineers systematically find high-impact problems

News time: 2026-08-24T02:36:12.389026+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Staff engineers don’t wait for problems to land on their desks—they actively seek them out. In a thoughtful new essay, Lalit M., an infrastructure and developer tools engineer at a large tech company, lays out a repeatable, observation-driven method for identifying which problems are truly worth solving. Rather than relying on top-down mandates or abstract strategy sessions, he starts by watching how engineers actually work: where they pause, complain, rerun commands, or build one-off scripts. These friction points—especially recurring ones across teams—are early signals of systemic inefficiency.

He then deepens the inquiry through structured stakeholder engagement: not just asking ‘What’s broken?’, but listening for patterns in how people describe their workflows, bottlenecks, and workarounds. He treats engineering teams like customers—mapping pain points to underlying system constraints, tooling gaps, or misaligned incentives. Crucially, he filters findings through a strategic lens: Does this problem affect multiple teams? Does it compound over time? Can solving it unlock future velocity—or prevent future toil?

The approach assumes a bottom-up engineering culture, where engineers have autonomy to shape roadmaps and invest in foundational work. That context matters: as one commenter notes, this method may not translate directly to highly centralized or execution-focused organizations. Still, the core discipline—observing behavior, validating with users, and prioritizing by leverage—is portable. The essay avoids prescriptive checklists; instead, it models reflective practice, showing how experienced engineers calibrate judgment over years of shipping, observing trade-offs, and learning what ‘high impact’ really means in their specific environment.

Ultimately, the piece reframes problem-finding not as a skill to be mastered overnight, but as a habit cultivated through curiosity, empathy, and rigor. For senior engineers navigating ambiguity—especially in infrastructure or platform roles—it offers both practical scaffolding and quiet reassurance: the work isn’t about having all the answers, but about asking better questions—and knowing where to look for the answers that matter most.
