Sal Khan’s video-first teaching misses the core of learning by making

News time: 2026-08-24T02:36:12.389246+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new essay by Punya Mishra challenges a foundational assumption in educational technology: that delivering clear, well-structured content — like Sal Khan’s signature instructional videos — automatically leads to deep learning. Drawing on decades of learning science, the author argues that real understanding emerges not from watching or listening, but from doing: solving novel problems, building models, debugging code, writing explanations, and revising ideas through feedback. This ‘learning by making’ aligns with constructivist theory, which holds that knowledge is actively built by learners, not passively absorbed.

The critique targets not Khan Academy’s intentions or accessibility — it remains free, widely used, and pedagogically valuable for many — but its dominant design pattern. Most Khan lessons begin with a video lecture followed by algorithmic practice. While helpful for procedural fluency or initial exposure, this sequence often skips the messy, generative work of sense-making: asking questions, testing hypotheses, confronting contradictions, and integrating new ideas into existing mental frameworks. As one commenter notes, early Khan videos served as useful scaffolding — but scaffolding must eventually be removed so learners can stand on their own.

This isn’t a call to abandon explanation altogether. Rather, it’s a plea to rebalance edtech priorities: shift from optimizing delivery (video length, voice tone, adaptive pacing) toward designing environments where students use knowledge to create, argue, simulate, or teach others. University STEM courses already do this — via labs, problem sets, peer review, and design projects — yet much K–12 edtech still treats cognition as information transfer.

The Hacker News discussion reflects this tension thoughtfully. Educators affirm that ‘we mostly learn by doing’, while former Khan learners acknowledge its value as an entry point — provided it’s not mistaken for the full learning process. Others raise pragmatic concerns: scalability, teacher training, assessment complexity. Still, the consensus leans clear: no amount of polished telling replaces the cognitive work of active construction.
