How to cite your own preprint in a camera-ready conference paper

News time: 2026-08-24T02:36:12.545308+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning researcher recently posted on Reddit’s r/MachineLearning asking how to handle citations in the camera-ready version of a conference paper that originated as a preprint — especially when subsequent works have already built upon it. This is not an edge case: in fast-moving fields like ML, it’s common for papers to appear first on arXiv, get cited by others within weeks, and then be accepted to conferences months later. The researcher worries that citing their own preprint in the final version might look self-referential or redundant, yet omitting it risks misrepresenting the timeline of ideas and undermining proper attribution.

The core tension lies in scholarly norms versus practical reality. Conference camera-ready versions are the final, non-editable PDFs submitted after peer review — they must reflect accurate provenance while preserving perceived novelty. Unlike journal articles, which often explicitly allow citation of prior preprints (with clear disclaimers), many computer science conferences don’t publish formal guidance on this. As a result, authors navigate unspoken conventions: some replace preprint citations with the upcoming conference version once accepted; others retain the preprint but add a note like “This work first appeared as a preprint in [Month Year]” — a practice endorsed by Springer Nature and COPE for transparency.

Crucially, the issue isn’t just about self-citation etiquette — it’s about intellectual lineage. If follow-up papers cite the original preprint, omitting it from the camera-ready version breaks the citation chain and may inadvertently suggest those works appeared before the foundational idea. That could confuse readers, reviewers, or future meta-analyses. At the same time, overemphasizing the preprint risks diluting the contribution claimed in the conference version — especially if the camera-ready includes significant new results or analysis beyond the preprint.

Best practice, supported by publication ethics guidelines, is to cite the preprint and clarify its relationship to the current work. For example: “An earlier version of this work appeared as a preprint [arXiv:XXXX.XXXXX] and has since inspired several extensions [Citation A, Citation B].” This honors priority, acknowledges influence, and maintains scholarly integrity — without compromising novelty. Ultimately, clarity and consistency matter more than avoiding self-citation altogether.
