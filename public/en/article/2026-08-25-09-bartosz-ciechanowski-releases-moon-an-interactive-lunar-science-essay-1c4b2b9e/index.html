Bartosz Ciechanowski releases Moon: an interactive lunar science essay

News time: 2026-08-25T02:22:44.911604+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Bartosz Ciechanowski’s latest work, Moon (2024), is not just another explainer—it’s a meticulously crafted, browser-native science experience. Hosted at ciechanow.ski/moon/, the essay uses real-time physics simulations, draggable celestial bodies, and layered visual explanations to demystify how the Moon formed, orbits Earth, and displays its phases. Readers don’t just read about tidal locking or orbital resonance—they feel them by adjusting sliders, rotating virtual viewpoints, and watching gravitational forces update live. Every diagram responds; every animation teaches. This isn’t passive consumption—it’s guided discovery built into the web itself.

Ciechanowski has long been a pioneer in technical storytelling—his earlier essays on optics, relativity, and planetary motion set new standards for clarity and interactivity. Moon extends that legacy with even tighter integration of narrative, math, and interface. The page loads entirely client-side, requires no sign-in or tracking, and runs smoothly on laptops and tablets alike. Its design avoids decorative flourishes: every UI element serves pedagogy—labels appear only when relevant, controls are minimal but precise, and transitions are purposeful, not performative.

What makes Moon especially timely is its quiet embrace of AI-assisted development—not as a gimmick, but as a pragmatic tool. While Ciechanowski writes all prose and designs all interactions himself, he’s spoken openly about using LLMs to prototype JavaScript snippets, generate placeholder SVG paths, or draft explanatory phrasing during early iteration. This reflects a broader shift: AI is becoming part of the technical writer’s toolkit, like linters or version control—not replacing authorship, but accelerating refinement.

The response from educators, developers, and scientists has been enthusiastic. Commenters praise how the virtual planet view clarifies lunar libration, while others reflect on deeper questions: Is imitating Ciechanowski’s signature style for personal learning ethically sound? Should such essays include a table of contents—or does linear, scroll-driven progression better serve deep focus? These aren’t nitpicks; they’re signs of a maturing genre where form, function, and philosophy are now debated with equal rigor.
