Should MARL architectures share hyperparameters for fair comparison

News time: 2026-08-25T02:22:45.061404+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher training multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) models on the VMAS simulator has raised a subtle but consequential methodological question: when comparing different MARL architectures—such as Independent PPO and Graph-based PPO (e.g., HetGPPO)—is it scientifically sound to use architecture-specific optimal hyperparameters, or must all models be evaluated using identical hyperparameters to ensure fairness?

The question arises from practical experience: while tuning hyperparameters like learning rate, entropy coefficient, and batch size separately for each architecture often yields stable, convergent training, forcing them to share a single hyperparameter set sometimes causes catastrophic non-convergence—especially in complex, heterogeneous robot coordination tasks. Yet the researcher’s end goal isn’t just performance on clean tasks; it’s evaluating adversarial robustness of frozen, pre-trained policies under test-time perturbations.

This distinction matters deeply. In single-agent RL, hyperparameter unification is often treated as a baseline fairness requirement—but MARL introduces compounding challenges: agent interdependence, non-stationarity, credit assignment complexity, and graph-structured information flow. A hyperparameter that stabilizes Independent PPO (where agents learn in isolation) may destabilize Graph PPO (which shares gradients across a learned communication topology). As shown in Bettini et al.’s HetGPPO work, architectural differences aren’t just implementation details—they reflect fundamentally distinct assumptions about agent interaction.

The broader implication touches on reproducibility standards in empirical MARL research. If robustness evaluation depends on models that barely converge—or fail entirely—under shared hyperparameters, then strict unification may sacrifice validity for superficial comparability. Conversely, allowing per-architecture tuning risks conflating architectural merit with tuning effort, especially when robustness is measured post-training on frozen models. The tension reflects an ongoing debate in top ML venues: whether benchmarking should prioritize achievable best practice or controlled experimental equivalence—particularly when assessing properties like adversarial resilience that are sensitive to training dynamics.

Ultimately, this isn’t just about PPO variants or VMAS tasks. It’s about how the field defines rigor in comparative MARL evaluation—and whether fairness means equal conditions, or equal opportunity to succeed.
