Xiaomi Xring O3 CPU beats Apple in multi-core benchmarks but not real-world use

News time: 2026-08-25T02:22:44.911555+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Xiaomi has unveiled its new Xring O3 system-on-chip, claiming it is the fastest smartphone processor to date—scoring over 5.2 million points on AnTuTu and achieving a Geekbench single-core score of 3,945. That figure sits between Apple’s M5 iPad (3,556) and M5 Max (4,300), while its multi-core result of 15,221 narrowly trails the M5 Max’s 29,200 but surpasses the standard M5’s 15,285. The chip’s headline-grabbing performance stems from an all-big-core CPU design: two ARM C1-Ultra cores at up to 4.35 GHz, four C1-Premium cores at 3.68 GHz, and four C1-Pro cores at 3.15 GHz—all fabricated on TSMC’s 3nm process and paired with first-in-industry LPDDR6 memory support.

But context matters. As community commentators quickly pointed out, the Xring O3 does not feature custom CPU cores designed by Xiaomi. Instead, it uses ARM’s licensable C1-Ultra core—the same one found in MediaTek’s Dimensity 9500. This means Xiaomi’s role was integration and configuration—not microarchitecture innovation. The chip also lacks efficiency cores entirely, trading battery life and thermal headroom for peak synthetic scores. In practice, as one user noted, the Dimensity 9500’s C1-Ultra implementation dropped from over 4,000 in controlled lab tests to around 3,300 under smartphone-level power and cooling constraints.

The broader implication isn’t about dethroning Apple, but reshaping the mobile chip hierarchy. Xiaomi—the world’s third-largest smartphone vendor—is now vertically integrating at the SoC level, joining Samsung and Apple as companies capable of shipping chips built around ARM’s highest-tier licensed cores. While it doesn’t yet rival Apple’s full-stack silicon control, Xiaomi’s move signals growing leverage against traditional suppliers like Qualcomm and MediaTek—especially as it gains scale, manufacturing access, and IP ownership in adjacent areas like NPUs and memory controllers.

Still, raw benchmark numbers alone mislead. As one engineer observed, many server CPUs easily outperform Apple chips in multi-core Geekbench—but none are viable in phones due to power density and thermal throttling. The Xring O3’s 10-core all-big layout may deliver impressive numbers on paper, but without sustained clock stability, efficient voltage scaling, or robust thermal management in slim devices, those gains won’t translate to everyday responsiveness or longevity. For consumers, the real test will be how well the chip balances speed, battery life, and heat across real apps—not just synthetic suites.
