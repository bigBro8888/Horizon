X Corp sends cease-and-desist to Nitter and XCancel

News time: 2026-08-26T02:38:28.019093+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In early 2024, X Corp. — formerly Twitter — issued cease-and-desist letters to two widely used open-source alternatives: Nitter, a lightweight, privacy-respecting front-end for browsing X without JavaScript or login; and XCancel, a tool that strips X’s tracking scripts and ads while preserving read-only access. The notices triggered immediate shutdowns across most public Nitter instances, with the project’s maintainer confirming on GitHub that all instances would remain offline pending legal counsel. XCancel’s website also went offline shortly after, though its source code remains publicly available on GitHub.

These tools were never intended to replace X’s core functionality — they don’t support posting, DMs, or authentication — but instead offered a faster, ad-free, and tracker-free way to read public posts. For many users, especially those in government, journalism, and local communities, Nitter was the de facto way to monitor official announcements without creating an account or consenting to surveillance. As one commenter noted, their local council still relies exclusively on X for public updates — now inaccessible without logging in.

The legal basis cited by X appears to center on unauthorized scraping of its API and alleged violations of its Terms of Service, particularly Section 6.1 (prohibiting automated access) and Section 8.2 (banning derivative services). Notably, X has not pursued similar action against Mastodon or Bluesky — federated platforms that implement full interoperability via open protocols like ActivityPub and AT Protocol. Instead, the enforcement targets lean, read-only proxies built on reverse-engineered web interfaces — tools that operate entirely client-side or via public HTML parsing.

This escalation reflects a broader tightening of platform control under Elon Musk’s leadership: since 2023, X has repeatedly restricted API access, revoked developer keys, and removed legacy endpoints — moves widely interpreted as efforts to eliminate third-party clients and force users into its official app. Critics argue that such actions undermine the open web’s foundational principle: that public content should remain publicly accessible without gatekeeping. As developers weigh legal risks, many are now migrating toward decentralized alternatives — not because they’re technically superior, but because they’re legally sustainable.
