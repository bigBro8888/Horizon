Researcher seeks best AI approach for medicine reminders under uncertainty

News time: 2026-08-26T02:38:28.159567+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning researcher has posted a high-signal question on Reddit’s r/MachineLearning, asking how to formally model a medicine-reminder agent that must make time-sensitive, safety-aware decisions—'remind', 'wait', or 'notify a caregiver'—despite incomplete knowledge about the patient’s current state. The core challenge is partial observability: the system rarely knows for sure whether a dose was already taken, whether the patient is cognitively present or physically nearby, or whether environmental barriers (e.g., noise, device out of reach) prevent timely response.

The poster initially leans toward framing the problem as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), a rigorous framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. But they’re rightly cautious—POMDPs are computationally demanding, require precise modeling of transition and observation probabilities, and often rely on assumptions (e.g., stationary dynamics, known reward structure) that rarely hold in real-world healthcare settings. Instead, they’re exploring pragmatic alternatives: contextual bandits that adapt reminders using real-time features like time of day, recent activity sensor data, or historical adherence patterns; simplified MDPs with hand-crafted belief states; or hybrid rule-based systems with uncertainty thresholds (e.g., 'if no confirmation within 5 minutes and heart rate is elevated, escalate').

What makes this question especially valuable is its grounding in deployment realities—not just theoretical optimality. The researcher explicitly asks about pitfalls like alert fatigue (over-reminding leading to user disengagement), reward design trade-offs (e.g., balancing immediate compliance vs. long-term habit formation), and evaluation metrics beyond accuracy—such as escalation latency, caregiver burden, or false-negative rates in missed doses. They also seek concrete starting points: open-source simulators, published clinical reminder systems, or minimal viable prototypes that prioritize interpretability and safety over complexity.

This reflects a broader trend in applied AI for health: moving away from monolithic, black-box models toward transparent, modular, and human-in-the-loop architectures. As contextual bandit methods gain traction in adaptive clinical interventions—and as FDA guidance increasingly emphasizes explainability and failure-mode analysis—the choice isn’t just between POMDPs and bandits, but between formal guarantees and operational resilience. For a medication reminder, being almost right most of the time may be less valuable than being conservatively correct when it matters most.
