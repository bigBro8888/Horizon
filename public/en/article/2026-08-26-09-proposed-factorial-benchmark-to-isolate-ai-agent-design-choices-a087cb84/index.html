Proposed factorial benchmark to isolate AI agent design choices

News time: 2026-08-26T02:38:28.159760+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher has proposed a rigorous experimental framework to evaluate AI agent systems—not just their underlying language models, but the full stack of architectural decisions that shape real-world performance. Current coding-agent benchmarks, the author argues, conflate model capability with system-level design: whether a task fails may stem from poor context assembly, brittle tool interfaces, aggressive output truncation, or superficial acceptance criteria—not the model’s reasoning ability. This makes it impossible to attribute success or failure to any single component.

To address this, the proposal introduces a 2×2 factorial design crossing two independent variables: workflow structure (monolithic vs. decomposed) and model routing policy (frontier-only vs. capability-graded escalation). The four resulting conditions—frontier monolith, routed monolith, frontier decomposed, and routed decomposed—allow researchers to disentangle how much performance depends on how tasks are structured versus which model handles each step. Crucially, the frontier-decomposed condition isolates architectural change while holding model tier constant—a key control for fairness.

All conditions share identical frozen parameters: original task definitions, source revisions, available tools, total retry budget, final acceptance criteria, validator versions, and verifiers. Evaluation focuses on objective outcome-based metrics—not persuasive intermediaries—such as cost per accepted change, false acceptance/rejection rates, first-pass yield, verification time, and reproducibility across three fresh runs. Secondary measures include token usage, latency, escalation count, and context volume. The author explicitly acknowledges one unresolved tension: budget normalization. Decomposition inherently increases call count and context fragmentation—yet allocating equal per-slice budgets would unfairly advantage decomposition, while sharing a system-wide budget may obscure where capacity bottlenecks truly lie.

This is not a claim that decomposition or routing wins. It’s a methodological intervention: a preregistered, falsifiable design meant to make comparisons interpretable. As the post concludes, the goal isn’t to declare a winner—it’s to ensure that when we say ‘Agent A outperforms Agent B,’ we know why.
