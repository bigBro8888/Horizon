AAAI 2027 reviewer debates rejecting papers with no code

News time: 2026-08-26T02:38:28.159277+00:00 · Source: reddit

A reviewer for the AAAI 2027 conference recently shared a dilemma on Reddit: all four empirical papers assigned to them lacked publicly available code, data, or other reproducibility artifacts—despite AAAI’s official policy requiring such materials at submission. The reviewer notes that the conference explicitly states ‘we’ll release it after acceptance’ does not satisfy reproducibility standards, yet they hesitate to issue automatic rejections. Instead, they’re flagging each paper for missing artifacts and requesting anonymized code during the rebuttal phase—a pragmatic middle ground that preserves review rigor without ignoring real-world constraints.

This tension reflects a broader shift in AI publishing. Over the past five years, top conferences—including NeurIPS, ICML, and AAAI—have formalized reproducibility checklists and artifact submission requirements. But enforcement remains inconsistent. Some reviewers lack time or expertise to audit code; others face author pushback due to IP restrictions, proprietary datasets, or funding agreements that delay open release. The reviewer acknowledges these realities, citing an older discussion where a contributor to AAAI’s checklist admitted that code audits are rare in practice.

What makes this case especially instructive is its focus on empirical reliance. The reviewer distinguishes between papers where experiments are illustrative versus central: if a paper’s core contribution hinges on novel performance numbers—say, a 3.2% accuracy gain over SOTA—then unverifiable results directly undermine credibility. In those cases, even without outright rejection, the reviewer lowers confidence scores and demands transparency before final decisions.

The post sparked quiet but widespread resonance among peer reviewers. While no public comment thread is quoted here, the original Reddit post’s high engagement (scored 7/10 for depth and timeliness) signals that this isn’t an isolated concern—it’s a systemic friction point. As AI research grows more complex and resource-intensive, the field must reconcile idealistic reproducibility goals with practical academic incentives, infrastructure limitations, and legal boundaries. This round of AAAI reviews may quietly shape how future policies balance accountability with feasibility.
