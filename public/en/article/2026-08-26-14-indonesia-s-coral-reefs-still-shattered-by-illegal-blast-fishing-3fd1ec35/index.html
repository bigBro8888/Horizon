Indonesia's coral reefs still shattered by illegal blast fishing

News time: 2026-08-26T02:38:28.019127+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Indonesia’s coral reefs—among the most biodiverse marine ecosystems on Earth—are being systematically destroyed by blast fishing, a destructive practice where fishermen detonate homemade explosives underwater to stun or kill fish. Though banned nationwide since 1985 and reinforced with strict penalties including up to five years in prison, the practice persists across remote archipelagos like Maluku and North Sulawesi. Divers and researchers report vast expanses of reef reduced to rubble: branching corals pulverized, reef structures flattened, and recovery stunted for decades. The ecological toll extends far beyond aesthetics—these damaged habitats no longer support fish spawning, diminish coastal protection from storms, and erode livelihoods for thousands who depend on healthy fisheries.

What makes the problem especially stubborn is its socioeconomic roots. In many villages, poverty, limited alternative income sources, and weak law enforcement converge to normalize blast fishing—even among communities that understand its long-term harm. Local authorities often lack patrol boats, radar, or jurisdictional clarity across Indonesia’s 17,000 islands, allowing offenders to operate with near impunity. Meanwhile, demand for cheap seafood in domestic markets and export hubs continues to incentivize high-volume, low-effort harvest methods.

The situation stands in stark contrast to Thailand, where similar laws were enacted around the same time but enforcement improved dramatically after the early 2000s through coordinated naval patrols, community surveillance programs, and rapid prosecution pathways. As one Thai diver notes in the comments, blast-damaged sites there—though still visible—have largely stabilized or begun recovering, unlike in parts of Indonesia where new blast craters appear yearly.

Efforts to counter the practice go beyond policing: NGOs have piloted coral gardening and artificial reef projects, while some villages have adopted ‘no-blast’ pledges backed by peer monitoring. Yet without sustained investment in both enforcement capacity and equitable alternatives—like cold-chain infrastructure for legal catch or microloans for seaweed farming—the cycle remains hard to break. For now, Indonesia’s reefs remain caught between resilience and ruin—one explosion at a time.
