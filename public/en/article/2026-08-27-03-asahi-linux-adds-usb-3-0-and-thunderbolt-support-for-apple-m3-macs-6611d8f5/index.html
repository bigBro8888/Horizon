Asahi Linux adds USB 3.0 and Thunderbolt support for Apple M3 Macs

News time: 2026-08-27T11:10:18.562904+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a major leap for open-source hardware enablement, Asahi Linux has successfully brought full USB 3.0 and Thunderbolt connectivity to Apple’s M3-based Macs — including the M3, M3 Pro, and M3 Max models. This milestone was achieved not through official documentation or vendor cooperation, but by reverse-engineering Apple’s proprietary ACE3 power management controller and its communication interface over SPMI (System Power Management Interface). The breakthrough unlocks previously unusable ports on M3 laptops, enabling external displays, high-speed storage, docking stations, and other peripherals that rely on these standards.

The effort centered on understanding how ACE3 — a critical chip responsible for regulating power states across USB, Thunderbolt, and PCIe subsystems — interacts with the rest of the SoC. Developers mildsunrise and chaos_princess discovered that ACE3 shares nearly identical register layouts with the older CD3217 controller used in prior Apple silicon generations, but communicates via SPMI instead of I²C. That insight allowed them to adapt existing driver logic and build new SPMI bus support into the Linux kernel, culminating in stable, upstream-merge-ready code.

This isn’t just about plugging in devices: it represents a maturation of Asahi Linux’s low-level firmware and power management stack. Reliable USB and Thunderbolt require precise coordination between clock gating, link training, wake-up signaling, and thermal throttling — all managed by ACE3. Getting this right means the system can now enter and exit low-power states correctly, reducing idle power draw and improving overall stability. For users, it transforms the M3 MacBook Pro from a Linux curiosity into a viable daily driver.

While battery life and GPU acceleration remain active work-in-progress areas, this release marks the first time all major I/O subsystems on M3 are functional under Linux. It also sets a precedent: Apple’s tightly controlled silicon ecosystem is no longer an impenetrable barrier, but a challenge that persistent open-source collaboration can systematically dismantle — one register map at a time.
