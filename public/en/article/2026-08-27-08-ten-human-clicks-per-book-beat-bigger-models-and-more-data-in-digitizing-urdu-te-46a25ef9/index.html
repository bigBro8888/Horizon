Ten human clicks per book beat bigger models and more data in digitizing Urdu texts

News time: 2026-08-27T11:10:18.707169+00:00 · Source: reddit

For ten years, the Ibteda Digital Library — a community-driven archive in Pakistan — manually digitized rare Urdu books: lithographs, dictionaries, and historical periodicals. Volunteers used a DIY camera rig and painstakingly cropped each scanned page in Photoshop, one by one. When operations wound down, the project lead realized those decades of manual labor had quietly generated something rare: 575,729 precise crop labels, tied to raw images via robust SIFT + MAGSAC feature matching. Instead of discarding them, he turned this human-curated dataset into ground truth for an automated cropping model.

The results defied conventional scaling wisdom. Doubling the training set from 378 to 572 books? No improvement on unseen volumes. Swapping in ResNet-50? Better fit on training data, but no gain — and even worse performance after calibration — on held-out books. Increasing input resolution to 1024px or adding a spatial head also failed. Ablation revealed the core issue: systematic per-book offset biases — subtle, consistent margin preferences made by individual operators over years. These weren’t visible in pixel patterns; they were invisible human habits embedded in the labels.

The breakthrough wasn’t more data or compute — it was calibration. Just ten operator-corrected crops per new book, aggregated as element-wise median residuals, lifted pass@80 (a strict metric requiring correct crop geometry for ≥80% of pages) from 0.71 to 0.83. That modest human-in-the-loop step outperformed every architectural or data-scaling lever tested. For stain and stamp removal, the team adopted a hybrid approach: a U-Net detected regions needing retouching, but classical OpenCV filled them — ensuring zero byte-level alteration outside the mask. Crucially, labels included REMOVE/KEEP/IGNORE states, and any predicted removal that erased an Urdu diacritic — even with high IoU — automatically vetoed deployment.

This isn’t just about books. It’s a sobering, empirically grounded case study in domain-specific vision tasks where human judgment encodes non-pixel information — preferences, conventions, cultural layout norms — that no amount of resolution or parameter count can infer. The work challenges assumptions baked into modern AI pipelines: that bigger is always better, that pixels contain all necessary signals, and that general-purpose architectures generalize cleanly across niche archival domains.
