NeurIPS 2026 acceptance calculator estimates paper odds from mock scores

News time: 2026-08-28T12:44:25.690451+00:00 · Source: reddit

A lightweight web tool called the NeurIPS 2026 Acceptance Calculator has appeared on GitHub Pages, offering researchers a quick way to estimate their paper’s chances of being accepted to the upcoming NeurIPS conference. Built by Reddit user u/levydawg and hosted at levilingsch.github.io/neurips-acceptance-estimator, the calculator asks users to input mock reviewer scores—such as confidence, technical quality, and broader impact—and select an assumed overall acceptance rate (e.g., 22% or 25%). It then applies a simple statistical model to return an estimated probability of acceptance.

The tool does not access real NeurIPS review data or official scoring thresholds. Instead, it relies on heuristic assumptions: for example, that final decisions correlate with the average of normalized reviewer scores, and that the distribution of those scores approximates a known pattern (e.g., Gaussian or truncated normal). This makes it a rough sanity-check aid—not a predictive engine. As noted in the Reddit post, it’s explicitly labeled a ‘small model’, and its creator encourages users to treat outputs as directional guidance rather than definitive forecasts.

Its timing is notable: NeurIPS 2026 reviewing is already underway, and recent critiques—including reports of high score variance, reviewer overload, and security flaws like PDF prompt injection—have heightened anxiety among submitters. With acceptance rates hovering near 22–24% in recent years and decisions increasingly influenced by subjective Area Chair (AC) and Senior Area Chair (SAC) judgments, many authors seek any foothold for calibration. The calculator fills that niche: low-friction, open, and immediately usable—no login or installation required.

Still, the tool has clear limitations. It lacks empirical validation against past NeurIPS outcomes, doesn’t incorporate meta-factors like author reputation, submission timing, or contribution type (e.g., Theory vs. Use-Inspired), and assumes uniform score weighting across criteria—a simplification contradicted by NeurIPS’ own 2026 Reviewer Guidelines, which emphasize differentiated evaluation standards. For all its utility, it remains what its creator calls it: a heuristic, not a model trained on ground-truth decisions.
