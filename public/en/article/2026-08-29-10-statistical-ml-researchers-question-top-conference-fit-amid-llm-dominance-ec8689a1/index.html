Statistical ML researchers question top conference fit amid LLM dominance

News time: 2026-08-29T07:50:43.838629+00:00 · Source: reddit

A seasoned statistical and probabilistic machine learning researcher recently voiced growing unease about the shifting landscape of top-tier ML conferences. In a candid Reddit post on r/MachineLearning, they described how ICLR and NeurIPS — long considered the flagship venues for foundational ML work — have become overwhelmingly dominated by large language model (LLM) and agent-centric research. At this year’s ICLR, they observed that fewer than one in ten poster presentations addressed classical statistical inference, Bayesian modeling, or probabilistic reasoning; most instead focused on prompting, fine-tuning, or evaluating LLMs on narrow benchmarks. Similarly, NeurIPS workshops leaned heavily into agentic AI, with little room for topics like stochastic optimization, variational inference, or causal learning from limited data.

This isn’t just about aesthetics or personal preference — it reflects a deeper structural shift. The researcher notes that while figures like Arnaud Doucet, Aapo Hyvärinen, and Stefano Ermon still publish in NeurIPS or ICLR, their work often appears in specialized tracks or workshops, not the main conference. That marginalization raises practical concerns: junior researchers may struggle to gain visibility, citations, or hiring traction if their core contributions lie outside the current spotlight. The post suggests a strategic pivot toward AISTATS and UAI — conferences explicitly designed for statistical rigor, uncertainty quantification, and principled modeling — as more natural homes for such work.

AISTATS, now in its 28th year, emphasizes the intersection of statistics, machine learning, and data science, with strong ties to theoretical foundations and real-world applications. UAI (Uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence) has similarly prioritized probabilistic reasoning, graphical models, decision theory, and causal inference since its founding in 1985. Both are peer-reviewed, highly selective, and recognized by the CCF (China Computer Federation) as tier-B or tier-C venues — respected within academia, though less visible to industry recruiters than NeurIPS or ICML.

The concern goes beyond venue choice. It’s about disciplinary identity: whether ‘machine learning’ as a field still meaningfully includes statistical depth, or whether it’s converging into a narrower engineering discipline centered on scaling and prompting foundation models. For researchers committed to interpretability, robustness under distribution shift, or learning from scarce or noisy labels, the question isn’t just where to submit — it’s whether the broader community still values what they do.
