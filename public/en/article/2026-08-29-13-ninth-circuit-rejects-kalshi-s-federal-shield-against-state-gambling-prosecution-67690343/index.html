Ninth Circuit rejects Kalshi’s federal shield against state gambling prosecutions

News time: 2026-08-29T07:50:43.465778+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a unanimous decision issued on August 28, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled that Kalshi’s sports-related event contracts — though regulated by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) as derivatives — do not immunize the company from state-level criminal prosecution for illegal gambling. The case centered on Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes’ effort to prosecute Kalshi under state law for offering sports betting–linked contracts to Arizona residents. The court held that the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) does not expressly preempt state gambling statutes, and Congress never intended to displace decades of state regulatory authority over wagering.

The ruling reaffirms a foundational principle of American federalism: unless federal law explicitly overrides it, state power to regulate or prohibit gambling remains intact. Judge Ryan Nelson wrote that 'Congress did not take a wrecking ball to all sports gambling regulations built up over decades by federal, state, and tribal governments when it amended the CEA.' This language underscores that CEA authorization for certain prediction markets operates alongside — not above — state laws. Kalshi had argued its CFTC registration granted it immunity from state enforcement, but the Ninth Circuit found no statutory basis for that claim.

Technically, the dispute turned on whether Kalshi’s contracts fall under the CEA’s narrow exemption for 'event contracts' — which the CFTC approved in 2022 — and whether that approval displaces state prohibitions. The court concluded it does not: the CEA regulates how such contracts are traded (e.g., transparency, clearing), not whether they constitute illegal gambling under state definitions. Arizona law defines sports wagering broadly, and Kalshi’s offerings fit squarely within that definition — meaning state prosecutors retain full authority to act.

This decision doesn’t ban prediction markets outright, nor does it invalidate Kalshi’s CFTC license. But it clarifies a critical boundary: federal oversight of financial derivatives does not erase state police powers. For fintech startups operating at the intersection of finance and gaming, the message is clear — compliance with federal regulators is necessary but insufficient. They must also navigate a patchwork of state laws, some of which treat prediction markets as gambling per se. As more than 20 similar lawsuits wind through courts nationwide, the Ninth Circuit’s reasoning may influence how other circuits interpret the limits of CEA preemption.
