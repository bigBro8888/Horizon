Texas added $1 auto insurance fee to fund 3200+ Flock surveillance cameras

News time: 2026-08-30T06:36:52.270210+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In 2023, the Texas Legislature quietly passed a law requiring all auto insurance policies in the state to include a mandatory $1 surcharge. Officially billed as a measure to combat catalytic converter theft—a fast-growing crime fueled by soaring prices for precious metals like rhodium and palladium—the fee was directed to the Motor Vehicle Crime Prevention Authority (MVCPA), a state agency overseen by a board largely appointed by Governor Greg Abbott. What followed was rapid, low-profile expansion: MVCPA used the collected funds to deploy over 3,200 Flock Safety license plate recognition (LPR) cameras across Texas cities and counties—many installed without public hearings, local council approval, or clear disclosure to residents.

Flock cameras differ from conventional security systems. Rather than recording continuous video, they capture high-resolution images of license plates, extract alphanumeric data, and log timestamps and GPS coordinates. That data is uploaded to Flock’s cloud platform, where it can be searched by law enforcement agencies—often with minimal oversight or audit requirements. While proponents cite successes in recovering stolen vehicles, critics stress that Flock’s system enables persistent, location-based tracking of any vehicle passing a camera, raising concerns about mass surveillance far beyond its stated anti-theft purpose.

The effectiveness of this approach against catalytic converter theft remains unverified. No peer-reviewed study or official MVCPA report has demonstrated a measurable decline in such thefts attributable to the cameras. Meanwhile, thefts continue: catalytic converters remain easy targets—mounted underneath vehicles and removable in under two minutes—and metal prices remain high. Some communities have even reported spikes in related crimes, including violent confrontations during attempted thefts.

Beyond efficacy, transparency and accountability are under fire. The $1 fee is embedded in insurance bills without itemization, making it invisible to most consumers. Flock’s contracts with Texas agencies reportedly contain clauses limiting public access to usage logs and shielding data-sharing practices from open records requests. As one commenter noted, the irony isn’t lost: a nation founded on resistance to taxation without representation now funds pervasive surveillance through an opaque, compulsory insurance levy.
