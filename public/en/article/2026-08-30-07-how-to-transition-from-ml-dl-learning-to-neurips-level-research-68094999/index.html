How to transition from ML/DL learning to NeurIPS-level research

News time: 2026-08-30T06:36:53.244561+00:00 · Source: reddit

A motivated learner who has gone beyond library-based tutorials—mastering the mathematical foundations of machine learning and deep learning—is now asking a pivotal question: What comes next on the path to publishing at top-tier conferences like NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR? This isn’t about quick wins or tutorial projects; it’s about building the habits, skills, and mindset of a researcher. The question reflects a common but under-discussed inflection point: when foundational knowledge is solid, the real work begins—not with more courses, but with deliberate practice in problem framing, literature engagement, code reproducibility, and iterative experimentation.

Transitioning to research requires shifting from consumption to creation. That means reading recent papers not just to understand results, but to dissect methodology, identify assumptions, and spot open questions. It means implementing papers from scratch—not copying GitHub repos, but re-deriving equations, debugging gradient flows, and varying hyperparameters to test robustness. Projects should prioritize depth over breadth: for example, reproducing a single NeurIPS paper end-to-end, then modifying one component (e.g., swapping attention mechanisms or loss functions) and rigorously evaluating the impact.

Mentorship and community integration are equally critical. Top conferences don’t reward isolated effort—they value contributions that engage with ongoing scholarly conversations. Attending reading groups, contributing to open-source ML libraries (e.g., PyTorch, JAX, or Hugging Face), or submitting well-documented bug reports and PRs builds visibility and credibility. Many early-career researchers land their first publications through collaborations sparked at workshops or via cold emails to authors whose work they’ve deeply engaged with.

A realistic roadmap spans 12–24 months: start with workshop papers (e.g., NeurIPS workshops, MLSys, or AABI), then target second-tier conferences (e.g., UAI, AISTATS), before aiming for ICML or NeurIPS. Each submission—even if rejected—yields invaluable feedback. Crucially, NeurIPS and ICML enforce strict dual-submission policies and require anonymized submissions via OpenReview, with abstract deadlines acting as hard cutoffs. Success hinges less on genius and more on consistency: reading 2–3 papers weekly, coding 10+ hours weekly, and writing drafts early and often.
