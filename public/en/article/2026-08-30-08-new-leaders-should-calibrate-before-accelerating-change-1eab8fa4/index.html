New leaders should calibrate before accelerating change

News time: 2026-08-30T06:36:52.270234+00:00 · Source: hackernews

When engineers step into their first leadership role—whether as a team lead, engineering manager, or CTO—they often face intense pressure to prove themselves quickly. A common reflex is to launch initiatives: reorganize teams, adopt new tools, rewrite documentation, or overhaul processes. But as the essay 'Calibrate Before You Accelerate' argues, this 'bias toward action' is frequently counterproductive. Rather than demonstrating competence, premature change can erode trust, disrupt workflows, and obscure real problems beneath layers of self-inflicted complexity.

The article draws on lived experience—not abstract theory—to make its case. It urges new leaders to spend their first weeks observing, asking questions, mapping informal power structures, and understanding why systems exist as they do. This isn’t passive waiting; it’s active learning. One reader recounts a CTO who, within days of joining, replaced a seasoned Cisco-certified network administrator with help-desk staff—triggering audit failures and operational chaos. Another cites Chesterton’s Fence: before removing a fence, ask why it was built. The principle applies equally to Jira migrations, sprint rituals, or promotion criteria.

This perspective stands in deliberate contrast to Silicon Valley mantras like 'move fast and break things.' While speed has value, velocity without direction—or worse, without awareness of terrain—is motion without progress. The essay doesn’t oppose change; it opposes untethered change. It reframes leadership not as an act of imposition but as one of stewardship: protecting what works while carefully improving what doesn’t.

For engineering organizations, this mindset shift matters deeply. Teams led by calibrated managers report higher psychological safety, lower turnover, and more sustainable delivery rhythms. And for individual contributors stepping up, resisting the urge to 'leave a fingerprint' may be the most powerful signal of maturity—and the best foundation for lasting impact.
