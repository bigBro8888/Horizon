Many ML practitioners miss whiteboard thinking in code-heavy workflows

News time: 2026-08-30T06:36:53.244298+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent Reddit post from a radar digital signal processing (DSP) engineer sparked quiet but widespread resonance across the machine learning community. The author recalls how, as an undergraduate, they relied heavily on whiteboards: sketching diagrams, annotating equations, talking ideas aloud, and iteratively refining concepts through visual and verbal exploration. That tactile, low-friction ideation — where erasing is effortless and spatial reasoning comes naturally — formed the backbone of their early technical learning.

Now working in radar DSP — a field increasingly intertwined with deep learning, numerical simulation, and long training cycles — the practitioner finds themselves spending most days in IDEs and Jupyter notebooks. Code has become the default medium for testing hypotheses, yet something feels missing: the spontaneity, the shared physical space of co-thinking, the ability to step back and see relationships at a glance. The post isn’t a critique of modern tooling, but a gentle inquiry: How do others preserve that exploratory, visual mode of reasoning when the workflow is dominated by scripts, logs, and GPU wait times?

Responses in the thread reflect a spectrum of adaptation. Some users describe repurposing notebooks as ‘digital whiteboards’ — using markdown cells for sketches, inline plots for quick validation, and rich output for iterative feedback. Others keep physical whiteboards nearby for architecture design or debugging sessions, especially when collaborating remotely via screen share. A few mention hybrid tools like Excalidraw or Mermaid embedded in Obsidian or VS Code, bridging hand-drawn intuition with reproducible documentation.

What emerges isn’t a technical breakthrough, but a subtle cultural observation: as ML and DSP mature into production-oriented disciplines, the cognitive scaffolding once provided by chalk and dry-erase markers hasn’t vanished — it’s been redistributed, often informally and unevenly, across digital tools, habits, and team rituals. The question lingers not because there’s a single right answer, but because recognizing this shift helps practitioners intentionally design workflows that support both rigor and creativity.
