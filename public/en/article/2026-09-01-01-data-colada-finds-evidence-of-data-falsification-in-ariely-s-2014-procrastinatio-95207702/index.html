Data Colada finds evidence of data falsification in Ariely’s 2014 procrastination study

News time: 2026-09-01T06:13:45.552003+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new investigation by Data Colada—a collaborative blog run by psychology and economics researchers—has uncovered strong statistical evidence of data fabrication in a highly influential 2014 field experiment on procrastination, co-authored by behavioral economist Dan Ariely. The study, which claimed that simple commitment devices (like self-imposed deadlines) significantly improved task completion among university students, has been cited over 1,100 times and featured in Ariely’s popular books and public talks. Using forensic data analysis techniques—including tests for improbable duplication, unnatural symmetry, and implausible effect sizes—Data Colada identified patterns inconsistent with authentic human-subject data collection.

This is not the first time Ariely’s work has faced such scrutiny. In 2021, Data Colada exposed fabricated data in a 2012 dishonesty study he co-authored; that paper was retracted, and all authors acknowledged Ariely as the sole person with pre-publication access to the raw data. The latest finding reopens questions about oversight, accountability, and the durability of findings that shape public policy and self-help advice. Unlike lab-based experiments requiring specialized equipment, this procrastination study involved straightforward tasks—proofreading, deadline setting, and self-reporting—making replication both feasible and urgent.

The broader context is the ongoing replication crisis in social science. A landmark seven-year effort to replicate 3,900 social-science papers found only half could be successfully reproduced. Misaligned academic incentives—such as rewarding novel, statistically significant results over careful replication or null findings—have long encouraged questionable research practices. As one commenter noted, an unusually large effect size can itself serve as a red flag, even without deep statistical training: when results look ‘too good to be true,’ they often are.

What makes this case especially consequential is its visibility. Ariely’s work helped mainstream behavioral economics beyond academia, influencing corporate HR policies, education tools, and personal productivity apps. If foundational studies like this contain fabricated data, it undermines trust not just in individual authors but in how scientific consensus forms—and how quickly flawed ideas spread before correction. The episode underscores a sobering reality: detecting fraud often depends less on high-tech tools than on careful attention to data texture, consistency, and plausibility.
