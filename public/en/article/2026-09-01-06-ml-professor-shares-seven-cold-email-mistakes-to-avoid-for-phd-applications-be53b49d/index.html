ML professor shares seven cold email mistakes to avoid for PhD applications

News time: 2026-09-01T06:13:46.031156+00:00 · Source: reddit

It’s that time of year again: prospective PhD students are flooding professors’ inboxes with unsolicited emails seeking supervision. A seasoned machine learning professor recently shared blunt, practical advice on Reddit—distilling years of experience into seven clear pitfalls to avoid when reaching out cold. The post quickly resonated across the r/MachineLearning community, not because it offers new theory or technical insight, but because it tackles a high-stakes, emotionally fraught ritual many applicants navigate blindly.

The professor begins by affirming that cold emailing itself isn’t problematic—it’s standard practice in many countries and often necessary given how PhD admissions work outside centralized application systems. But effectiveness hinges entirely on execution. First and foremost: brevity is non-negotiable. Long emails drastically reduce the chance of being read at all. Equally important is relevance—applicants should target only those whose research genuinely aligns with their own intellectual focus, not just loosely related application domains. For instance, if a professor works on foundational ML theory (e.g., optimization, generalization, representation learning), an email pitching "AI for healthcare" or "LLMs for finance" signals misalignment—not curiosity.

The post then highlights deeper red flags: vague research interests like "Machine Learning and AI" suggest superficial engagement; listing workshop papers as peer-reviewed conference publications raises serious integrity concerns; over-reliance on LLMs for drafting—even for framing research ideas—produces generic, undifferentiated proposals that read like undergraduate projects. Professors aren’t looking for summaries of their papers—they want evidence of independent thinking: how you’d extend, challenge, or connect their work to something new. Finally, ignoring explicit instructions on faculty websites (e.g., required subject-line tags) almost guarantees your email lands in spam.

What makes this advice especially valuable is its grounding in real-world gatekeeping. Unlike generic career blogs, this comes from someone who reads hundreds of such emails annually—and knows exactly what gets deleted versus what sparks a reply. It reframes cold outreach not as a formality to check off, but as a first research artifact: a concise, thoughtful, technically grounded expression of scholarly intent. For applicants, the takeaway is clear—your email isn’t just a request; it’s your earliest demonstration of readiness for doctoral work.
