Kākāpō population reaches 325 after record breeding season

News time: 2026-09-01T06:13:45.276198+00:00 · Source: rss

In a rare and heartening conservation milestone, the kākāpō — New Zealand’s iconic, flightless, nocturnal parrot — now numbers 325 individuals. This figure reflects the successful integration of this year’s juvenile chicks into the official population count, following what conservationists are calling a record-breaking breeding season. The growth is especially meaningful given that just three decades ago, in 1995, only 51 kākāpō remained alive, teetering on the absolute brink of extinction.

The kākāpō recovery effort is one of the most intensive wildlife interventions ever undertaken. Every known bird is individually named, monitored via radio transmitters, and managed across predator-free offshore islands like Whenua Hou (Codfish Island) and Anchor Island. Breeding is carefully timed and supported: scientists track hormonal cues, hand-raise chicks when necessary, supplement diets with high-calorie ‘kākāpō cake’, and even intervene to prevent inbreeding through strategic pairing and artificial insemination.

Andrew Digby, a conservation biologist and kākāpō scientist who has worked closely with the species for years, shared the news on Bluesky — calling it ‘the best news of the year’. His post underscores not just the numerical gain, but the broader principle it validates: that even species reduced to microscopic populations can rebound when conservation is long-term, science-led, and relentlessly resourced. It’s a powerful rebuttal to ecological fatalism — proof that extinction is not inevitable, even for species with extreme biological constraints.

Still, challenges remain. Kākāpō are slow-maturing (females don’t breed until age 6–10), highly susceptible to disease, and genetically bottlenecked due to their near-extinction event. Climate change also threatens their primary food source — the rimu tree, whose mast fruiting cycles are becoming less predictable. Yet the 325 figure represents more than survival: it signals growing resilience, increased genetic diversity from recent outcrossing efforts, and hard-won operational confidence in the recovery program. For conservation globally, the kākāpō story is both a blueprint and a benchmark.
