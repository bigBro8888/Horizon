FBI investigates service that stored and sold 153 million driver's license images

News time: 2026-09-02T05:47:15.248076+00:00 · Source: hackernews

The FBI has opened a criminal investigation into a third-party identity verification service that collected, stored, and allegedly sold or exposed over 153 million driver’s license images — a breach affecting roughly half of all U.S. licensed drivers. According to reporting by KrebsOnSecurity, the service operated under the guise of helping businesses comply with Know Your Customer (KYC) and anti-fraud requirements, but instead retained highly sensitive government-issued ID documents far beyond what was necessary for verification.

This isn’t just a case of poor cybersecurity hygiene — it reflects a systemic failure in how identity verification is practiced across industries. Many online platforms require users to upload both front and back scans of their driver’s licenses and perform real-time liveness checks (like head tilting), creating rich biometric and document datasets. Yet as commenters point out, these checks often rely on easily spoofed app-based methods rather than secure, direct access to state DMV databases — meaning attackers can bypass them with forged images while ordinary users surrender detailed personal data.

What makes this incident especially alarming is the scale of data retention: 153,347,439 driver’s license images were held indefinitely, with no evidence they were ever deleted after verification. Under U.S. privacy norms — and principles like GDPR’s storage limitation — such long-term retention violates the core idea of data minimization: collect only what you need, and delete it once the purpose is fulfilled. Yet no federal law mandates strict time limits for private-sector ID data retention, leaving companies free to hoard documents for years, whether for fraud analytics, AI training, or other unannounced uses.

The fallout extends beyond individual risk. Because many affected IDs were linked to marijuana dispensaries — businesses operating in legal gray zones — exposure could enable harassment, discrimination, or even voter suppression efforts, as one commenter darkly speculated. Meanwhile, liability remains weak: without statutory penalties or strict liability rules, firms face little incentive to minimize data collection or invest in robust deletion protocols. Until laws change, this breach may be less an outlier and more a preview of what happens when verification becomes extraction — and compliance becomes cover.
