Nori Robotics launches $1,688 bimanual mobile humanoid for developers

News time: 2026-09-02T05:47:15.248229+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Nori Robotics, a Y Combinator–backed startup founded by Columbia University robotics researcher Antonio, has launched Nori — a compact, bimanual mobile humanoid robot priced at $1,688. Designed explicitly for developers and academic labs, Nori aims to solve a long-standing bottleneck in robotics R&D: the scarcity of affordable, capable hardware. Most research institutions rely on one or two high-end robots — like Boston Dynamics’ Atlas or Toyota’s T-HR3 — which cost hundreds of thousands of dollars and are often locked down for proprietary use. That makes large-scale dataset collection, reproducible experiments, or multi-robot testing nearly impossible for students and early-stage teams.

Nori packs 19 degrees of freedom across its body: two 7+1 DOF arms (each lifting up to 1.5 kg), a 55 kg telescoping torso lift, a differential wheeled base for stable navigation, four RGB cameras, 2D LiDAR, dual microphones with full-duplex voice support, and a 432 Wh battery. Its onboard Raspberry Pi 5 handles real-time SLAM and safety-critical functions, while heavier AI workloads — like vision-language action (VLA) models or imitation learning pipelines — run remotely over LAN or WAN. The robot is assembled in San Francisco and ships with an open SDK, teleoperation tools, and a browser-based simulator for zero-hardware prototyping.

Cost reduction was central to Nori’s design philosophy. To hit sub-$2,000 pricing, the team opted for high-ratio RC-style servos instead of expensive torque-controlled actuators (e.g., QDD motors), and chose wheels over legs — trading humanoid locomotion for reliability, simplicity, and manufacturability. Over seven hardware iterations, they prioritized repairability: all structural parts are designed for 3D printing, and CAD files are publicly available. Nori already performs basic tasks — opening drawers, restocking shelves, pouring beer — as shown in publicly shared demo videos.

The launch reflects a broader shift in embodied AI: from monolithic, lab-bound platforms toward accessible, developer-first hardware. Competitors like Reachy 2 ($9,990) and AhaRobot ($1,000, but less integrated) occupy adjacent niches, but Nori stands out for its balance of mobility, dexterity, and price. While not intended for industrial deployment, it lowers the barrier for researchers exploring imitation learning, human-in-the-loop training, or real-world manipulation datasets — areas where data scarcity remains the biggest roadblock.
