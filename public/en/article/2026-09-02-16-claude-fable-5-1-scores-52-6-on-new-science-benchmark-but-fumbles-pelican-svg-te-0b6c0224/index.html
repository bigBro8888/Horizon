Claude Fable 5.1 scores 52.6% on new science benchmark but fumbles pelican SVG test

News time: 2026-09-02T05:47:14.737519+00:00 · Source: rss

Anthropic has released Claude Fable 5.1, touting it as a major leap for coding, knowledge work, and long-horizon problem solving. The model’s most eye-catching claim is a 52.6% score on Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 — a brand-new, terminal-native benchmark launched just days earlier that evaluates AI agents on end-to-end scientific research workflows. That result dwarfs prior versions: Fable 5 scored 24.7%, Opus 5 got 29.0%, and even GPT-5.6 Sol managed only 22.4%. Other benchmarks like Terminal-Bench 4.0 and CursorBench also improved, but none matched the magnitude of the science-bench jump.

Yet Simon Willison, in his characteristically witty and grounded analysis, asks a deceptively simple question: How well can it pelican? He’s referring to the ‘pelican benchmark’ — an informal, self-deprecating test he invented in October 2024: prompt the model with “Generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle” and assess whether the output is syntactically valid, visually coherent, and physically whimsical (but not broken). Once a surprisingly reliable proxy for multimodal reasoning and instruction-following, the pelican test has lately lost predictive power — as Willison himself noted in July — prompting even Andrej Karpathy to declare it ‘retired’ in early August.

Willison ran the pelican prompt across all five reasoning effort levels in Fable 5.1 (low, medium, high, xhigh, max) using his open-source llm-anthropic tool. Surprisingly, both low and medium efforts produced SVGs without any visible reasoning traces, despite Anthropic’s documentation stating reasoning is always active. The outputs were minimalist flat-style illustrations — one pelican pedaling left, another right — but neither included motion details like pedaling legs or handlebar grips beyond the base prompt. At high effort, reasoning appeared, token count rose sharply (2,612), and cost jumped to 13.1 cents — yet the SVG remained functionally similar.

The tension here isn’t about failure; it’s about misalignment. A model excelling at complex scientific reasoning doesn’t automatically translate to robust, consistent, creative code generation — especially under constrained, open-ended prompts. Willison’s experiment underscores a growing industry concern: benchmark inflation risks obscuring real-world capability gaps. As AI systems grow more specialized, we need evaluation tools that reflect actual user tasks — not just what’s easiest to automate and score.
