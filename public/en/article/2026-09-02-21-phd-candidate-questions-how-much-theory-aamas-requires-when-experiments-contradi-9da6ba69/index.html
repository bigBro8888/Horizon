PhD candidate questions how much theory AAMAS requires when experiments contradict hypothesis

News time: 2026-09-02T05:47:15.455703+00:00 · Source: reddit

A second-year PhD student recently posted on r/MachineLearning seeking advice ahead of their first submission to AAMAS—the top-tier conference on autonomous agents and multiagent systems. Their project began with a clean, testable hypothesis about architectural robustness under increasing perturbation difficulty. But after running controlled experiments, the results only partially supported the claim—revealing nuanced boundary conditions instead of a clear ordering. Rather than reporting this honestly, the student dove into post-hoc theoretical modeling, quickly realizing they’d slipped into HARKing: formulating hypotheses after seeing the data and presenting them as if predicted in advance.

The situation worsened when they discovered hidden bugs in their codebase—a dependency on an undocumented public repository with misconfigured parameters. Now re-running all experiments from scratch, they’re left questioning whether their earlier theoretical sketches hold any weight. Their core dilemma is deeply familiar to early-career researchers: how much formal theory does AAMAS actually expect for an empirical multiagent reinforcement learning (MARL) paper? Is a strong empirical characterization paired with a plausible but incomplete theoretical sketch acceptable—or does it risk desk rejection? And crucially, what do you do when you realize mid-project that your narrative has been shaped by results rather than principles?

AAMAS explicitly welcomes both theoretical and empirical contributions, emphasizing originality, soundness, reproducibility, and clarity—not rigid methodological prescriptions. Past proceedings show many accepted papers feature empirically grounded insights with modest or evolving theory, especially in applied MARL. What reviewers consistently flag is not lack of theory per se, but lack of intellectual honesty: overstating claims, hiding negative results, or disguising exploratory analysis as confirmatory. The student’s self-awareness—naming HARKing, auditing their pipeline, and pausing to re-run experiments—is itself a sign of rigorous research practice.

Their path forward likely lies not in scrapping the work, but reframing it: treat the boundary conditions as the central finding, document the debugging process transparently, and position the partial theory as a testable conjecture—not a conclusion. That kind of integrity, paired with careful experimentation, often resonates more at AAMAS than polished-but-fragile narratives. After all, science advances not just through confirmation, but through precise, well-documented exceptions.
