New 'true unemployment' metric highlights underpaid and underemployed workers

News time: 2026-09-02T05:47:15.248021+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A growing critique of official U.S. unemployment statistics argues that the headline figure—often hovering near 4%—paints an overly optimistic picture of labor market health. The article from lisep.org introduces a broader concept called 'true unemployment,' which expands the definition beyond those actively seeking work and unable to find it. It includes people who are employed but earn less than a living wage, those working part-time involuntarily, and individuals who have dropped out of the labor force entirely—so-called 'discouraged workers.' By incorporating these groups, the proposed metric suggests that functional joblessness is far more widespread than standard reports indicate.

This idea isn’t entirely new: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) already publishes the U-6 unemployment rate, its broadest measure of labor underutilization. U-6 includes not only the officially unemployed but also marginally attached workers (e.g., those who want a job but haven’t searched recently) and people working part-time for economic reasons—such as inability to find full-time work. Yet even U-6 stops short of factoring in wage adequacy. The 'true unemployment' proposal goes further by asking whether employment itself is economically sufficient—raising questions about labor quality, not just quantity.

The article pegs a conservative living wage at $26,000 annually (in 2025 dollars), before taxes—a figure that draws skepticism from commenters who note regional cost-of-living disparities and point out that $52,000 may barely cover basics in many U.S. cities. It also sidesteps methodological transparency: no clear data sources, sampling strategy, or weighting scheme is disclosed. That lack of rigor limits its use as a policy tool, even if its underlying concern—that many jobs fail to provide economic security—is widely shared across academic, advocacy, and journalistic circles.

Still, the debate reflects a real tension in how we measure economic well-being. Governments define unemployment narrowly for administrative and statistical consistency—focusing on labor force participation and job search behavior—not income adequacy or job stability. But for workers facing rent hikes, student debt, and stagnant wages, that narrow lens can feel disconnected from lived reality. As one commenter observed, the stronger message may not be about redefining unemployment, but about confronting the proliferation of jobs that pay too little to live on.
