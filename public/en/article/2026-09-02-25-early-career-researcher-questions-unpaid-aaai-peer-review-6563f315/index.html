Early-career researcher questions unpaid AAAI peer review

News time: 2026-09-02T05:47:15.455203+00:00 · Source: reddit

A candid Reddit post titled 'I regret reviewing for AAAI [D]' has resonated with many early-career researchers in machine learning. The author — who identifies as an early-career academic without tenure-track or PI status — describes signing up to review papers for the Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AAAI) conference out of a mix of idealism and ego: they felt flattered by the automated invitation email and wanted to contribute to the community. Yet almost immediately, doubt set in. They ask themselves bluntly: Why agree to unpaid, uncredited labor that benefits the conference and authors but offers no tangible professional return? And why do it when rejection decisions — however well-reasoned — inevitably frustrate submitters?

The reviewer acknowledges the personal upside: reading papers outside their immediate specialty sharpens critical thinking and exposes them to new ideas and writing styles. They also recognize that no one forced them to accept — the invitation arrived en masse to past AAAI authors, not as a personalized request. Still, the asymmetry weighs heavily: reviewers invest time and expertise, while authors receive feedback (often negative), and organizers gain free labor to maintain rigorous selection — all without compensation, formal recognition, or guaranteed reciprocity in future submissions.

This tension reflects a broader structural issue in AI conferences. Unlike journals, which sometimes offer reviewer certificates or editorial board roles, top-tier AI venues like AAAI, NeurIPS, and ICML rely almost entirely on volunteer reviewers — many of whom are PhD students, postdocs, or non-tenure-track faculty. These individuals bear disproportionate service burdens despite having less institutional power or job security than tenured professors. The post doesn’t propose reforms, but its raw honesty underscores how easily goodwill can curdle into resentment when expectations around academic citizenship go unexamined.

What makes the post notable isn’t novelty — similar concerns appear regularly on r/MachineLearning — but its emotional transparency. It names the quiet calculus many make: 'Is this worth my limited time?' 'Do I get anything back?' 'Am I just being used?' For early-career researchers navigating precarious employment and intense publication pressure, voluntary service can feel less like contribution and more like extraction. The closing question — 'How does everyone else feel about non-reciprocal reviewing?' — invites reflection, not resolution.
