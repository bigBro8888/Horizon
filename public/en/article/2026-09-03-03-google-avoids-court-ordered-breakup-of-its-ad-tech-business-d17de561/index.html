Google avoids court-ordered breakup of its ad tech business

News time: 2026-09-03T05:45:02.385033+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a pivotal antitrust ruling on September 2, 2026, a U.S. federal court declined to order Google to divest parts of its advertising technology business — despite finding that the company holds monopoly power in multiple key segments of the digital ad tech stack. The Department of Justice had argued that Google’s vertical integration across ad servers, supply-side platforms (SSPs), demand-side platforms (DSPs), and ad exchanges created insurmountable barriers to competition. But the judge concluded that structural remedies like forced divestiture were not warranted at this stage, opting instead for behavioral restrictions and enhanced transparency requirements.

The decision marks a major inflection point in U.S. antitrust enforcement against Big Tech. Unlike the 2023 Microsoft consent decree or the ongoing Meta case, this ruling explicitly affirms monopoly status while stopping short of structural relief — raising questions about whether courts still view breakups as viable tools. Legal experts note the judge emphasized Google’s declining ad tech revenue — $30 billion last year, just 8% of Alphabet’s total, and down for 16 consecutive quarters — as evidence that the unit is no longer central to the company’s dominance or profitability.

Critically, the ruling does not absolve Google: it confirms monopolization in three markets — publisher ad servers, ad exchange services, and open auction demand-side platforms — and mandates new compliance measures, including third-party audits of auction fairness and data access rules for rival publishers and advertisers. Yet many observers see the outcome as a de facto win for Google’s long-standing argument that its ad tech ecosystem delivers net benefits through automation, efficiency, and real-time bidding — core features of programmatic advertising.

The broader implications extend beyond Google. With over 75% of Alphabet’s revenue still coming from advertising overall, the court’s distinction between ‘ad tech’ (the infrastructure layer) and ‘advertising’ (the revenue-generating service) may reshape how regulators define relevant markets in future cases. It also intensifies debate over whether tax-based disincentives or merger reform — rather than litigation-driven breakups — offer more practical paths to restoring competition in concentrated digital markets.
