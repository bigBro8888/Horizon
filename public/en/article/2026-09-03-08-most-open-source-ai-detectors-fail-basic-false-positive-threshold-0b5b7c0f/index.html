Most open-source AI detectors fail basic false-positive threshold

News time: 2026-09-03T05:45:03.041747+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new, rigorously controlled benchmark has exposed deep flaws in the current generation of open-source AI text detectors. Researchers tested six widely used models—including Tropa-mini, Desklib’s ai-text-detector-v1.01, and the legacy OpenAI RoBERTa detector—on a standardized evaluation protocol using only publicly available data. Human text came from three carefully curated sources: 1,992 human-written documents from the 2025 NBER Jabarian & Imas dataset; 1,060 essays by non-native English speakers (Liang 2023 TOEFL corpus); and 5,000 pre-LLM web pages from FineWeb (2018) serving as a baseline 'native human' pool. AI-generated text included outputs from frontier models like GPT-5.x, Claude Opus 5, and Gemini 3.x—as well as versions deliberately paraphrased using 'humanizer' tools to evade detection.

Each detector was calibrated to hit exactly a 0.5% false-positive rate (FPR) on the 6,930-strong human validation set—a stringent but realistic bar for real-world deployment, where flagging innocent human writers is ethically and operationally unacceptable. Shockingly, four of the six models could not even reach that threshold without flagging large swaths of ordinary human writing. For example, MAGE flagged 26% of human web text with near-certainty (>0.9999 score), while the original OpenAI RoBERTa detector achieved an AUC of just 0.31—worse than random guessing on modern LLM output.

The most dramatic failure occurred on paraphrased AI text: the top-performing model (Tropa-mini) detected only 42% of humanized AI, while the second-best caught just 4%. All models also exhibited strong bias against non-native English writers—flagging their essays at higher rates than native speakers’, revealing a systemic fairness problem baked into training data and feature engineering. Performance on frontier-model outputs was similarly abysmal: Tropa-mini achieved only 33.6% recall, and several models scored below 1%.

This isn’t a matter of tuning or minor updates—it points to foundational limitations in how these detectors work. They rely heavily on statistical surface patterns (e.g., token frequency, burstiness, perplexity) that are easily disrupted by paraphrasing and confounded by linguistic diversity. As one researcher noted in the discussion, "These tools don’t detect AI—they detect how closely text matches the narrow distribution of training-time human corpora." Until detectors move beyond shallow heuristics toward semantic or behavioral modeling, they remain unfit for high-stakes use in education, publishing, or legal review.
