Mistral changes opt-out settings for model training without clear notice

News time: 2026-09-03T05:45:02.385082+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A growing number of enterprise users are expressing frustration after Mistral AI quietly altered its data usage controls—specifically, the ability to disable training on user inputs and outputs across organizational tiers. What began as a key differentiator for European customers seeking privacy-conscious AI providers has eroded: organizations that upgraded to Mistral’s Team tier expecting centralized, enforceable opt-outs discovered those controls had been removed or downgraded, with no public announcement or migration path. The official help page still states users retain "full control" and can opt out "at any time," but real-world configuration options in the dashboard no longer reflect that promise.

This isn’t just a UI glitch—it reflects a broader tension in the LLM industry. As companies like Mistral scale, their data policies often shift faster than documentation or contractual commitments can keep up. Users report that opt-out toggles disappeared mid-subscription, and default settings flipped from opt-out to opt-in without consent or even email notification. One enterprise engineer described switching to Mistral precisely to avoid the kind of 'rug pull' they’d experienced with GitHub Copilot—only to encounter a similar pattern months later.

The legal framing adds another layer of uncertainty. Mistral’s privacy policy distinguishes between "Input," "Output," and "Usage Data," but doesn’t clarify whether anonymized or aggregated logs used for model improvement fall under the same opt-out scope. Meanwhile, industry-wide claims of "fair use" for scraping user prompts—even when explicitly opted out—have deepened skepticism. As one Hacker News commenter bluntly put it: "The idea that they’ll steal from everyone except you is just wishful thinking."

For enterprises building production AI systems, this volatility undermines core governance requirements: auditability, data sovereignty, and contractual enforceability. Without immutable, organization-wide controls—and without transparency about how and when data flows into training pipelines—privacy assurances risk becoming marketing language rather than technical guarantees. The conversation isn’t about whether opt-outs should exist; it’s about whether they can be meaningfully implemented, monitored, and trusted in practice.
