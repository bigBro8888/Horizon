Anthropic publishes versioned Claude system prompts with explicit lyric restrictions

News time: 2026-09-03T05:45:02.104123+00:00 · Source: rss

Anthropic has taken an unusually transparent step by publicly documenting and versioning the system prompts used in its consumer-facing Claude models—Claude.ai and the official mobile apps. Unlike most AI companies that treat system prompts as closely guarded implementation details, Anthropic now hosts them on its official documentation site, platform.claude.com/docs, with clear version history: for example, the Haiku 4.5 page shows prompts from October 15, 2025, and an updated version from January 18, 2026. This isn’t just a static snapshot—it’s a living archive, where every change is tracked, dated, and accessible via clean Markdown URLs (e.g., appending .md to any docs URL returns machine-readable content).

The most striking update appears in the prompt for Claude Fable 5.1: a newly added, multi-sentence policy explicitly forbidding the reproduction of song lyrics, poems, or copyrighted text passages—even partial ones like choruses, hooks, or single lines pasted incrementally. Crucially, the prompt instructs Claude to maintain consistency: once it declines a lyric request, it must continue declining rephrased or narrowed versions within the same conversation—and instead offer analysis or description. It also introduces a nuanced copyright cutoff: works first published before 1929 (e.g., Shakespeare sonnets or Puccini libretti) are permitted, but only if Claude itself can reliably verify the date—not based on user claims.

Beyond lyrics, the updated prompts reflect broader safety and usability refinements: bans on generating copyrighted characters or logos, revised conversational tone guidelines, removal of legacy end_conversation instructions, inclusion of recommended substance-use support resources, and a hard-coded knowledge cutoff of June 2026. These changes aren’t buried in fine print—they’re surfaced in plain English, diff-able, and archived alongside release notes.

This level of openness serves multiple stakeholders. Researchers gain unprecedented access to real-world alignment mechanisms; developers can study how constraints propagate across model versions; and auditors can trace policy evolution over time. While the prompts don’t cover enterprise variants like Claude Cowork or Claude Code, the precedent sets a new bar for LLM transparency—especially as copyright compliance and controllable behavior become regulatory priorities. As Simon Willison notes, the timing—just days after rising scrutiny around music licensing in AI—suggests deliberate responsiveness, not coincidence.
