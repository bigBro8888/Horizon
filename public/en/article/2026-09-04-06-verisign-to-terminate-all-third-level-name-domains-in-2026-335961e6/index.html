Verisign to terminate all third-level .name domains in 2026

News time: 2026-09-04T05:50:51.986002+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Verisign, the registry operator for the .name top-level domain, has announced plans to terminate all third-level registrations—such as alice.bob.name or dev.example.name—effective September 2026. Under this policy, existing third-level domains will be deleted, and their corresponding second-level domains (e.g., bob.name or example.name) will be released into the open market for new registration. The move follows Verisign’s review of the .name TLD’s usage patterns and operational sustainability, though no formal public consultation or ICANN-approved policy change process was cited in the announcement.

The .name TLD launched in 2001 as one of the first ICANN-accredited generic top-level domains, originally intended to support personal naming—like firstname.lastname.name for websites and email. Unlike most TLDs, .name permits two-tier delegation: registrants can register at the second level (e.g., jane.name), or at the third level under a second-level domain owned by someone else (e.g., blog.jane.name). This structure, while flexible, created complexity in ownership, renewal, and abuse mitigation—issues Verisign now cites as justification for simplification.

Critics argue the termination undermines stability and security—the core pillars of ICANN’s mission. As one commenter noted, abruptly deleting active domains contradicts ICANN’s mandate to ensure ‘the stable, secure operation of the Internet’s unique identifier systems.’ Others point out that releasing previously reserved second-level domains without grace periods invites squatting and brand confusion. Still, clarifications from community members emphasize that second-level domains (e.g., dvt.name) remain fully intact and unaffected—only third-level subdomains vanish.

For users relying on third-level .name names—especially those embedded in legacy applications, documentation, or IoT device identifiers—the change poses real continuity risks. One engineer recalled designing an IoT identity system specifically to avoid domain dependency, citing exactly this kind of fragility: ‘Domain names are leased. Things that are leased can disappear.’ While Verisign has not committed to reserving released second-level domains or offering sunset protections, affected registrants have until mid-2026 to migrate or upgrade to second-level registration if available. The episode underscores how infrastructure decisions made by registry operators—even in niche TLDs—can ripple across technical ecosystems far beyond DNS itself.
