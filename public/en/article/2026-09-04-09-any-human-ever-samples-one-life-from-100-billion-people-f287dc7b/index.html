Any Human Ever samples one life from 100 billion people

News time: 2026-09-04T05:50:51.986145+00:00 · Source: hackernews

What would it be like to live as a randomly selected human among all who have ever lived? That’s the question behind Any Human Ever, an interactive web project that draws and displays a single biographical profile—complete with birth year, location, estimated income, marriage age, mortality risk, and historical context—from the estimated 108 billion humans who have existed since the emergence of Homo sapiens. Each profile is generated algorithmically using demographic models, historical estimates, and cited scholarly sources, aiming to reflect real-world distributions of population growth, life expectancy, and socioeconomic conditions across time and place.

The site leans into exponential population growth: because over 90% of all humans who ever lived were born after 1000 CE—and more than half after 1900—the random draw is heavily weighted toward recent centuries. A user might land on a subsistence farmer in 12th-century China, a textile worker in 19th-century Manchester, or a software engineer in present-day Bangalore. Each result includes footnotes linking to academic references (though some links are broken or misattributed), and attempts to contextualize daily realities—like food access, gender roles, or disease burden—within broader historical trends.

Yet the project has sparked vigorous technical debate. Commenters on Hacker News pointed out internal inconsistencies—for instance, claiming 96% marriage rates and 44% childhood mortality for women in 8th-century China, which mathematically undermines plausible family formation patterns. Others criticized the use of modern purchasing-power-parity (PPP) income conversions for pre-monetary economies, noting that comparing a medieval peasant’s grain yield to $3.04/day distorts economic meaning. Still others observed sampling bias: despite the stated principle that “a random birth is far more likely to fall near the present,” several users reported disproportionately few modern-era draws in small samples—suggesting the underlying probability distribution may not be correctly implemented.

Still, these critiques don’t diminish the project’s conceptual power. Any Human Ever succeeds not as a definitive demographic tool, but as a pedagogical and philosophical provocation: it forces users to confront scale, inequality, and contingency in human history. By making abstraction visceral—turning billions into one named, situated life—it invites reflection on whose stories get told, how data shapes narrative, and what ‘representativeness’ really means when modeling deep time.
