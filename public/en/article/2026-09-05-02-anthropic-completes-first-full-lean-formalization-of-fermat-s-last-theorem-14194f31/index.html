Anthropic completes first full Lean formalization of Fermat’s Last Theorem

News time: 2026-09-05T05:42:58.807914+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Anthropic has announced the first complete, machine-checked proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem (FLT) in the Lean 4 theorem prover — a landmark achievement in formal mathematics. The proof, developed over 11 days with substantial autonomous assistance from Claude AI, verifies Andrew Wiles’s 1995 argument as adapted by Darmon, Diamond, and Taylor. Unlike earlier partial efforts or proofs restricted to special cases, this is a fully mechanized, line-by-line verification covering all major components: Ribet’s level-lowering theorem, the Langlands–Tunnell theorem, Fontaine theory for flat deformations of Galois representations, and Mazur’s work on the Eisenstein ideal — culminating in the impossibility of a Frey curve with a point of order p > 13.

This effort builds on Mathlib, Lean’s extensive mathematical library (v4.33.0), and uses a carefully pinned dependency stack to ensure reproducibility. The repository includes PROOF-PATH.md — a human-readable roadmap linking each high-level mathematical step to its corresponding Lean theorem — and an HTML-rendered version of the entire proof that can be browsed offline. Crucially, the formalization does not rely on the more recent modular approaches pursued by Kevin Buzzard and others using Khare–Taylor ideas; instead, it faithfully follows the original 1995 exposition, making it both historically grounded and technically self-contained.

The scale is staggering: over 13 million lines of Lean code underpin the project — though most are from Mathlib and supporting infrastructure, not newly written logic. Still, the core proof contributes thousands of original lines, rigorously encoding decades of deep number theory. As one commenter noted, the speed of development signals a turning point: large swaths of advanced mathematics may now be formalized in weeks rather than decades. That opens real possibilities — not just for catching subtle errors in published proofs, but also for transforming how new mathematics is peer-reviewed and taught.

Yet the milestone raises profound questions about trust. A formal proof is only as sound as its foundations: the Lean kernel, the correctness of Mathlib’s axioms, and the absence of bugs in the toolchain itself. As another user pointed out, verifying 13 million lines sounds impressive — but what guarantees those lines are error-free? The answer lies in Lean’s design: its small trusted kernel checks every inference, and Mathlib’s community-driven, incremental verification process provides layered confidence. Still, this work doesn’t replace human insight — it augments it, turning intuition into irrefutable computation.
