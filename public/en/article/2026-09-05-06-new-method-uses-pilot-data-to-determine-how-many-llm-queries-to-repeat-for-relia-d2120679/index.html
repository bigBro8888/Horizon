New method uses pilot data to determine how many LLM queries to repeat for reliable auditing

News time: 2026-09-05T05:42:59.260280+00:00 · Source: reddit

When auditing large language models—especially for sensitive tasks like brand recommendations—researchers often run the same prompt multiple times to account for output variability. But how many repetitions are enough? A new preprint by the founder of Rankfor.AI proposes a statistically principled answer: apply generalizability theory (G-theory) to pilot data to estimate variance components, then compute the minimum number of repeats needed to achieve a target reliability coefficient (e.g., G-coefficient ≥ 0.9). This shifts the decision from heuristic guesswork to quantifiable design.

The protocol was tested not on the original brand-recommendation use case, but on three independently collected external corpora—including political-orientation questionnaires and benchmark stability datasets—totaling 39 distinct prediction cells. Remarkably, 37 cells fully met the prespecified replication criterion (i.e., observed reliability matched predicted reliability within tolerance), and two were partial matches. This high replication success rate suggests the statistical machinery is robust across domains—even when the task differs from the original application.

However, the study also reports important failures. Fixed iteration thresholds (e.g., “always repeat 5 times”) did not transfer across settings. Some preregistered drift diagnostics also failed, underscoring that reliability isn’t guaranteed by repetition alone—it depends on context, model, and prompt structure. The author openly acknowledges a key limitation: no external validation yet exists on actual repeated brand-recommendation data, since none of the three external corpora involved commercial brand prompts. That gap remains open.

The paper is preregistered, transparent about both successes and failures, and releases all validation materials publicly on GitHub. It invites scrutiny—not just of the conclusions, but of the core assumptions: How stable are pilot-based variance estimates when prompts or models change? Is G-theory’s assumption of crossed random effects realistic for real-world LLM querying? These aren’t rhetorical questions; they’re explicit calls for community engagement, especially from researchers with access to independently collected, repeated-brand-query datasets.
