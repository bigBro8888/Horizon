Simon Willison benchmarks GPT-6 Astra against GPT-5.6 models using pelican SVGs

News time: 2026-09-05T05:42:58.354175+00:00 · Source: rss

When Simon Willison gained early access to OpenAI’s newly launched GPT-6 Astra on September 4, 2026, he didn’t reach for a standard benchmark — he asked it to generate SVG images of pelicans riding bicycles. Not as a joke, but as a deliberate, repeatable, and visually interpretable test across five reasoning levels: low, medium, high, xhigh, and max. He then rendered those outputs alongside equivalent generations from GPT-5.6’s three-tiered lineup — Sol (deep reasoning), Terra (balanced), and Luna (cost-optimized) — into a side-by-side comparison grid. The result is both whimsical and technically revealing: each pelican’s anatomical coherence, leg placement, frame alignment, and stylistic consistency serve as proxies for model fidelity, multimodal grounding, and reasoning stability.

The grid shows clear qualitative leaps. Even GPT-6 Astra’s lowest reasoning setting produces a more recognizable, structurally sound pelican than any GPT-5.6 Sol output — despite Sol being the most capable variant in the GPT-5.6 family. At higher reasoning levels, Astra’s pelicans become strikingly coherent: legs appear correctly on both sides of the bicycle frame, beaks and wings retain proportion, and SVG code remains clean and valid. Yet a subtle limitation persists — even at xhigh, Astra still occasionally misplaces one leg, suggesting residual fragility in spatial composition tasks.

Token efficiency emerges as another key differentiator. While Astra’s API pricing is roughly double that of GPT-5.6 Sol ($10/$50 per million input/output tokens vs. $5/$30), its input token usage is significantly lower: just 16 tokens for the prompt, compared to 26 for Sol and Terra. That compression helps narrow the effective cost gap across reasoning levels — especially at low and medium settings, where Astra delivers superior visual quality for under 10 cents. This hints at architectural improvements in prompt encoding or internal representation, not just raw scale.

Willison also notes an intriguing similarity: both Astra and Luna used exactly 16 input tokens, while Sol and Terra used 26. Given Luna’s role as OpenAI’s lightweight, high-throughput model, this shared token count raises questions about possible shared components or inference optimizations between Astra and Luna — a detail OpenAI hasn’t publicly confirmed. Ultimately, this pelican benchmark isn’t about birds; it’s a clever, low-overhead stress test for how well models translate abstract instructions into structured, compositional outputs — a capability increasingly vital for code generation, UI prototyping, and multimodal tool use.
