Chrome preserves Google site data despite user privacy settings

News time: 2026-09-06T05:53:56.050411+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A recent technical investigation reveals that Google Chrome selectively retains site data—including cookies, local storage, and cache—for Google-owned domains (e.g., google.com, youtube.com, gmail.com), even when users explicitly configure Chrome to block or clear such data for third-party sites. The test involved enabling strict settings like 'Block third-party cookies' and using Chrome’s 'Clear browsing data' tool with options set to delete cookies and site data for all sites except those the user manually whitelists. Despite this, Google domains consistently retained their data—while non-Google sites lost theirs as expected.

This behavior is not new: similar findings surfaced in 2021 and 2023, prompting renewed scrutiny. Unlike typical third-party contexts, Google services often operate within Chrome’s first-party storage partitioning framework—even when embedded via iframes or loaded alongside other sites. Chrome’s storage partitioning, introduced under the Privacy Sandbox initiative, isolates third-party storage by default but treats same-site origins (including Google’s many subdomains) as unified first-party contexts. Crucially, Chrome also integrates tightly with Google Account sign-in states, which may trigger automatic exceptions to preserve login sessions across Google properties.

Critics argue this undermines user agency: if a setting says 'clear all cookies', users reasonably expect it to apply uniformly—not just to 'others'. The inconsistency blurs the line between browser functionality and platform self-preferencing. While Chrome offers granular per-site controls (e.g., clicking the lock icon → Site Settings → Clear data), those require manual intervention and are rarely used by average users. Moreover, the exemption isn’t documented in Chrome’s official privacy controls UI or help pages—making it invisible to most.

The issue sits at the intersection of privacy, antitrust, and web standards. Regulators in the EU and U.S. have previously cited such differential treatment as evidence of anti-competitive conduct. Meanwhile, developers point out that Chrome’s own storage partitioning documentation acknowledges the risk of over-permissive first-party treatment—but stops short of addressing how Google domains benefit from that design in practice. For users seeking consistent data control, the takeaway is clear: Chrome’s privacy settings don’t apply equally—and Google services remain functionally exempt by architecture, not accident.
