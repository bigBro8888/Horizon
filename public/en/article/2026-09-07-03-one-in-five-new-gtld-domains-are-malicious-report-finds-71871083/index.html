One in five new gTLD domains are malicious, report finds

News time: 2026-09-07T06:04:01.051131+00:00 · Source: rss

A stark new analysis from Interisle Consulting Group reveals that roughly 20% of all newly registered generic top-level domains (gTLDs) in 2025 were used for scams and cybercrime — a figure Terence Eden calls 'a bloody crisis.' The report, published in June 2026, examined 85 million gTLD registrations made last year and found that 8.5 million had already been added to global blocklists by May 2025. While even a 10% abuse rate would be alarming, Interisle’s conservative modeling points to 16.8 million malicious domains — precisely 20% of the total — purchased deliberately by bad actors.

This isn’t just about phishing links or fake login pages. These domains serve as infrastructure for malware distribution, botnet command-and-control servers, and large-scale fraud operations. The scale suggests systemic failure: domain registration remains cheap, fast, and largely anonymous, with minimal real-time vetting. Unlike traditional gatekeepers like banks or telecom providers, domain registrars face no universal liability for downstream harm — and enforcement mechanisms remain fragmented across jurisdictions and policies.

ICANN has formally recognized DNS abuse since 2024, embedding mitigation requirements into its Registry Agreement and Registrar Accreditation Agreement. Yet compliance is uneven, and definitions of abuse — limited to malware, phishing, pharming, botnets, and spam-as-delivery — exclude many borderline but harmful uses, such as impersonation sites or scam landing pages that avoid technical red flags. Meanwhile, reputation blocklists (RBLs), which power much of today’s detection, vary widely in coverage and timeliness — meaning abuse metrics depend heavily on which lists you consult.

The implications go beyond cybersecurity professionals. Every time someone clicks a link in an email, social media post, or search result, they’re trusting the DNS layer — a system built for convenience, not accountability. As Eden bluntly puts it, the DNS no longer feels like neutral infrastructure; it increasingly functions as a delivery mechanism for crime. Without coordinated action — tighter registration controls, shared threat intelligence, and updated accountability frameworks — the internet’s foundational naming system risks becoming synonymous with deception.
