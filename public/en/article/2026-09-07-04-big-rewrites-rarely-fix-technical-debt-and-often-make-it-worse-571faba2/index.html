Big rewrites rarely fix technical debt and often make it worse

News time: 2026-09-07T06:04:01.051160+00:00 · Source: rss

When a legacy codebase becomes painfully unwieldy, the instinctive reaction among engineers and managers is often to scrap it entirely and start over. It feels clean, decisive, and full of promise — like hitting a reset button on years of accumulated compromises. But as Simon Willison argues in his widely cited Lobsters comment, this ‘burn it down’ approach almost never delivers on its promise.

The problem isn’t just technical. It’s deeply human and organizational. As soon as a rewrite is announced, the old system doesn’t freeze in time — it keeps running the business. New features, regulatory changes, and urgent bug fixes still land there. Yet the team maintaining it loses motivation: why invest in thoughtful design or test coverage when their work is officially ‘temporary’? Meanwhile, the new team starts strong on greenfield terrain — but quickly hits reality. Without complete documentation or comprehensive tests, they’re reverse-engineering behavior that evolved organically over years. Subtle edge cases, undocumented integrations, and implicit business rules surface only in production — too late for elegant architecture.

Months pass. Pressure mounts. Instead of launching a full replacement, leadership demands something shipped — so the new system goes live handling only a fraction of the original scope, often just one shiny new feature the old stack couldn’t support. The result? Two parallel systems: one brittle and neglected, the other underused and bloated with untested, inactive code meant to ‘eventually replace’ the first. And if timelines slip or strategy shifts, the rewrite gets quietly shelved — leaving twice the maintenance burden and zero progress on debt reduction.

Willison points to Will Larson’s essay ‘Migrations’ as the antidote: incremental, reversible, test-backed migrations that replace parts of the legacy system piece by piece — not all at once, but with measurable progress and continuous value delivery. His recommendation is pragmatic: double down on automated testing for the old system, then surgically refactor high-impact modules. It’s less glamorous than a greenfield launch, but far more likely to succeed.
