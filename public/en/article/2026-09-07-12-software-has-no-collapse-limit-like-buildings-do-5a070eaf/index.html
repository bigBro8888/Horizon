Software has no collapse limit like buildings do

News time: 2026-09-07T06:04:01.051184+00:00 · Source: rss

Zach Kehs’ short but incisive observation cuts to the heart of a daily reality for software engineers: unlike physical structures, software doesn’t fail catastrophically when pushed too far — it just keeps getting worse. A building overloaded with floors and rooms will eventually collapse under its own weight — a clear, physical boundary enforced by gravity and material science. Software faces no such natural law. There’s no hard ceiling on how many layers of abstraction, indirection, or inefficient abstractions can be piled on top of legacy systems. Performance degrades incrementally, readability erodes silently, and coupling tightens imperceptibly — all without triggering an immediate failure.

This isn’t theoretical. Every large-scale application carries decades of accumulated decisions: wrappers around wrappers, configuration-driven logic that obscures control flow, microservices calling microservices across network hops for trivial operations, and ORM-generated SQL that scans entire tables instead of using indexes. Each change may seem justified in isolation — a quick fix, a rushed integration, a temporary workaround — but collectively they compound into what’s known as software entropy: the gradual, inevitable decline in internal quality despite outward functionality remaining intact.

Kehs’ metaphor resonates because it reframes technical debt not as a budgetary line item or a sprint backlog task, but as a structural inevitability — one that accelerates silently. Unlike financial debt, which accrues interest at a known rate, software decay compounds unpredictably: a poorly named variable today may mislead three developers next quarter; a missing test suite may allow a subtle regression to slip into production years later; an over-engineered interface may block a critical feature for months. The cost isn’t always visible until it’s too late.

What makes this insight especially valuable is its diagnostic power. When teams ask, “Why does everything take so long now?” or “Why does every small change break something else?”, Kehs’ quote offers a grounding answer: it’s not incompetence or bad luck — it’s the natural trajectory of unmanaged complexity. Recognizing that software can always get worse is the first step toward intentional countermeasures: ruthless refactoring, strict architectural guardrails, observability-first development, and treating code health as non-negotiable infrastructure — not optional polish.
