Researcher factors 1990s RSA-512 CA key in two days on consumer GPU

News time: 2026-09-08T05:55:48.879877+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A security researcher has successfully factored the 512-bit RSA public key of a 1990s Certificate Authority—using only modern consumer-grade GPU hardware and completing the computation in about two days. The target was not a theoretical exercise: it was the actual root certificate used by a real-world SSL/TLS deployment from the late 1990s, specifically tied to Netscape Communicator 4.51. By recovering the private key, the researcher demonstrated that encrypted traffic from that era—especially connections lacking forward secrecy—could now be decrypted retroactively.

This achievement underscores a long-standing concern in cryptography: 'harvest now, decrypt later.' In the 1990s, 512-bit RSA keys were widely deployed despite known theoretical weaknesses. Export restrictions limited many browsers (like Netscape’s 40-bit 'export-grade' builds) to weak cipher suites, and SSLv3—the dominant protocol at the time—did not support ephemeral key exchange. As a result, much historical web traffic was encrypted with static keys, meaning that once the CA’s private key is recovered, all past sessions using its certificates become vulnerable.

The technical approach combined classical number-theoretic methods with modern acceleration. The researcher used the General Number Field Sieve (GNFS), implemented via open-source tools like CADO-NFS, optimized for NVIDIA GPUs. Unlike brute-force attacks, GNFS scales sub-exponentially with key size—making 512-bit keys tractable today but utterly infeasible in the 1990s. Notably, the researcher built a custom TLS stack in Go to interface with the legacy client, since standard libraries (e.g., Go’s crypto/tls) dropped SSLv3 support years ago.

Beyond technical proof, the experiment raises sobering questions about archival surveillance. Governments or well-resourced actors who recorded bulk internet traffic in the 1990s could now decrypt it—if they retained the ciphertext and knew which certificates were in use. It also serves as a stark reminder that cryptographic obsolescence isn’t just theoretical: keys once deemed secure can become trivial to break within decades. As post-quantum migration gains urgency, this work illustrates why long-term data confidentiality requires more than just today’s best practices—it demands proactive key rotation, forward secrecy, and awareness of historical deployment flaws.
