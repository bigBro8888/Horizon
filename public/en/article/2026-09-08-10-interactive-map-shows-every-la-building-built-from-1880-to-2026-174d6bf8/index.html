Interactive map shows every LA building built from 1880 to 2026

News time: 2026-09-08T05:55:48.880000+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new interactive time-lapse map lets users watch Los Angeles grow — building by building — from 1880 through projected construction in 2026. Hosted at lax-skyline.parcelscope.net, the tool renders over 1.3 million structures using publicly available parcel and assessor data, assigning each a construction year based on county records. As the timeline advances, neighborhoods bloom, downtown rises and falls with demolition cycles, and vast swaths of the San Fernando Valley or South LA appear nearly static for decades — revealing not just growth, but deliberate pauses shaped by policy.

The visualization is not a simulation or artistic reconstruction; it’s grounded in real administrative data — primarily from the Los Angeles County Assessor’s portal — though that introduces important caveats. Many early buildings no longer exist, so what appears as ‘1890s construction’ in a neighborhood like Palms often reflects the age of today’s replacement structures, not original ones. Similarly, dense historic districts such as Downtown or Boyle Heights show surprising gaps because few pre-1920 buildings survived redevelopment waves.

What emerges is a spatial history of constraint: long periods of near-zero construction after the 1980s downzoning, sharp spikes tied to transit-oriented development incentives (e.g., near Expo Line stations), and stark disparities between high-rise corridors and low-density residential zones locked in by single-family zoning. Users can pause, zoom, filter by decade or building type, and even compare timelines across neighborhoods — making it a rare public-facing tool that turns abstract housing policy into visible, visceral change.

Critics rightly note limitations — including data lag (some 2024–2026 projects are speculative permits, not shovels-in-ground), inconsistent recordkeeping across municipalities, and no distinction between new builds, major renovations, or adaptive reuse. Yet precisely because it foregrounds these gaps, the map invites deeper questions: Why did LA stop building where it once built densely? How much of today’s housing shortage stems from decisions made in city halls — not geography or geology? For planners, journalists, and residents alike, it transforms decades of bureaucratic records into something legible, debatable, and urgent.
