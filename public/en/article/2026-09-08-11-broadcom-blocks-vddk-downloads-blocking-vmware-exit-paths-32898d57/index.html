Broadcom blocks VDDK downloads, blocking VMware exit paths

News time: 2026-09-08T05:55:48.880030+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Enterprises planning to migrate away from VMware vSphere now face a major technical roadblock: Broadcom has removed public access to the VMware Virtual Disk Development Kit (VDDK) — a critical SDK used by backup, replication, and migration tools to read and copy virtual disks directly from ESXi hosts. Without VDDK, many third-party migration utilities — including those used in large-scale VMware-to-Hyper-V, VMware-to-KVM, or VMware-to-Proxmox projects — cannot reliably access VM disk data at the hypervisor level. This change, confirmed by Broadcom’s official developer portal and widely reported across infrastructure blogs, took effect in mid-2026 and applies to all versions of VDDK, including legacy releases.

The impact is immediate and operational. Migration teams that had already begun VMware exit strategies — especially those relying on automated tooling for lift-and-shift workloads — are now forced to pause, re-evaluate, or pivot to manual, slower, and riskier methods like exporting OVF/OVA files or using guest-level agents. While some vendors claim partial workarounds exist (e.g., leveraging VMware’s newer REST APIs or PowerCLI), these lack the low-level disk access, performance, and consistency guarantees that VDDK provided for block-level operations.

This move intensifies long-simmering concerns about vendor lock-in under Broadcom’s ownership. Since acquiring VMware in late 2023, Broadcom has steadily reduced developer-facing resources: deprecating open-source integrations, sunsetting community forums, and tightening licensing terms. The VDDK removal isn’t just a technical inconvenience — it signals a strategic shift toward making VMware less interoperable by design. As one ex-VMware engineer noted on Hacker News, ‘Broadcom sees VMware not as a platform to evolve, but as a cashflow engine to extract from.’

Still, alternatives are gaining traction. Proxmox VE users report surprisingly smooth migrations via direct datastore mounting — bypassing VDDK entirely — while KVM adopters emphasize building internal tooling around libvirt and QEMU. Microsoft Hyper-V shops face steeper challenges due to fragmented tooling, but are doubling down on Azure Stack HCI as a hybrid bridge. Ultimately, the VDDK cutoff doesn’t halt migration — it raises its cost, time, and skill requirements. For infrastructure teams, the message is clear: escaping VMware now demands deeper engineering investment, not just procurement decisions.
