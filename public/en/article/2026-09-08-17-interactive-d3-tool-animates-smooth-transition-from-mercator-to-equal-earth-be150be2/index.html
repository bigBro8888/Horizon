Interactive D3 tool animates smooth transition from Mercator to Equal Earth

News time: 2026-09-08T05:55:48.592681+00:00 · Source: rss

A new web-based visualization tool lets users watch — and control — a real-time morphing animation between two fundamentally different world map projections: the familiar Mercator and the newer Equal Earth. Created by developer Simon Willison and powered by D3.js, the tool is not just a static comparison but a fluid, parameterized interpolation that visually reveals how landmasses shift, stretch, and reorient as the underlying mathematical projection changes.

The timing is notable: the tool was built shortly after the United Nations General Assembly passed a resolution in September 2026 encouraging global institutions to adopt equal-area projections — with explicit mention of Equal Earth. While the resolution isn’t binding, it signals growing institutional recognition of cartographic bias, especially the well-documented distortion of land area in the Mercator projection (e.g., Greenland appearing roughly the same size as Africa, though it’s actually only 1/14th the area).

Willison describes the project as ‘vibe-coding’ — a rapid, curiosity-driven build assisted by GPT-6 Astra, which generated much of the D3 projection logic and interpolation scaffolding. The result is lightweight, open, and educational: users can drag a slider to pause or reverse the transition, observe graticule behavior, and compare how coastlines, continents, and polar regions respond differently under each projection’s geometric constraints.

Technically, the animation doesn’t simply fade or warp images — it recomputes every point on the globe using interpolated projection equations, blending Mercator’s conformal (angle-preserving) properties with Equal Earth’s strict equal-area guarantee. This requires careful handling of coordinate transformations and inverse projections, especially near poles and datelines. The implementation avoids heavy dependencies, runs entirely in-browser, and is publicly viewable and inspectable at tools.simonwillison.net/equal-earth.

For educators, journalists, and geospatial developers, the tool offers more than novelty: it makes abstract cartographic trade-offs tangible. Seeing Russia shrink and Africa expand during the transition underscores why projection choice matters — not just for navigation or aesthetics, but for perception, policy, and equity. As more governments and schools consider adopting Equal Earth, tools like this help bridge the gap between technical specification and public understanding.
