Terence Tao warns AI is depleting math’s intellectual ecosystem

News time: 2026-09-09T05:57:31.956208+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a striking metaphor published on Mathstodon, Fields Medalist Terence Tao compares the current wave of AI-driven solutions to open mathematical problems to 'non-renewable mining' — extracting answers without replenishing the human processes that make mathematics thrive. He argues that while AI can brute-force proofs or verify conjectures with increasing speed, it risks bypassing the slow, collaborative, and pedagogically rich work of formulating meaningful questions, building intuition, sharing partial insights, and teaching others how to think mathematically.

Tao’s concern goes beyond correctness or efficiency. It targets the ecosystem of mathematics: the informal networks of seminars, student supervision, shared notebooks, and incremental dialogue that incubate deep understanding. When AI delivers a final answer in isolation — say, a proof of a long-standing conjecture — it may skip over the scaffolding that helps new generations learn why the result matters, how it connects to other ideas, and what questions it opens next.

This isn’t a rejection of AI as a tool. Tao has long engaged constructively with computational methods — from his work on the Navier-Stokes equations to collaborative polymath projects. But he stresses that AI should augment, not replace, the human practices that sustain mathematical culture over decades and centuries. As one commenter noted, echoing Isaac Asimov’s 1956 story 'Jokester', the hardest part of using any super-intelligence isn’t answering questions — it’s asking the right ones.

The discussion on Hacker News reflects unusual depth: commenters draw historical parallels — Gauss’s competitiveness, Newton–Leibniz priority disputes, even Pythagorean secrecy — to argue that mathematics has always involved tension between competition and collaboration. Yet Tao’s point remains distinct: those tensions historically occurred within a shared, evolving framework of meaning-making. AI, by contrast, operates outside that framework unless deliberately embedded in it. The risk isn’t that AI will get math wrong — but that it will get it too right, too fast, and too alone.
