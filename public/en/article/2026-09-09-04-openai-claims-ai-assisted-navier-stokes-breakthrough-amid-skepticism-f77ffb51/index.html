OpenAI claims AI-assisted Navier–Stokes breakthrough amid skepticism

News time: 2026-09-09T05:57:31.956259+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In early September 2026, OpenAI announced it had used an internal AI model—trained for less than two weeks—to produce a claimed solution to the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems. The announcement stated that the model proved the breakdown of smooth solutions in three-dimensional space—a negative resolution meaning no globally smooth solution exists for all initial conditions. The result was formalized in the Lean proof assistant and submitted for review, though neither the Clay Institute nor any independent mathematical body has verified it.

The claim immediately ignited intense scrutiny. Experts noted that the work appears to build on recent advances by mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa (2023), who demonstrated blowup in related fluid equations, and also draws from analyses of the Euler equations by Levent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster. OpenAI’s announcement included no co-authorship or formal attribution to these researchers—prompting concerns about academic credit and research ethics. A statement from Buckmaster, hosted at cims.nyu.edu, clarified that his unpublished work was shared informally and later appeared in OpenAI’s technical narrative without consent or citation.

Terence Tao, a Fields Medalist and leading expert on fluid PDEs, responded publicly on Mathstodon, warning that AI systems may now “flatten” promising research directions before human-led projects mature—distorting incentives toward secrecy rather than open collaboration. Others highlighted deeper tensions: while AI excels at symbolic manipulation and formal verification, mathematics relies on conceptual insight, intuition, and community validation over decades—not just correctness in a proof assistant. As one commenter observed, even if the Lean formalization checks out, it does not automatically confer mathematical understanding or acceptance.

Beyond the technical dispute lies a broader question: what counts as a solution in foundational mathematics? The Clay Institute requires not only correctness but also clarity, reproducibility, and integration into the mathematical canon. OpenAI said it would decline the $1 million prize if awarded—framing the effort as a demonstration of AI’s emerging role in discovery. Yet without transparent methodology, full code release, or peer-reviewed exposition, many mathematicians remain unconvinced. For now, the Navier–Stokes problem remains officially unsolved—and the episode stands as a landmark case study in AI’s fraught entry into high-stakes theoretical science.
