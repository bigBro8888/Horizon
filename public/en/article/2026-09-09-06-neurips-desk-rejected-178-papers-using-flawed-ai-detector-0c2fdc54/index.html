NeurIPS desk-rejected 178 papers using flawed AI detector

News time: 2026-09-09T05:57:31.928459+00:00 · Source: reddit

NeurIPS 2026’s Position Paper Track made headlines—not for groundbreaking research, but for its controversial use of the Pangram AI detector to desk-reject 178 submissions without human review or appeal. The decision relied entirely on automated scores: papers flagged above a certain threshold were rejected outright, even though the same detector assigned alarmingly high AI probability scores—between 24% and 69%—to recent papers authored by the track chairs themselves. This self-incriminating result exposed a profound irony: the enforcement mechanism failed its own architects.

The technical rollout was equally troubling. Pangram’s default settings initially labeled 42.7% of all submissions as 90–100% AI-generated—a rate so implausible that organizers manually adjusted detection parameters, including shrinking text window sizes, to bring the flag rate down to 18.4%. No public documentation justified these ad-hoc changes, nor did NeurIPS release calibration data across language backgrounds. A Stanford study cited in the discussion found that 61.22% of human-written TOEFL essays—by non-native English speakers—were falsely flagged, raising serious equity concerns for ESL researchers.

Worse still, the process entered what critics dubbed the 'Circularity Trap': 22 papers were rejected not because of evidence of AI use, but because authors checked a box denying AI assistance while receiving a Pangram score above 0.5. In effect, the black-box score became proof of dishonesty—despite no independent verification. There was no recourse: no appeals, no transparency about thresholds, and no opportunity to contest results. Privacy questions also surfaced, as Pangram’s terms reportedly allow retention and analysis of submitted manuscripts.

The backlash has sparked urgent debate across machine learning policy circles. Many argue that deploying unvalidated, opaque detectors at scale undermines academic integrity more than AI writing ever could—especially when false positives disproportionately impact marginalized scholars. As one commentator noted, the real failure wasn’t AI generation; it was the abdication of human judgment in peer review. For affected authors, the path forward remains pragmatic: resubmit elsewhere—ICLR’s deadline is September 25, and ICML remains open.
