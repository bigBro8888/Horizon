Qwen 3.8 shows suspicious reasoning alignment with GPT-5.5 Pro outputs

News time: 2026-09-10T05:56:16.628203+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A recent technical analysis has raised concerns about the provenance of Qwen 3.8’s reasoning behavior. Researchers observed unusually high token-level similarity between Qwen 3.8’s chain-of-thought (CoT) traces and publicly recovered CoT outputs from GPT-5.5 Pro — a model that does not officially exist but is widely assumed to refer to an internal, unreleased OpenAI system. The comparison relied on ‘stolen thoughts’, a technique detailed in a paper from stolen-thoughts.com that extracts human-readable reasoning steps from proprietary LLMs by exploiting subtle output patterns and statistical anomalies in token probabilities.

The method works by first running benchmark questions through a strong proprietary model, recovering its step-by-step rationales using post-hoc analysis, then using only the first 1% of each recovered rationale as a ‘prefill’ prompt for an open-weight model like Qwen 3.8. When Qwen 3.8 continued those prefills with near-identical reasoning steps — especially on math and logic benchmarks — it triggered suspicion of distillation. Crucially, Qwen 3.8 (version 0902) was released after August 10, 2024 — the date the ‘stolen thoughts’ paper became publicly available — meaning those recovered CoT traces were theoretically accessible during its final training or fine-tuning phase.

This is not traditional knowledge distillation, which usually involves logits or hidden states. Instead, it suggests a potentially lightweight but ethically fraught shortcut: using publicly scraped, high-quality reasoning traces as synthetic supervision. If confirmed, it would mean Qwen 3.8 may have been fine-tuned — intentionally or inadvertently — on outputs derived from a closed, proprietary model without consent or licensing. That blurs legal boundaries around training data provenance and challenges assumptions about the independence of open-weight models.

The broader implications extend beyond Qwen. As more labs adopt similar ‘reasoning trace bootstrapping’ techniques — whether for efficiency, capability gains, or benchmark competitiveness — the integrity of model attribution erodes. Without robust provenance tracking tools like DebugLM or watermark-aware training pipelines, distinguishing original reasoning from imitation becomes increasingly difficult. For developers relying on open models for safety-critical applications, this raises urgent questions about transparency, auditability, and trustworthiness.
