348M model solves 14-digit arithmetic with step-by-step column reasoning

News time: 2026-09-10T05:56:16.941464+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher has trained a compact 348-million-parameter language model from scratch on 22.7 billion tokens—and fine-tuned it to perform multi-digit arithmetic not by guessing answers, but by explicitly simulating human-like columnar reasoning: carrying in addition, borrowing chains in subtraction, and partial-product decomposition in multiplication. Unlike most LLMs that treat arithmetic as a black-box prediction task, this model shows its work in natural language—e.g., 'ones: 10 − 2 = 8, borrow 1'—and those reasoning traces are tightly coupled to correctness: when the steps are valid, the final answer is right 95.3% of the time.

The model outperforms GPT-3 175B on nearly all digit-length arithmetic subtasks—even though it’s over 500× smaller. On five-digit addition, it achieves 100% accuracy versus GPT-3’s 9.3%; on four-digit subtraction, 98.3% versus 26.8%. Crucially, its strength isn’t memorization: it generalizes far beyond its training distribution. Trained only on place names up to 'hundred-thousands' (six terms), it spontaneously invented 'millions' and 'ten-millions'—enabling flawless 7- and 8-digit computation. When the vocabulary was extended to 19 place names, clean performance scaled cleanly to 14-digit problems.

This reveals a striking insight: for structured symbolic tasks like arithmetic, vocabulary coverage—not model capacity or data volume—is the primary bottleneck. The model already understood the algorithmic pattern; it just lacked lexical anchors for higher place values. Other capabilities include 98% accuracy on 3×3 multiplication (by folding partial products into the same column routine) and 85% on negative results—though magnitude comparison remains the weak link. It fails almost entirely on word problems (4% on GSM8K), not due to arithmetic errors, but because it misidentifies operations—e.g., reading 'drops in 836 more' as subtraction instead of addition.

The model requires greedy decoding: sampling breaks the deterministic column chain. It also lacks division and hits hard limits at 4×4 multiplication. Still, its interpretability stands out—every reasoning step is legible, editable, and causally linked to the output. As the author notes, this isn’t just about math: it’s a proof that small, purpose-built models can achieve superhuman reliability on well-structured reasoning tasks—if trained with the right scaffolding, vocabulary, and supervision.
