Real fly brain circuit failed to learn Pong — and the failure revealed critical bugs

News time: 2026-09-10T05:56:16.941606+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher attempted to train a biologically accurate subcircuit from the newly released MaleCNS v1.0 connectome — a full 166,000-neuron reconstruction of the male Drosophila central nervous system — to play Pong. Unlike viral demos where fly-brain models appear to ‘play’ Doom or Beat Saber in game engines that generously interpret neural noise as behavior, Pong offered a stark, binary test: hit or miss, frame by frame, with no room for illusion. The goal was modest: use dopamine-inspired synaptic plasticity to let photoreceptor-driven visual input guide paddle movement. It didn’t work — but what followed was far more valuable than success would have been.

The post-mortem audit became a masterclass in rigorous connectome-based validation. First, a subtle neuPrint bug was uncovered: a regex query intended to match neuron names used substring semantics instead of full-match, silently zeroing out two entire neuron populations — a flaw invisible in documentation and easy to overlook. Then, structural flaws emerged: the initial circuit had no anatomical path from photoreceptors to motion-processing neurons, because real fruit fly vision relies on multi-layered preprocessing — not direct connections. Even after fixing connectivity, turning learning on or off produced identical output across multiple random seeds, despite weights changing under the hood. Tracing further revealed half the motor neurons assigned to paddle control had zero incoming synapses — they were selected purely by array index, not biology.

After rebuilding the circuit around a better hypothesis — repurposing a courtship pursuit pathway — learning finally diverged between conditions. But instead of improving performance, the plasticity rule suppressed activity overall, worsening misses. Crucially, the author cross-checked other high-profile projects using MaleCNS: the Doom simulator admitted failing its own validation gates; the Minecraft mod acknowledged its motion-detection circuit remained silent, relying on hand-coded fallbacks; and the Beat Saber demo was overfit to one track using replay-injected data. The full writeup documents every step — from synapse-level tracing to comparative benchmarking — and ends with a call to focus next on the central complex, the fly’s navigation hub, rather than routing around it.

This isn’t just about Pong. It’s about how we validate biological AI. In an era of flashy demos, this work insists on transparency, reproducibility, and anatomical fidelity — treating failure not as embarrassment, but as the highest-yield data source. When a circuit doesn’t learn, the question isn’t ‘what went wrong?’ — it’s ‘what does the breakdown tell us about the model, the tools, and the biology?’
