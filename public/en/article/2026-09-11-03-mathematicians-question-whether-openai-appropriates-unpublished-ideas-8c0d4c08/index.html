Mathematicians question whether OpenAI appropriates unpublished ideas

News time: 2026-09-11T05:55:51.348058+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A growing number of mathematicians are raising urgent ethical alarms about how proprietary large language models—particularly OpenAI’s—interact with unpublished research. At the heart of the concern is a simple but unsettling possibility: when researchers use models like ChatGPT or Codex to explore open problems, test conjectures, or refine proofs, could those interactions—whether through training data ingestion or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)—be used to absorb, internalize, and later reproduce novel mathematical insights without attribution?

The debate crystallized after Andreas Thom, a mathematician at Technische Universität Dresden, publicly described a case where he shared unpublished work with an OpenAI model during exploratory dialogue. Shortly afterward, OpenAI published results closely aligned with his ideas—yet without citing him or acknowledging the interaction. Thom and others argue this resembles academic misconduct if done by a human collaborator; the fact that it’s performed by a corporate AI system doesn’t erase the epistemological and ethical stakes.

This isn’t just about credit—it’s about provenance, trust, and the integrity of mathematical discovery. Unlike code or text generation, mathematics relies on precise attribution, reproducibility, and communal verification. If models silently absorb and repurpose unshared ideas during RLHF—or even via passive logging of user chats—the line between collaboration and appropriation blurs. As one commenter noted, comparing OpenAI to a human coauthor makes the issue stark: no researcher would publish jointly derived insights without naming their collaborator.

The technical ambiguity deepens the concern. While OpenAI claims its models aren’t trained on user chats, RLHF inherently uses human feedback—including expert mathematical reasoning—to shape model behavior. That process may encode latent knowledge not present in pretraining data. Meanwhile, tools for verifying LLM provenance—like Stemma, a black-box fingerprinting method, or C2PA-style metadata standards—remain immature for text-based scholarly output. Without transparency into what data shaped a model’s reasoning, mathematicians face a new kind of vulnerability: contributing to AI progress while risking invisibility in the resulting science.
