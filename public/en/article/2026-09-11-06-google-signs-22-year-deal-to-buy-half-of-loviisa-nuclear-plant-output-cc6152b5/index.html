Google signs 22-year deal to buy half of Loviisa nuclear plant output

News time: 2026-09-11T05:55:51.348021+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a landmark move for sustainable cloud infrastructure, Google has signed a 22-year power purchase agreement (PPA) with Finnish utility Fortum to buy up to 50% of the electricity generated by the Loviisa Nuclear Power Plant. The deal—announced in early 2024—marks one of the largest corporate nuclear PPAs in Europe and underscores Google’s commitment to powering its expanding AI and cloud operations with low-carbon electricity.

Loviisa, located on Finland’s southern coast, houses two Soviet-designed VVER-440 reactors, each producing 507 megawatts. Together, they supply about 10% of Finland’s annual electricity and have operated reliably since the 1970s. Unlike newer nuclear builds facing cost overruns and delays, Loviisa is an existing, fully licensed asset—making it a stable, near-term source of carbon-free baseload power. Google’s choice reflects a pragmatic shift: rather than waiting for new clean energy projects, it’s securing long-term access to proven, operational nuclear generation.

Finland’s appeal goes beyond emissions. Its cool climate reduces data center cooling costs, its grid remains relatively uncongested compared to Western Europe, and its electricity mix is already among the cleanest in the world—averaging just 71 grams of CO₂-equivalent per kilowatt-hour, thanks largely to nuclear and hydro. For Google, this combination delivers both environmental credibility and operational resilience, especially as AI workloads demand ever more predictable, high-capacity power.

Critics note that the agreement doesn’t expand Finland’s total generation capacity—it simply redirects existing output. Some commenters argue it may even strain regional electricity markets, as neighboring countries like Estonia and Latvia historically imported surplus Finnish power. Others question whether surging AI compute demand will truly persist for two decades, pointing to advances in model efficiency and hardware compression. Still, the deal signals a broader industry pivot: major tech firms are no longer treating nuclear as a political footnote but as a strategic energy partner for decarbonizing digital infrastructure.
