NASA satellite image technique reveals hidden ancient rock art

News time: 2026-09-11T05:55:51.347992+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A decades-old NASA-developed image enhancement method—originally designed to sharpen details in satellite photos—is now helping archaeologists uncover ancient rock art that is nearly invisible to the naked eye. Known as Decorrelation Stretch (or DStretch), the technique works by mathematically separating and amplifying subtle color and spectral differences across multiple image bands—especially those beyond human vision, like near-infrared. While first applied to Earth-observing missions such as NASA’s ASTER instrument, researchers have increasingly adapted it to field-collected multispectral photographs and even drone-based imagery of rock shelters and cliff faces.

The power of DStretch lies not in adding new data, but in reorganizing existing pixel information to maximize visual contrast between materials with similar appearances. For example, faded ochre pigments on sandstone may blend into the background under normal light—but when processed through DStretch, slight variations in iron oxide content or surface weathering become dramatically visible. This has led to documented discoveries across North America, Australia, and southern Africa, where previously unrecorded petroglyphs and pictographs have emerged from decades-old digital photos.

What makes this approach especially impactful is its accessibility. Unlike proprietary remote sensing software, DStretch is open-source and integrated into widely used tools like ImageJ and GIMP. As one commenter demonstrated, users can replicate core functionality in GIMP using LAB color decomposition and manual level adjustments—no specialized hardware or training required. This democratization means community archaeologists, Indigenous heritage teams, and even students can apply the method without institutional support.

Still, experts caution against overinterpretation. DStretch enhances differences, not truth—it can exaggerate noise or create false patterns if misapplied. Its effectiveness also depends heavily on image quality, spectral band selection, and surface conditions: as one field researcher noted, trying it at Angkor Wat with handheld filter swaps and uncooperative guards yielded little success. Yet when paired with careful documentation and ground-truthing, it serves as a powerful first-pass tool—turning archival images into new sources of cultural insight.
