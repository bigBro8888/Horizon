Why hoarding cables is both practical and perilous

News time: 2026-09-11T05:55:51.347893+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a quietly resonant essay titled "Don't let anyone take away your big box of cables," Jim Nielsen reflects on the emotional weight and functional logic behind keeping dozens—sometimes hundreds—of cables in drawers, boxes, and travel bags. It’s not just clutter; it’s contingency planning. For many tech-savvy readers, that box represents readiness: the ability to revive an aging printer, power a forgotten NAS drive, or patch together a legacy audio interface at a moment’s notice. Nielsen doesn’t mock the habit—he validates it, framing cable hoarding as a form of low-stakes preparedness rooted in real-world experience with obsolescence and scarcity.

But the essay also opens space for sober second thoughts. As commenters quickly point out, not all cables are interchangeable—even when they fit. One user recounts frying a hard drive after plugging a SATA power cable into a modular PSU with mismatched pinouts, a risk invisible to the untrained eye. Another notes how grouping cables by type (e.g., USB-A, USB-C, legacy printer cables) reveals duplication and enables rational culling—turning hoarding into intentional archiving. The discussion becomes less about guilt over clutter and more about discernment: what’s genuinely useful, what’s dangerously misleading, and what’s merely nostalgic.

The theme extends beyond hardware into intergenerational knowledge transfer. A commenter describes unspooling decades-old workshop scraps from his late grandfather’s stash—wood off-cuts, copper pipe, salvaged connectors—and realizing those materials weren’t junk but infrastructure for improvisation. That same ethos lives in today’s cable boxes: not hoarding for its own sake, but preserving options in a world where standards shift, documentation vanishes, and manufacturers stop supporting devices overnight.

Still, there’s tension between utility and risk. Some cables degrade silently; others carry hidden incompatibilities. And while a well-organized cable kit can solve urgent problems—like helping a small business set up older office hardware three times in one month—it also demands ongoing vigilance. The takeaway isn’t “throw it all away” or “keep everything forever.” It’s that every cable tells a story—not just of past use, but of future possibility, fragility, and responsibility.
