TMLR review delays stall PhD student’s postdoc applications

News time: 2026-09-11T05:55:51.859580+00:00 · Source: reddit

A final-year PhD student recently voiced mounting frustration on Reddit over an unexplained two-month delay in TMLR’s editorial process—despite having received positive, timely reviews and submitting only minor revisions. The paper, solo-authored and scientifically sound, has been stuck in limbo since the revision was uploaded: one reviewer acknowledged satisfaction with the changes, but the other two remain silent, and no decision has been issued. The student contacted both the assigned Action Editor and the Editor-in-Chief, yet the submission status remains unchanged—leaving them unable to list the paper as accepted or conditionally accepted on their CV ahead of critical postdoctoral application deadlines.

This incident is not isolated. Independent audits—such as the 2026 OpenReview-based TMLRAudit—have documented growing variability in decision timelines, with some papers awaiting final decisions for over 100 days after revision. While TMLR was founded in part to offer a more transparent, efficient alternative to traditional journals like JMLR, its reliance on volunteer Action Editors (AEs) and reviewers—many juggling teaching, research, and service commitments—has introduced bottlenecks, especially during academic year transitions and conference seasons.

TMLR’s editorial model assigns each paper to an Action Editor who oversees double-blind review, synthesizes feedback, and renders the final decision. Unlike conference area chairs, AEs serve renewable two-year terms and handle papers year-round—but they have no formal deadline enforcement mechanism. Their availability must be manually updated on OpenReview, and the system offers no automatic reminders or escalation paths when reviews stall. As noted in official AE guidelines, pre-screening must occur within one week, but downstream steps—including reviewer follow-up and consensus building—lack time-bound protocols.

For early-career researchers, timing is career-critical. Postdoc applications often require evidence of peer-reviewed acceptance or strong conditional acceptances by late fall; delays at this stage can force candidates to omit promising work from their dossiers or miss windows entirely. The student’s experience underscores a structural tension in open, community-run publishing: ideals of openness and fairness depend on sustained human capacity—and when that capacity wanes, transparency alone cannot accelerate decisions.
