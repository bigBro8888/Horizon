Google replaces visible URLs in search results with opaque goto links

News time: 2026-09-12T05:44:44.377285+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Google has quietly overhauled how links appear in its search results: direct, human-readable destination URLs—like example.com/blog/post—are no longer shown. Instead, every organic result now points to an intermediate Google domain—www.google.com/goto?url=...—which redirects users only after JavaScript executes and a server-side hop completes. This change, confirmed by Google in late August 2026, is not tied to ranking or indexing updates; it’s purely a presentation-layer shift affecting the user-facing interface of Search Engine Results Pages (SERPs).

The move follows years of incremental obfuscation: first hiding full URLs behind "Visit" buttons in Chrome, then shortening display paths (e.g., showing only 'example.com' instead of the full path), and now fully replacing links with indirection. As noted in technical analyses, these goto links obscure the true destination domain until after navigation begins—making it impossible for users, screen readers, or privacy tools to preview where they’re going without executing Google’s code.

This affects more than aesthetics. SEO professionals report increased difficulty auditing link equity and tracking referral sources, since analytics platforms see traffic as coming from google.com/goto rather than the original domain. Accessibility advocates warn that screen readers and keyboard-only navigation suffer when links lack descriptive, stable href values. Developers running automated crawlers or archival tools must now handle JavaScript-driven redirects and rate-limiting—raising the technical and operational cost of reading Google’s results at scale.

Critics argue the change deepens Google’s control over information flow: it enables richer telemetry (e.g., precise click timing, device context, and session continuity), weakens link transparency central to web decentralization, and further distances users from the underlying architecture of the open web. While Google states the change improves anti-bot defenses and helps combat spam, independent observers note that legitimate researchers, journalists, and small-site owners bear disproportionate friction—while large platforms with engineering resources can still reverse-engineer or proxy around the restrictions.
