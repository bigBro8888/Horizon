Pandas is fading as Polars and AI reshape data science

News time: 2026-09-12T05:44:44.377401+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A provocative new essay titled 'Pandas Should Go Extinct' argues that pandas—the once-dominant Python library for tabular data manipulation—is no longer the best tool for most modern data tasks. The author contends that its design, built for single-threaded, in-memory workflows of the 2010s, now clashes with today’s demands: larger datasets, parallel execution, tighter integration with AI pipelines, and stricter performance expectations. Rather than incremental improvement, the piece calls for a deliberate phase-out—replacing pandas not just with faster alternatives like Polars, but with fundamentally different abstractions better suited to vector embeddings, query-optimized backends, and streaming data.

Polars emerges as the leading successor: a DataFrame library built on Apache Arrow and written in Rust, offering lazy evaluation, automatic query optimization, and true multithreading. Benchmarks consistently show Polars outperforming pandas on joins, aggregations, and filtering—especially beyond ~1 GB of data. Yet the article stresses this isn’t merely about speed: Polars’ functional, expression-based API encourages more declarative, composable, and testable code—aligning with how modern data engineers and ML practitioners think about transformations.

Still, the transition isn’t frictionless. As community commenters point out, pandas remains deeply embedded—not just in production codebases, but in pedagogy, documentation, and tooling ecosystems. Many data professionals still start with Excel, move to pandas at the gigabyte scale, and only consider alternatives when hitting memory limits or latency bottlenecks. And performance isn’t universal: one commenter notes Polars can be slower than pandas on small DataFrames (e.g., 5,000 × 3) due to thread overhead—highlighting the need for profiling before wholesale migration.

The broader shift reflects how AI itself is changing data science. Vector databases, embedding pipelines, and LLM-augmented analysis increasingly bypass traditional ETL steps entirely. When models ingest raw text or images directly, the role of row-column wrangling shrinks. Pandas wasn’t designed for that world—and retrofitting it may be less productive than adopting purpose-built tools. The essay doesn’t claim pandas is broken, but rather that its era has passed: good enough for yesterday’s problems, but no longer the right foundation for tomorrow’s.
