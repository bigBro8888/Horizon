Software engineers feel sadness when AI outcodes them

News time: 2026-09-12T05:44:44.087269+00:00 · Source: rss

Simon Willison’s short but resonant essay ‘Feeling sad about AI’ captures a quiet, widespread emotional turning point among software engineers: the moment they realize an AI coding agent has just completed, in an hour, work that would have taken them a week—and done it well. That feeling isn’t just frustration or competition; it’s a kind of grief—grief for a skill once central to professional identity, now rapidly commoditized by large language models and autonomous coding agents.

Willison doesn’t dismiss that sadness. Instead, he treats it as a legitimate, even necessary, phase—an existential checkpoint. He recalls experiencing it himself years ago, and notes how common it is among peers who’ve watched AI move from writing boilerplate to designing APIs, debugging complex race conditions, and even refactoring legacy systems. The sting comes not from being replaced outright, but from realizing that translation—turning precise specs into functional code—is no longer the scarce, high-value skill it once was.

What follows, he argues, is a productive reorientation. Once engineers accept that coding-as-translation is increasingly automated, they can redirect attention toward what remains deeply human: defining ambiguous problems, weighing trade-offs across security, scalability, ethics, and user experience; mentoring junior developers; shaping product vision; and integrating AI tools thoughtfully rather than reactively. These tasks demand judgment, context, and responsibility—qualities no current AI possesses.

Willison also grounds this shift in historical perspective. Software engineering has never been static: languages, frameworks, deployment models, and paradigms have cycled every few years—from COBOL to JavaScript, monoliths to microservices, servers to serverless. What’s new is the pace, not the pattern. Choosing this field, he reminds readers, was always a commitment to continual reinvention—not stability. The sadness, then, isn’t a sign of obsolescence. It’s the first signal that it’s time to level up.
