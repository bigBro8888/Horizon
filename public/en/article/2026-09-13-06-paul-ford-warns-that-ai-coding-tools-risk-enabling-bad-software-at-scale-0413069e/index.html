Paul Ford warns that AI coding tools risk enabling bad software at scale

News time: 2026-09-13T06:05:41.572100+00:00 · Source: rss

In a sharply observed essay published in the New York Times and highlighted by Simon Willison, veteran technologist Paul Ford pushes back against the assumption that generative AI has rendered human software developers obsolete. Rather than heralding an era of fully automated engineering, Ford argues that the real story is more subtle—and more urgent: AI excels at producing functional, boilerplate code, but it cannot replicate the collaborative judgment, domain intuition, or craft discipline required to build robust, maintainable, and truly innovative systems.

Ford’s reflection arrives at a pivotal moment—just as low-code interfaces, AI pair programmers, and LLM-powered IDE plugins have made coding accessible to millions with little formal training. That democratization, he cautions, has a dark side: it lowers the barrier not just to writing code, but to shipping software without design rigor, testing discipline, or architectural foresight. 'A.I. can write very good software,' he writes, 'but it also makes it easy to do someone else’s job badly'—a line that captures the core tension between productivity gains and systemic risk.

What distinguishes Ford’s take from earlier AI skepticism is its grounding in practice, not prophecy. He doesn’t deny AI’s utility—he acknowledges its strength in accelerating routine tasks—but insists that the hardest parts of software development remain stubbornly human: aligning technical choices with business goals, navigating legacy constraints, mentoring junior engineers, and making trade-offs under uncertainty. These are not tasks that respond well to prompt engineering or fine-tuning; they demand conversation, shared context, and iterative learning across teams.

The essay also subtly reframes the 'human-in-the-loop' ideal—not as a temporary bottleneck to be optimized away, but as the essential operating system of responsible software creation. When everyone can generate code, the value shifts from syntax mastery to critical curation: knowing when not to use AI, when to pause and sketch a diagram, when to escalate a design decision, and how to read error messages not as failures but as signals about deeper mismatches in assumptions. In that light, Ford’s warning isn’t anti-AI—it’s pro-software, pro-craft, and pro-human collaboration.
