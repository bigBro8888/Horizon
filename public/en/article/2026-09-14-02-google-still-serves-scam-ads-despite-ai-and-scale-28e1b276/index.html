Google still serves scam ads despite AI and scale

News time: 2026-09-14T06:13:36.844978+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A growing wave of user reports and technical analysis reveals that Google continues to serve highly convincing scam and malvertising ads across its ecosystem — from AdSense on publisher sites to YouTube pre-rolls. These ads mimic official government services (like UK visa applications), deploy fake legal warnings (“You owe $100”), and redirect users through fast-flux domains hosted on trusted cloud platforms — azurestaticapps.net, herokuapp.com, netlify.app — making them hard to block at the domain level. Publishers describe AdSense as a 'nightmare', with thousands of malicious impressions slipping through Google’s automated ad review pipeline, even though Google controls both the ad serving infrastructure and the policy enforcement mechanism.

The problem isn’t new, but its persistence is alarming in 2026. Google’s ad review center and Ad Manager tools offer manual oversight, yet they rely heavily on reactive flagging and batched machine learning models trained on historical patterns — not real-time behavioral cloaking or server-side redirection tactics used by campaigns like DarkRelay. As one commenter notes, scammers rotate subdomains daily, exploiting Google’s inability to treat entire cloud-hosted TLDs (e.g., *.azurewebsites.net) as high-risk vectors without false positives. Meanwhile, advertisers report increasingly aggressive monetization pressure: a source familiar with over $100M in Google Ads spend claims internal incentives now prioritize short-term revenue over long-term trust.

This isn’t just about bad UX — it’s systemic risk. Malvertising can deliver ransomware, credential stealers, or browser hijackers directly into enterprise networks. Fortinet warns such campaigns routinely cause 'inoperable computers and system networks'. And unlike traditional media, where publishers bore strict liability for fraudulent ads, Google operates under Section 230-like protections — enabling it to deflect responsibility while profiting from the traffic. Critics argue this creates a perverse incentive: the more traffic Google routes through unvetted, AI-generated landing pages, the more data it collects and the more revenue it generates — even as user harm mounts.

What makes this especially urgent is timing. With AI poised to disrupt digital advertising — through generative ad creation, hyper-personalized targeting, and automated bidding — Google’s current failure to police its own platform undermines confidence in its ability to govern AI-driven ad ecosystems responsibly. If today’s scam ads slip through, what happens when tomorrow’s AI agents autonomously negotiate, generate, and deploy deceptive creatives at scale? The question isn’t whether Google can fix this — it has the engineering talent and infrastructure — but whether it has the will.
