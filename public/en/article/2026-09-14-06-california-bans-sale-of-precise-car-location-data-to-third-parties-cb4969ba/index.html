California bans sale of precise car location data to third parties

News time: 2026-09-14T06:13:36.844911+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Your car is no longer just a mode of transport—it’s a rolling data factory. Modern vehicles, especially those with connected infotainment systems and built-in telematics control units (TCUs), continuously collect granular information: real-time GPS coordinates, speed, braking patterns, cabin temperature, voice commands, door lock status, and even driver attention metrics. Much of this data flows—often by default—to automakers’ cloud servers, where it’s aggregated, analyzed, and frequently sold or licensed to data brokers, insurers, advertisers, and municipal planners.

This practice has long operated in the shadows, with opaque privacy policies buried in lengthy terms-of-service agreements. Most drivers remain unaware that their daily commute, weekend errands, or late-night trips are being mapped, profiled, and monetized. As one commenter noted, even small-town street redesign proposals now rely on anonymized—but highly revealing—speed and location data sourced from cars, not traffic sensors.

The tide is turning. California Assembly Bill 1542, passed in September 2026 and poised for gubernatorial signature, directly targets this surveillance economy. The law amends the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) to classify geolocation data accurate within 1,850 feet (about 564 meters) as "sensitive personal information." Under AB-1542, selling or sharing such data without explicit, opt-in consent becomes illegal—not just unethical. Crucially, the bill applies to all entities handling vehicle-derived location data, including automakers, software providers, and third-party data aggregators.

Still, enforcement remains a challenge. Telematics data transmission is deeply embedded in vehicle architecture: TCUs use encrypted cellular modems to push data to the cloud, often with no user-accessible toggle. Disabling features like remote start or navigation may not stop background collection—and some functions require data sharing to operate at all. As one security-conscious owner discovered, even older Volkswagen models retain persistent connectivity pathways that outlive app accounts and settings changes. Legal reform is essential, but technical countermeasures—from Faraday pouches for key fobs to OBD-II port blockers—are gaining traction among privacy advocates.
