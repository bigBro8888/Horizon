Hoofs project models UK and Irish horse racing with 1.18 million runners

News time: 2026-09-14T06:13:36.997648+00:00 · Source: reddit

A UK-based practitioner has launched ‘Hoofs’, a long-running personal machine learning project focused on ranking horses in British and Irish flat and jump racing. Rather than treating racing as a simple classification or regression task, the author frames it as a nuanced ranking problem: each race has a variable number of runners (typically 3–20), only one winner per race, and all competitors share tightly coupled features—form, jockey, trainer, ground conditions, distance, track layout, and more. The dataset spans roughly ten years and includes 1.18 million historical runner records across over 80 racecourses and more than 900 distinct track/distance/race-type combinations—far richer in structural diversity than Hong Kong’s two-track system.

The modeling pipeline emphasizes temporal integrity. Every model is evaluated using walk-forward validation: training folds consist only of earlier seasons, with strict safeguards against look-ahead bias—no future odds, no delayed feature updates, and explicit out-of-fold calibration. Performance is measured both statistically (AUC, log loss, Brier score) and operationally (e.g., % of races where the winner ranks #1, top 3, or top 5). On a large 2018–2025 benchmark of 886,000 runners, the model-only win AUC reached 0.729—respectable, but notably below the market’s 0.790 win AUC. This gap underscores the central challenge: the betting market is highly efficient, meaning most publicly available signals are already priced in.

To navigate this, Hoofs separates modeling from market logic. Core models estimate win and place probabilities without odds or market data; a secondary race-level confidence model then assesses field competitiveness using entropy, probability spread, and field size. Market information enters only at the final decision layer—used for bet sizing and selection refinement, not model training. Recently, the author rebuilt the entire pipeline after detecting data inconsistencies and feature coverage gaps, releasing updated daily public reports. On its first live day post-rebuild, the Top 1 pick won 10 of 23 races (43.5% strike rate), and the winner appeared in the Top 3 in 16 of 24 races.

Beyond technical rigor, the post serves as an invitation. The author notes the surprising scarcity of open discussion around racing ML—even though it’s a canonical real-world testbed for ranking, non-stationarity, sparse feedback, and causal confounding. With daily reports freely published since early 2024 and evolving model families—including experimental late-market integrations—the project exemplifies applied ML grounded in domain realism, not synthetic benchmarks.
