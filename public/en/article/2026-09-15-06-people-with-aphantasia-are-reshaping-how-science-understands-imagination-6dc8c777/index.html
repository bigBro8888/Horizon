People with aphantasia are reshaping how science understands imagination

News time: 2026-09-15T06:08:50.109708+00:00 · Source: hackernews

For decades, cognitive science assumed that mental imagery — the ability to 'see' things in your mind’s eye — was central to imagination, memory, and even problem-solving. But a growing body of evidence from people who experience aphantasia — the lifelong inability to voluntarily generate visual mental images — is forcing researchers to rethink those assumptions. Far from being a rare curiosity, aphantasia affects an estimated 2–3% of the general population, and its study is revealing that imagination isn’t monolithic: it can be rich in conceptual, spatial, or verbal form without any pictorial component.

This shift isn’t just theoretical. Neuroscientists are now mapping distinct brain networks activated during visualization tasks in aphantasic individuals — networks that often emphasize frontal and parietal regions over the visual cortex. Meanwhile, real-world professionals — from photographers to Pixar animators — report thriving creatively despite lacking internal imagery. One photographer notes he ‘visualizes zero’ with eyes closed yet has built a 20-year career; another comment cites Ed Catmull, co-founder of Pixar, who discovered many top animators at the studio also have aphantasia — suggesting that struggle with internal imagery may foster stronger external expression and iterative design skills.

The implications extend beyond art and neuroscience. Aphantasia challenges long-held models linking imagery vividness directly to memory strength or creativity. Studies show aphantasics perform equally well — sometimes better — on certain spatial reasoning and memory tasks, indicating alternative cognitive strategies. Even dreams complicate the picture: some aphantasics report vivid dream imagery despite total absence of voluntary visualization, pointing to dissociation between conscious control and unconscious perceptual generation.

Importantly, aphantasia isn’t a deficit in the clinical sense — it’s a variation in neurocognitive style. As one commenter puts it, ‘It’s more of just a “knowingness”’ — a phrase capturing how abstract, schematic, or linguistic thinking can substitute for pictorial representation. This reframing supports broader neurodiversity principles: cognition isn’t broken when it diverges from the dominant model, but differently organized. The science of imagination is no longer about how well you picture things — but how many ways the human mind can represent, reason, and create.
