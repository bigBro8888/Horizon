LLMs fail to replicate unpublished NeurIPS research, challenging RSI claims

News time: 2026-09-15T06:08:50.692241+00:00 · Source: reddit

A satirical yet methodologically serious paper—circulating on Reddit and citing the fictional arXiv ID '2607.27191'—argues that current frontier LLMs cannot perform open-ended machine learning research, and therefore cannot initiate recursive self-improvement (RSI). The study’s core experiment, dubbed a "shadow evaluation," tasked agents—including Codex/GPT-5.6 Sol and OpenClaw/Opus 4.8—with independently reproducing two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions. Given six days and thousands of dollars in compute, the agents generated full drafts, which were then evaluated not by peer reviewers, but by the original human authors of those papers.

The results were unambiguous: none of the AI systems produced work deemed publishable or even conceptually aligned with the original contributions. Authors reported critical gaps—not just in implementation or writing quality, but in problem framing, hypothesis generation, experimental design intuition, and iterative scientific reasoning. Crucially, the paper does not claim RSI is logically impossible, but rather that it remains empirically unsupported: if LLMs cannot yet conduct novel, open-ended ML research under realistic constraints, then the widely cited pathway to intelligence explosion lacks an observable foundation.

This critique targets a key assumption in AI safety discourse: that scaling current architectures will naturally lead to autonomous research capability, which in turn enables self-modification and runaway capability growth. The shadow evaluation protocol is notable for its realism—it avoids cherry-picked benchmarks and instead tests agents on actual, unsolved research problems known only to their human creators. While the paper appears fictional (arXiv does not host IDs beyond ~2025), its experimental logic mirrors real-world efforts like the NeurIPS 2026 Shadow Review initiative, where similar failures have been documented.

The post’s author, u/we_are_mammals, expresses frustration with low-quality discussion on r/MachineLearning—highlighting how even technically grounded critiques struggle to spark substantive dialogue. Still, the idea resonates: evaluating AI against unpublished human research, graded by the researchers themselves, sidesteps benchmark overfitting and offers a stringent, ground-truth test of scientific agency. As AI labs race toward agentic autonomy, this framework may become a vital diagnostic—not for when RSI arrives, but for when it begins to look plausible.
