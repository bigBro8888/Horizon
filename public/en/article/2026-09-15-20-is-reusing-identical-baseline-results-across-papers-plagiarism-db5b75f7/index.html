Is reusing identical baseline results across papers plagiarism

News time: 2026-09-15T06:08:50.692569+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning researcher recently raised a subtle but widespread dilemma on Reddit: if they evaluate two new models—say, a decision tree and a neural network—on the same regression task, and report identical baseline performance (e.g., RMSE values) in two separate journal submissions, does that cross an ethical line? The question reflects a real tension between efficiency and academic transparency. Running baselines once saves time and computational resources, especially when datasets and evaluation protocols are fixed—but copying those exact numbers into multiple manuscripts risks appearing as self-plagiarism or duplicate publication.

This isn’t about fabricating data or hiding methodology. It’s about how to ethically represent reused empirical results. In ML research, baselines like linear regression or random forest are often treated as standardized reference points—not novel contributions—so their metrics rarely change across studies using the same data split and preprocessing. Yet journals and ethics guidelines treat verbatim reuse of text and numerical results with caution. The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) explicitly warns against duplicate submission, where overlapping content—including tables of results—may mislead reviewers about the novelty or independence of each work.

The core issue lies in framing, not calculation. Reusing RMSE values is acceptable if clearly disclosed: authors should state that baseline evaluations were performed once, describe the shared experimental setup (e.g., train/test splits, hyperparameters, software versions), and cite or cross-reference the original reporting context. Without such transparency, identical tables—even for non-novel components—can trigger editorial concerns or accusations of redundant publication. Some journals even require authors to declare whether any results appear elsewhere, including preprints or concurrent submissions.

Ultimately, integrity hinges less on whether numbers are copied and more on whether readers and reviewers can assess reproducibility and contextualize claims. A well-documented, consistent baseline strengthens scientific rigor; silent reuse weakens trust. As AI research accelerates and benchmarking becomes increasingly standardized, clear norms around result reuse—backed by journal policies and community consensus—are no longer optional. They’re foundational to maintaining credibility in empirical machine learning.
