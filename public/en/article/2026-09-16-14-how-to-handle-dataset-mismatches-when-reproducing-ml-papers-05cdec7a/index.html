How to handle dataset mismatches when reproducing ML papers

News time: 2026-09-16T05:58:30.506950+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher on Reddit’s r/MachineLearning forum is struggling with a common but rarely discussed hurdle in ML research: reproducing reported results due to inconsistent dataset preprocessing. They’re working with a paper that states its dataset statistics in Table 1 — yet even after carefully implementing every filtering and cleaning step described in the methods section, their computed numbers differ by an order of magnitude for one dataset. The paper notes that data is "available on request," so the researcher emailed the authors and sent a polite follow-up — still no reply.

This isn’t just about missing files. It’s about ambiguity in prose-based preprocessing instructions: vague terms like "standard filtering," "quality control," or unreported random seeds can silently derail reproducibility. Without access to the exact preprocessed splits or code used in the original experiments, even faithful reimplementation may yield divergent datasets — raising questions about whether the reported metrics reflect real model behavior or accidental data artifacts.

The researcher asks nuanced, practice-oriented questions: Should they publish with their best-effort version and transparently document the mismatch? Would subsampling to match the paper’s stated size help — or just introduce new bias and undermine integrity? When does it make sense to contact the journal’s editorial office, and how should that be framed? And crucially: what actually works for getting preprocessed data from authors — including timing, tone, and framing of follow-ups?

These questions cut to the heart of modern ML research culture. Reproducibility isn’t only a technical challenge; it’s a social one. It depends on norms of transparency, responsiveness, and shared responsibility — none of which are formally enforced. Until standardized data release practices (e.g., versioned, containerized preprocessing pipelines) become routine, researchers will keep relying on informal networks, persistence, and tactful negotiation to close the gap between published claims and executable science.
