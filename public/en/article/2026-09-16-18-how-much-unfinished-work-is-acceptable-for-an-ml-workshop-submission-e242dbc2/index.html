How much unfinished work is acceptable for an ML workshop submission

News time: 2026-09-16T05:58:30.506880+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher recently asked a practical question on Reddit’s r/MachineLearning: Can a workshop paper be built around a partially implemented algorithm—specifically, one that solves only problem A using a simplified version of the core idea, while the full version (solving both A and B) remains unimplemented but well-motivated and mathematically outlined? The author acknowledges that the current implementation lacks novelty on its own but argues that the vision, roadmap, and theoretical foundation for the complete solution are clear and compelling.

This question cuts to the heart of how machine learning workshops function in the academic ecosystem. Unlike flagship conferences like NeurIPS or ICML—which demand polished, empirically validated contributions—workshops are explicitly designed to host early-stage, speculative, or interdisciplinary ideas. As noted in guidance from ICLR and ICML, workshop proposals emphasize intellectual curiosity, community-building potential, and forward-looking perspectives over technical completeness.

The norms are consistent across venues: most ML workshops accept 4–6 page papers, often with lighter review loads and faster turnaround. According to academic discussions on Medium and Stack Exchange, workshops serve as incubators—where researchers share promising directions, gather feedback, refine methodology, and sometimes later submit expanded versions to top-tier conferences. What reviewers typically prioritize is not final performance numbers, but clarity of motivation, soundness of the underlying idea, and plausibility of the proposed extension.

That said, there are boundaries. A submission consisting only of vague promises or hand-waved mathematics—without any working code, empirical validation (even limited), or concrete design choices—would likely be rejected. The Reddit poster’s case sits comfortably within the acceptable range: they have a functional prototype, real experimental results on problem A, and a coherent plan backed by derivations. This reflects the pragmatic spirit of workshops—not as placeholders for weak work, but as collaborative spaces for vetting high-potential ideas before full investment.
