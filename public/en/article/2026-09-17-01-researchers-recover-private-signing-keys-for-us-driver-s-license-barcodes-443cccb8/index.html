Researchers recover private signing keys for US driver's license barcodes

News time: 2026-09-17T06:07:11.316740+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a landmark security disclosure, researcher Ryan Speers and collaborators have recovered the private cryptographic signing keys used to sign machine-readable PDF417 barcodes on millions of US driver’s licenses. These barcodes—standardized by the American Association of Motor Vehicle Administrators (AAMVA)—appear on physical licenses and contain personal data like name, address, date of birth, and sometimes even a digital signature image. Crucially, AAMVA-compliant licenses are designed so that scanners at bars, airports, or government checkpoints can cryptographically verify that the barcode hasn’t been tampered with—relying on digital signatures generated using secret private keys held by issuing authorities.

The breakthrough came not from breaking cryptography, but from reverse-engineering public key infrastructure misconfigurations. By collecting and analyzing hundreds of real-world AAMVA-compliant barcodes—many of which include the public verification key embedded directly in plaintext—the team identified repeated patterns in key generation and weak entropy usage across multiple state DMVs. Using known-plaintext attacks and lattice-based cryptanalysis, they successfully derived the corresponding private keys for several widely deployed signing certificates. This means attackers no longer need to compromise government systems: they can now forge barcodes that pass cryptographic validation checks.

This isn’t just theoretical. The vulnerability undermines the foundational trust model behind physical ID verification in the US. Unlike modern verifiable credentials—where users hold cryptographically signed, privacy-preserving attestations—the AAMVA system assumes the barcode is both static and trustworthy once issued. But with private keys recovered, counterfeit licenses can be generated with valid signatures, enabling bypasses of age verification, identity checks, and even border control systems relying solely on barcode validation.

Importantly, the flaw does not stem from broken algorithms like RSA or ECC, but from systemic implementation failures: predictable key generation, insufficient randomness, and the inclusion of public keys inside the very data being signed—a design choice that ironically aids attackers. As one commenter noted, the photo remains unsigned and unlinked to the barcode, meaning a forged barcode paired with a real photo passes most current checks. The research underscores a broader tension: legacy ID systems built for physical scanning are ill-suited for today’s digital threat landscape—and patching them may require replacing entire infrastructures, not just updating software.
