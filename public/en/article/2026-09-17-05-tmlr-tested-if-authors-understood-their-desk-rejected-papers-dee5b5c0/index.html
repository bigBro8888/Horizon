TMLR tested if authors understood their desk-rejected papers

News time: 2026-09-17T06:07:11.540750+00:00 · Source: reddit

In an unprecedented meta-scientific experiment, the Transactions on Machine Learning Research (TMLR) — a leading open-access journal in machine learning — reached out to the authors of 10 papers flagged for immediate desk rejection. Rather than simply rejecting them, Co-Editor-in-Chief Nihar Shah personally contacted each group and asked basic technical questions about their own submissions: What problem does the paper solve? How does the method work? What assumptions underlie the claims? The goal was not to re-review the papers, but to probe author engagement, conceptual clarity, and accountability at the earliest stage of publication.

The results were sobering. Of the 10 authors contacted: one withdrew the submission; one cited scheduling conflicts; one failed to attend a scheduled call; three could not answer even foundational questions about their methodology or evaluation; three grasped high-level motivations but faltered when pressed on implementation details, ablation design, or theoretical justification; and only one author answered all questions coherently — though Shah later identified a major technical flaw in that paper. No author demonstrated full command of both the what and the why behind their work.

This experiment reflects growing pressure on TMLR’s editorial team. With desk rejection rates soaring from ~6% in 2023 to over 53% today — driven by surging submission volume and limited reviewer bandwidth — editors face tough triage decisions. Yet the findings suggest that many desk rejections may stem not just from formatting violations or overlap (per official guidelines), but from deeper issues: superficial engagement with the science, fragmented authorship, or reliance on automated tools without full comprehension. As one commenter noted, it’s increasingly common for papers to be co-authored by individuals who contributed code, writing, or experiments — but not the core intellectual scaffolding.

The implications extend beyond TMLR. If authors cannot explain their own methods, reproducibility suffers, peer review becomes performative, and readers lose trust. The journal has not yet announced policy changes, but the experiment signals a shift toward accountability as a prerequisite for consideration — not just correctness. For the broader ML community, it’s a stark reminder that publishing velocity must not eclipse understanding.
