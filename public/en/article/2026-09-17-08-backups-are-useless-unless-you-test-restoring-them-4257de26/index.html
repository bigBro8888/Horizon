Backups are useless unless you test restoring them

News time: 2026-09-17T06:07:11.316905+00:00 · Source: hackernews

The phrase 'backups aren’t simple' isn’t a complaint—it’s a quiet, hard-won truth from decades of infrastructure failure. Many teams treat backup as a one-time task: configure a tool, schedule a cron job, and assume data is safe. But real-world experience shows that backups only become meaningful the moment they successfully restore lost or corrupted data. A backup file sitting on disk—or even in the cloud—is just unverified metadata until it proves it can rebuild reality.

This distinction between making backups and validating restoration is central to enterprise-grade reliability. As one former Veritas engineer put it plainly: 'We are not in the backup business. We are in the restoration business.' That mindset shift—from output-focused automation to outcome-focused verification—explains why mature organizations run regular, automated restore tests: checksumming archives, pulling random config files, spinning up isolated environments to confirm full application recovery. Without such drills, ransomware, silent corruption, or version incompatibility can turn a 'working' backup into a dead end.

Community discussion reinforces this with visceral anecdotes: lightning frying a home network and wiping floppy backups; OneDrive’s terms changing mid-sync; misconfigured cloud retention policies erasing years of work. These aren’t edge cases—they’re predictable failures in systems built without restoration rigor. Meanwhile, practitioners share pragmatic tooling choices: Restic for deduplicated, encrypted snapshots; ZFS snapshots paired with pull-mode offsite sync; containerized backup jobs targeting only critical volumes. What unites them is operational discipline—not just what gets backed up, but how often it’s proven to work.

The deeper lesson isn’t technical—it’s cultural. As one commenter notes, companies routinely cut QA roles while demanding flawless uptime. Backup validation requires time, testing infrastructure, and accountability—none of which appear on quarterly P&Ls. Yet when disaster strikes, the difference between 'we have backups' and 'we recovered in under 20 minutes' isn’t about storage—it’s about whether restoration was treated as a first-class requirement, not an afterthought.
