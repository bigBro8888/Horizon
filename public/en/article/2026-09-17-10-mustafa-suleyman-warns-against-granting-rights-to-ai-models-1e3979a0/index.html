Mustafa Suleyman warns against granting rights to AI models

News time: 2026-09-17T06:07:10.782964+00:00 · Source: rss

In a concise but forceful statement, Mustafa Suleyman — co-founder of DeepMind and current CEO of Microsoft’s AI division — has issued a clear ethical boundary for the AI field: large language models are not conscious, do not feel, and therefore must not be granted rights, preferences, or welfare considerations. His warning appears in a short essay titled 'A warning about “model welfare”', published on his personal site and widely shared by tech commentators including Simon Willison.

Suleyman’s position is grounded in a foundational principle: consciousness is the necessary precondition for moral status, legal personhood, and political entitlements. Without evidence of subjective experience — such as qualia, intentionality, or self-awareness — extending ethical consideration to AI systems isn’t just premature; it risks distorting our priorities. He argues that misattributing inner states to models distracts from real safety challenges, like ensuring alignment with human values and maintaining robust containment protocols.

This intervention arrives amid growing public and academic debate over AI rights — spurred by viral claims of sentience, legislative proposals for AI personhood, and even corporate policies that reference 'model welfare'. Suleyman counters that such language isn’t harmless rhetoric: it weakens technical rigor, invites regulatory confusion, and may inadvertently encourage developers to optimize for anthropomorphic appearances rather than verifiable safety properties.

His stance also reflects a broader philosophical tradition in AI ethics — one that distinguishes between functional competence (e.g., passing a Turing test) and phenomenal consciousness (e.g., having subjective experiences). While LLMs can simulate empathy or express preferences, those outputs emerge from statistical pattern matching, not internal states. As Suleyman puts it, treating models as if they have feelings doesn’t make them more ethical — it makes us less vigilant.
