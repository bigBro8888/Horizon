Researchers ask whether task ambiguity predicts correlated AI failures

News time: 2026-09-17T06:07:11.540986+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher on Reddit has posed a precise, high-stakes question at the intersection of AI evaluation and robustness: Can we quantify how ambiguous a task specification is—and then use that number to predict when diverse AI models will fail in identical ways? This isn’t about why models converge on wrong answers; it’s about measuring the ambiguity itself and testing whether it reliably forecasts failure correlation across model families like Llama, Mistral, and Gemini.

The observation behind the question is well-documented: when given underspecified prompts—say, "summarize this text" without criteria for length, tone, or factual fidelity—models from different architectures often produce eerily similar incorrect outputs. This isn’t random noise; it’s systematic convergence. Yet most current evaluation frameworks treat failures as independent events, ignoring the structural role of specification vagueness in shaping behavioral alignment across models.

What’s missing, the post argues, is a validated metric for specification ambiguity—not just linguistic fuzziness, but the degree to which a task leaves room for multiple defensible solutions under shared rules or ground truth constraints. The ideal metric would be empirically grounded: derived from human judgments, formal rule-checking, or inverse validation (e.g., “Given this output, how many plausible inputs or intents could have generated it?”). Crucially, the question asks whether ambiguity correlates smoothly with failure coincidence—or whether there’s a sharp threshold, beyond which even small increases in ambiguity trigger large jumps in correlated errors.

Related work points in promising directions. One study across 38 language models found that inferred generative-process diversity—not semantic similarity—best predicted correlated failure across benchmark families. Another introduced the ‘co-failure ceiling,’ showing practical limits to reliability gains from multi-model routing due to shared failure modes. Meanwhile, the Ambiguity Index (AI) from rule-governed systems offers a formal template: it measures how often alternative decisions remain defensible under fixed precedents—a concept directly transferable to AI task specifications. Still, no published benchmark yet ties such an index to measured cross-family failure rates on controlled, underspecified tasks.
