Never adopt LLM-suggested phrasing when writing

News time: 2026-09-18T05:53:30.025846+00:00 · Source: rss

In a sharply argued essay, security researcher Thomas Ptacek proposes a deceptively simple but powerful rule for ethical AI-assisted writing: You may not use a single word an LLM suggests to you. This isn’t a stylistic preference—it’s framed as intellectual personal protective equipment (PPE), designed to safeguard voice, authenticity, and analytical rigor. Ptacek insists that LLMs should function strictly as copyeditors—checking grammar, spotting typos, flagging factual inconsistencies—not as co-authors or idea generators.

Simon Willison, who highlights and endorses the piece on his weblog, echoes this boundary. He explicitly refuses to let LLMs draft blog content but relies on them for targeted, low-risk tasks: verifying claims, correcting spelling and grammar, and occasionally sourcing synonyms—using a carefully engineered proofreading prompt he’s published publicly. The discipline of rejecting all suggested phrasing, he notes, feels intuitively right: LLM output often carries a subtle, uncanny ‘weird smell’—a telltale sign of homogenized, context-light language that erodes individual voice.

Ptacek illustrates his approach with a screenshot of a custom-built LLM copyediting tool—a lightweight interface that accepts raw text and returns only objective, rule-based feedback (e.g., ‘passive voice detected’, ‘ambiguous pronoun’, ‘citation needed’). Crucially, it never offers rewritten sentences. Instead, it nudges the human writer to make deliberate, conscious revisions. He also shares a starter prompt template to help others build similar tools—emphasizing constraints like ‘do not generate alternative phrasings’ and ‘flag issues without rewriting’.

This philosophy stands in contrast to mainstream prompt engineering trends that optimize for fluency, polish, or speed. Here, the goal isn’t smoother text—it’s sharper thinking. By forbidding phrase adoption, writers retain full ownership of every syntactic choice, forcing deeper engagement with structure, logic, and tone. As Ptacek puts it, the LLM becomes a mirror, not a mouthpiece: it reflects flaws so the author can correct them—not a crutch that quietly substitutes judgment with statistical likelihood.
