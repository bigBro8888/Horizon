XGBoost falls short of human market predictions in financial forecasting

News time: 2026-09-18T05:53:30.507218+00:00 · Source: reddit

A practitioner on Reddit recently shared a sobering observation: when trained on the same public information available to traders, an XGBoost model consistently underperforms the aggregate human market — not just slightly, but by a meaningful margin. Specifically, its Top 1 accuracy (predicting the single most likely outcome) lags far behind market consensus, and even its Top 2 accuracy trails by 10 percentage points. Strikingly, feeding the model the market’s own pricing signals — such as implied probabilities or odds — fails to boost performance; in some cases, it even degrades accuracy compared to using market prices alone.

This isn’t just about tuning hyperparameters or gathering more data. The poster explicitly questions whether the gap reflects a fundamental limitation of gradient-boosted trees in modeling adaptive, self-referential systems — where participants observe and react to each other’s behavior — or whether it stems from solvable issues like feature encoding, label leakage, or misaligned evaluation metrics. In efficient markets, price formation already embodies collective reasoning, so adding a model on top may offer diminishing returns unless it captures truly non-public structure or behavioral anomalies.

XGBoost remains a workhorse in quantitative finance for tasks like credit scoring and demand forecasting, where ground-truth labels are stable and causal relationships are relatively static. But financial markets differ: they’re reflexive, feedback-driven, and constantly evolving. A model that excels at predicting retail sales based on historical trends may struggle when the ‘label’ itself — e.g., tomorrow’s stock move — is partly determined by today’s model outputs, news sentiment, and herd behavior.

The post resonates because it surfaces a quiet tension in applied ML: we often benchmark models against benchmarks that themselves embed intelligence. When the ‘baseline’ is human aggregation — as on prediction markets like Polymarket or through consensus analyst forecasts — outperforming it isn’t just a matter of better engineering. It may require rethinking representation, incorporating game-theoretic priors, or accepting that some predictive ceilings are structural, not technical.
