How hard is it to publish in Pattern Recognition versus NeurIPS

News time: 2026-09-19T05:49:41.729602+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning researcher facing likely rejection from NeurIPS—after receiving borderline scores of 2/3/3 and 3/4/4—is weighing whether to submit their Vision Transformer attention paper to mid-tier journals instead of re-submitting to ICLR or CVPR. Their question cuts to a practical dilemma many early- and mid-career researchers face: when conference submission feels like a long shot, do journals offer a realistic, less competitive alternative—or just a slower, equally demanding path?

The researcher specifically names five established but non-top-tier AI/ML journals: Pattern Recognition, Neurocomputing, Knowledge-Based Systems, Neural Networks, and Expert Systems with Applications. These are not elite venues like TPAMI or IJCV, nor open-access preprint-first outlets like TMLR, but respected, Scopus- and Web of Science-indexed journals with solid impact factors—Pattern Recognition currently at 9.1 (2026), Neurocomputing at 6.0—and decades-long publication histories.

Unlike top conferences, which operate on rigid annual cycles and single-round, high-stakes reviews, these journals use rolling submissions and multi-round revision processes. Acceptance is rarely outright; instead, editors often invite major or minor revisions, giving authors concrete feedback and time to strengthen claims, experiments, or presentation. This can be both a benefit—more iterative support—and a drawback—longer timelines (often 4–8 months from submission to decision) and higher expectations for completeness, reproducibility, and contextualization than conference papers typically require.

That said, acceptance standards aren’t uniformly lower. While NeurIPS’s ~22% acceptance rate reflects intense competition for novelty and broad appeal, journals like Pattern Recognition report no official acceptance rate—but community estimates suggest 30–50%, with emphasis shifting from ‘breakthrough potential’ to ‘technical soundness, clarity, and incremental contribution’. For a paper refining attention mechanisms in ViTs—solid but not paradigm-shifting—the journal route may indeed be more viable, especially if the work includes thorough ablation studies, additional baselines, or expanded discussion of limitations.

Ultimately, the choice isn’t just about odds—it’s about goals. Conferences prioritize visibility, speed, and community engagement; journals emphasize archival rigor, completeness, and longevity. For researchers building a track record or targeting industry-academia hybrid roles, a well-positioned journal publication can carry comparable weight—especially in regions or institutions where journal metrics still dominate evaluation.
