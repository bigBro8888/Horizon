AI-generated event posters can be functional not awful

News time: 2026-09-20T06:11:05.768240+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new essay by John Hartnup challenges the widespread assumption that AI-generated event posters are inherently terrible. Rather than dismissing them outright, he argues that many AI posters meet basic functional needs—clear hierarchy, legible text, appropriate imagery—and serve their purpose well enough for low-stakes contexts like local meetups, academic seminars, or community fairs. His examples, drawn from tools like Piktochart and Fotor, show posters that avoid glaring errors in typography, alignment, or color contrast—flaws common even in human-made amateur designs.

This isn’t a claim that AI posters rival award-winning graphic design. Hartnup explicitly acknowledges their limitations: predictable compositions, shallow cultural references, and stylistic homogeneity. But he reframes the debate around practicality, not perfection. When organizers have tight budgets, no in-house designer, and only 20 minutes to promote an event, an AI poster that’s ‘good enough’ may be more ethical—and more effective—than waiting days for a freelancer who delivers inconsistent results.

The Hacker News discussion that followed—807 comments long—reveals deeper tensions about labor, aesthetics, and authenticity. Some users defend AI as a pragmatic upgrade over low-tier freelance designers on platforms like Fiverr, citing repeated experiences with poorly kerned fonts, mismatched palettes, and missed deadlines. Others criticize AI’s reliance on clichés: a ‘Japanese minimal poster’ defaults to cherry blossoms and flag motifs, while a ‘90s drum & bass flyer generates warped wireframe spheres—not because they’re stylistically accurate, but because those are the most statistically probable outputs.

What emerges is not a binary choice between AI and humans, but a spectrum of collaboration. As research shows, designers increasingly use AI for rapid ideation, layout scaffolding, or accessibility checks—then refine manually. The real bottleneck isn’t technical capability; it’s how we define value in design: Is it novelty? Efficiency? Cultural resonance? Or simply getting the date, time, and venue across without confusion? Hartnup’s quiet conclusion is that for many real-world uses, ‘not horrible’ is already a meaningful win.
