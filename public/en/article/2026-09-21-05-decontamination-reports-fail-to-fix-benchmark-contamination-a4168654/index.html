Decontamination reports fail to fix benchmark contamination

News time: 2026-09-21T06:17:03.700384+00:00 · Source: reddit

In early 2024, OpenAI quietly retired SWE-bench Verified — a widely cited coding benchmark designed to measure AI systems’ ability to fix real-world GitHub issues. The reason wasn’t underperformance: every frontier model they tested could reproduce human-written fixes or even verbatim problem details for some tasks. Progress had stalled — just six points gained over six months — and it became unclear whether remaining score gains reflected genuine coding ability or mere memorization of benchmark artifacts.

The conventional response to such concerns is a ‘decontamination report’: a lab asserts its training data contains no exact matches to the benchmark test set. But as Noah Persaud argues in a widely discussed Reddit post, this approach is fundamentally broken. First, the lab audits itself — no external party can verify the search because the full training corpus is proprietary and legally undisclosable. Second, even perfect n-gram matching misses semantic contamination: paraphrased tutorials, Stack Overflow walkthroughs, GitHub solutions, and synthetic data derived from the benchmark all teach models the answers without sharing a single substring.

Worse, technical workarounds like private set intersection or proof-of-training schemes don’t solve the core problem. They only verify claims about a declared corpus — not what the model was actually trained on — and recent research shows such proofs can be spoofed. So Persaud proposes flipping the responsibility: instead of trusting labs’ self-reports, evaluators must control the entire evaluation pipeline. That means no network access during inference, evaluator-built code from pinned commits, hidden test data generated after submission deadlines, and — critically — requiring independent reproduction before any result counts.

He’s already implemented a minimal version for tabular ML models, where funders post problems and performance thresholds, and submissions are scored on private, non-reusable test sets. The system openly acknowledges its limits: it doesn’t guarantee benchmark validity, prevent label leakage, or ensure third-party reproducibility — but it shifts verification from uncheckable assertions to observable, repeatable actions. As he puts it: ‘Reproduce it, or it doesn’t count.’
