Sherline Tools ends US production after 50 years

News time: 2026-09-21T06:17:03.389489+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Sherline Tools, a California-based manufacturer of precision miniature milling machines and lathes since 1974, has announced it will cease all US-based production. Founded by Joe Martin in the early 1970s, Sherline built its reputation on compact, high-accuracy machines designed for hobbyists, educators, and small workshops — tools that were often the first CNC-capable machines many makers ever owned or converted. The company’s products were proudly ‘Made in the USA,’ with tight tolerances, modular accessories, and decades of consistent design. But now, after nearly half a century, the factory in Vista, California, is winding down operations.

The decision reflects deeper structural shifts in the maker and small-scale manufacturing landscape. As community commenters on Hacker News observe, demand for traditional homebrew CNC projects — where users build or retrofit machines from scratch — has steadily declined. Instead, accessible integrated tools like desktop CNC routers (e.g., Bantam Tools, Nomad), laser cutters, and especially 3D printers have absorbed much of the hobbyist workflow. These systems offer faster iteration, lower entry barriers, and strong software ecosystems — often at prices far below what a fully equipped Sherline mill or lathe would cost.

At the same time, global supply chains have reshaped expectations around value. High-precision components once sourced domestically — linear rails, stepper motors, controllers — are now widely available from Asia at dramatically lower costs. Commenters note that converting older industrial machines (like Grizzly mills or Bridgeports) with modern motion controllers (e.g., Masso, Acorn, or open-source Smoothieboard firmware) delivers far more rigidity and capability per dollar than new Sherline units. One longtime owner lamented that his Sherline lathe and mill sit unused while his 3D printers run daily.

Beyond economics, broader cultural and institutional factors are at play: aging maker demographics, difficulty attracting younger engineers to hands-on metalworking, rising regulatory and compliance burdens in US/EU manufacturing, and the erosion of local supplier networks that once made small-batch prototyping agile and affordable. Sherline’s closure isn’t just about one company — it’s a quiet milestone marking the end of an era defined by incremental, tool-first craftsmanship in favor of digital-first, system-integrated fabrication.
