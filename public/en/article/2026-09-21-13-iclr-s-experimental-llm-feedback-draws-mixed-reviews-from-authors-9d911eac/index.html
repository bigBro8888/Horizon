ICLR’s experimental LLM feedback draws mixed reviews from authors

News time: 2026-09-21T06:17:03.700201+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher recently shared candid reflections on ICLR’s pilot use of large language models to generate supplementary feedback for submitted papers — a move that has sparked quiet but growing debate in the machine learning community. In a Reddit post on r/MachineLearning, the author described receiving three pages of LLM-generated comments alongside just one or two genuinely helpful suggestions. While they acknowledged the system helped refine their paper in subtle ways, they expressed concern over its tendency toward superficial nitpicking — flagging minor phrasing issues or formatting inconsistencies while missing deeper conceptual weaknesses.

This experiment is part of ICLR’s broader exploration of AI-assisted review processes, especially as submission volumes surge and human reviewer capacity strains. According to ICLR’s official blog, the 2026 conference explicitly permits limited, transparent use of LLMs — not as replacements, but as aids — with strict guidelines around disclosure, anonymization, and human oversight. The initiative aligns with ongoing workshops on human-in-the-loop reasoning systems, where explainability and interpretability are central themes.

What stands out is not just the uneven quality of the feedback, but the lack of clarity around how it’s integrated. The author asked whether these LLM-generated reviews remain publicly visible — and whether authors will be warned in advance if they are. That question cuts to core concerns about transparency, consent, and accountability: if an LLM’s critique appears alongside human reviewers’ comments without clear labeling, readers may misattribute authority or intent. It also raises practical questions about reviewer burden — are authors now expected to respond to both human and algorithmic critiques with equal weight?

The broader context matters: recent surveys and studies warn that LLM reviewers often diverge significantly from human judgments, exhibit prompt-dependent biases, and struggle with domain-specific nuance. One 2025 analysis in ScienceDirect identified ‘hallucinated critiques’ and inconsistent scoring as key red flags. Still, proponents argue that even imperfect LLM feedback can surface overlooked issues — like ambiguous definitions or unstated assumptions — when used thoughtfully and in tandem with expert review. Ultimately, this isn’t just about ICLR: it’s a real-world stress test for how academic institutions govern AI’s role in scholarly evaluation.
