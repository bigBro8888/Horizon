Boris Cherny’s essay on being wrong sparks debate about LLMs and management

News time: 2026-09-21T06:17:03.389451+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A reflective essay titled 'I am often wrong' by software engineer and product leader Boris Cherny has drawn attention—not for its stated theme of intellectual humility in product and management work, but for the unusually heated and technically grounded discussion it provoked in the comments. Though the post itself is sparse and lacks full exposition of its core idea, readers seized on it as a lens to examine deeper tensions in modern engineering culture: the growing influence of large language models on communication style, the erosion of accountability in tool-mediated workflows, and the gap between rhetorical ideals like 'embracing error' and real-world practices like delayed bug fixes.

Several commenters pointed to concrete shortcomings in Claude Code—a developer tool built by Anthropic—highlighting long-unfixed bugs, such as Fable messages disappearing from user view despite being referenced by the model. One commenter estimated that 15–20% of Fable-generated messages vanish silently, undermining trust in the system’s transparency. Others questioned internal contradictions in Cherny’s described workflow—for instance, defining the goal after identifying a solution—which seemed to clash with standard problem-solving logic.

A particularly resonant critique came from a reader who observed that Cherny’s writing and speaking style closely resembles output from LLMs, not because he uses them directly, but because years of interacting with agent-based tools have subtly reshaped his rhetorical habits. This observation points to a broader phenomenon: as engineers increasingly interface with AI systems—not as assistants but as primary communication channels—their own voice, reasoning patterns, and even sense of urgency may become unconsciously aligned with token-generation logic rather than human-centered judgment.

The discussion also surfaced concerns about leadership tone. One commenter interpreted Cherny’s stated principle—'act with urgency to achieve the goal'—as a red flag, arguing that declaring everything urgent effectively eliminates prioritization, a hallmark of unsustainable management. While the original essay aimed to normalize error as part of learning, the community response centered instead on accountability, clarity, and the material consequences of abstraction layers between people and code. In that light, the post functions less as a manifesto and more as a diagnostic mirror—revealing how deeply AI tools are already embedded in, and altering, professional practice.
