ICLR’s automatic reviewer assignment raises fairness concerns

News time: 2026-09-21T06:17:03.700149+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent Reddit post has drawn attention to a potentially problematic aspect of the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) review policy: its automatic reviewer assignment rule. According to the policy, any individual whose name appears on three or more papers—regardless of author order, contribution level, or academic seniority—is expected to serve as a reviewer. The post, authored by a user identifying as an early-career researcher with only two paper appearances, questions whether this threshold is reasonable—or even ethical—when applied to junior researchers who may lack the experience, training, or domain depth required for rigorous peer review.

This concern isn’t merely theoretical. Consider a hypothetical graduate student, Alex, listed as fourth author on three lab papers—likely reflecting modest contributions such as data collection or minor coding help. Under ICLR’s current rule, Alex would be automatically flagged for reviewer duty, despite having no formal mentorship in reviewing, limited exposure to conference standards, and possibly little familiarity with evaluation criteria like novelty, technical soundness, or reproducibility. The policy documentation, including the official ICLR 2026 and 2027 Reviewer Guides, confirms that no explicit qualification requirements—such as prior reviewing experience, PhD status, or minimum publication record—are stated.

The issue touches on broader tensions in academic service expectations. Conferences like ICLR rely heavily on volunteer reviewers to maintain quality and scale, but shifting that burden onto unprepared early-career researchers risks undermining review quality, increasing burnout, and reinforcing inequities. Junior researchers already face steep learning curves in writing, presenting, and navigating peer feedback; adding mandatory reviewing without scaffolding—like training modules, shadow-review opportunities, or opt-out pathways—may do more harm than good.

That said, ICLR is not alone in using co-author count as a proxy for eligibility. Similar heuristics appear at NeurIPS and other top ML venues, though some now offer opt-in reviewer nomination or allow authors to designate experienced co-authors. A recent arXiv study analyzing ICLR 2024–2025 reviews highlights how reviewer expertise correlates strongly with review quality and author satisfaction—suggesting that indiscriminate assignment could weaken the very process it aims to sustain. As the field grows, balancing scalability with fairness remains a live challenge—one that demands transparency, flexibility, and intentional design.
