Writers should not outsource thinking to AI when drafting

News time: 2026-09-22T06:10:08.660387+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a quietly urgent essay titled I don’t want to read what you didn’t write, software engineer and technical writer Colin Breck issues a timely warning about the erosion of intellectual authenticity in the age of large language models. He observes a growing habit: engineers and domain experts draft sparse, half-formed notes—sometimes just bullet points or code comments—then hand them to an AI to “expand” into polished blog posts, design documents, or RFCs. The result, he argues, isn’t clarity—it’s semantic inflation: verbose text that sounds authoritative but carries little of the author’s actual understanding.

Breck’s concern goes beyond style or voice. It’s epistemic: if the writer hasn’t fully formed the idea before prompting the AI, then the final text doesn’t represent their knowledge—it represents the model’s plausible interpolation. Readers, especially technical ones, rely on writing as a proxy for the author’s mental model. When that model is absent or underdeveloped, the document becomes unreliable—not just as communication, but as knowledge transfer. As one commenter notes, approving a 20-line code change accompanied by pages of AI-generated justification forces reviewers into an impossible position: either skim superficially (risking oversight) or invest hours deciphering prose that wasn’t authored with intent.

This isn’t a rejection of AI tools altogether. Breck acknowledges AI’s utility in editing, formatting, or translating well-understood ideas into different registers. But he draws a firm line at using it to generate the core insight. That step—distilling experience into meaning—requires struggle, iteration, and personal accountability. As hatthew puts it in the comments, writing is information transfer: if you only supply 300 bits of real understanding and ask an LLM to invent the other 700, those extra bits aren’t yours to claim—and they may not even be true.

The stakes are practical and cultural. In engineering teams, over-reliance on AI-generated documentation risks degrading shared context, slowing onboarding, and weakening collective reasoning. Worse, it trains people to defer to fluency over fidelity—to value the appearance of clarity more than the substance of thought. Breck’s essay is ultimately a call to protect the slow, messy, human work of sense-making—not because it’s nostalgic, but because it’s irreplaceable.
