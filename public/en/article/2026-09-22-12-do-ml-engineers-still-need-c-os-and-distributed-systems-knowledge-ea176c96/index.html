Do ML engineers still need C, OS, and distributed systems knowledge

News time: 2026-09-22T06:10:08.716063+00:00 · Source: reddit

A computer engineering graduate with embedded systems experience recently asked a question resonating across AI infrastructure teams: Is deep systems knowledge—like C/C++ programming, Linux internals, memory management, multithreading, and distributed systems—still practically essential for ML engineering roles? The query reflects growing tension between rising abstraction layers in ML tooling (e.g., high-level frameworks like PyTorch Lightning or Hugging Face Transformers) and the persistent need for low-level control when scaling models, optimizing latency, or debugging production failures.

The answer, according to both industry practice and emerging research, is a firm yes—but with nuance. While many entry-level ML roles focus on data pipelines and model tuning, the most impactful work in AI infrastructure—building faster training loops, efficient inference servers, or compiler-integrated optimizations—relies heavily on systems fundamentals. For example, Google’s MLGO project embeds machine learning directly into LLVM to replace hand-crafted compiler heuristics, but developing and maintaining those models requires fluency in both compiler design and ML. Similarly, companies like Meta and NVIDIA routinely use C++ for core components of their inference engines (e.g., TorchScript runtime, TensorRT), where memory layout, cache behavior, and thread safety directly impact throughput and cost.

Distributed systems knowledge remains indispensable—not just for training large models across thousands of GPUs, but also for managing heterogeneous hardware fleets, implementing fault-tolerant model serving, and designing scalable feature stores. Even seemingly high-level abstractions like Ray or Kubeflow sit atop deeply systems-aware implementations. Meanwhile, compiler expertise is no longer niche: LLVM-based MLGO models are now shipped with official LLVM releases, and practitioners train policies using reinforcement learning on real codebases like Fuchsia.

That said, automation is accelerating—not replacing—systems skills. AI-assisted optimization tools don’t eliminate the need to understand what is being optimized; they shift the engineer’s role from manual tuning to defining reward functions, curating training corpora, and validating model behavior under edge cases. In short: abstraction raises the ceiling of what’s possible, but systems knowledge remains the foundation that keeps the ceiling from collapsing.
