Digital platforms erode attention through bad design and lost tools

News time: 2026-09-22T06:10:08.660303+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a quietly powerful essay titled 'Attention is all you have', Alice Egg reflects on how the web—once a space of intentionality and control—has become an engine of distraction. She traces this shift not to user weakness, but to deliberate design choices: autoplaying videos, infinite scroll, algorithmically amplified outrage, and the quiet burial of tools that once helped users curate and recall information meaningfully. The essay isn’t about AI’s attention mechanism—it’s a human-centered lament for what we’ve given up in exchange for convenience and engagement metrics.

The piece grounds its critique in tangible losses: the disappearance of full-text search in browser history (a feature present in Mosaic in 1993), the marginalization of RSS feed readers, and the replacement of user-controlled information flows with opaque, ad-driven feeds. As one commenter notes, Firefox once supported RSS natively—but now ships with social media buttons in the address bar instead. These aren’t accidents; they’re symptoms of a business model where attention is harvested, not nurtured.

Egg also highlights how the decline of foundational tools reshapes behavior. Without reliable ways to revisit or organize past reading—no searchable history, no persistent feeds—users default to reactive consumption: refreshing feeds, chasing notifications, scrolling without purpose. This isn’t laziness; it’s adaptation to an environment engineered to make focus costly and fragmentation frictionless.

Yet the essay avoids fatalism. It points to quiet acts of resistance: disabling autoplay, installing privacy-respecting extensions like Hister or WorldBrain for full-text history search, reviving RSS with Feedly or Feeder, and practicing ‘intentional media’—deciding in advance what to read or do online. As one reader shares, after quitting social media, they realized their phone held nothing compelling enough to justify picking it up—a simple but profound recalibration of value.

Ultimately, 'Attention is all you have' reframes digital wellbeing not as self-discipline, but as infrastructure advocacy. Restoring attention means rebuilding tools that return agency: search over suggestion, subscription over surveillance, and memory over monetization.
