FoxPro is back as open-source Rust and WebAssembly project

News time: 2026-09-23T05:56:01.492020+00:00 · Source: hackernews

More than fifteen years after Microsoft officially discontinued Visual FoxPro with version 9 in 2007, a surprising revival has emerged—not as nostalgia, but as pragmatic engineering. The new FoxScript project reimplements the Visual FoxPro language runtime using Rust, compiles it to WebAssembly, and rigorously validates behavior against the original vfp9.exe binary. Its goal isn’t to replace legacy systems, but to extend their lifespan: businesses still running decades-old FoxPro applications—often on aging 32-bit Windows—can now add modern capabilities without rewriting from scratch.

This isn’t a rewrite in spirit only. The project preserves deep compatibility: existing .DBC database containers, .DBF table files, and even legacy 32-bit .FLL add-in libraries load and execute unchanged. At the same time, it removes hard limits—tables no longer cap at 2 GB—and adds features the original never had: first-class lambdas, native JSON support, and an embedded HTTP server. These aren’t bolted-on wrappers; they’re integrated into the language’s execution model, enabling lightweight web APIs or data export pipelines directly from FoxPro logic.

The motivation is grounded in real-world inertia. As one commenter noted, a $400M+ niche industry still relies on FoxPro in 2026—because rewriting a mission-critical business application carries enormous risk, cost, and downtime. For many small-to-midsize enterprises, especially in regulated or conservative sectors like healthcare, real estate appraisal, or manufacturing, 'if it ain’t broke' isn’t laziness—it’s operational survival. FoxScript meets them where they are: not by demanding migration, but by upgrading the foundation beneath what already works.

That said, the revival comes with caveats. Builds are currently unsigned, reports are incomplete, and security concerns linger—especially around the DBC’s lack of access controls and plain-text stored procedures. Still, the project’s MIT license and transparent design invite scrutiny and contribution. In an era obsessed with greenfield frameworks, FoxScript stands out as a rare example of respectful, technically rigorous legacy stewardship—where modern tooling serves continuity, not obsolescence.
