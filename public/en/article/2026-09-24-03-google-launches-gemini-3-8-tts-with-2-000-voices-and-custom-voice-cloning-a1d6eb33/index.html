Google launches Gemini 3.8 TTS with 2,000+ voices and custom voice cloning

News time: 2026-09-24T06:08:52.118837+00:00 · Source: rss

Google has quietly but significantly expanded its AI audio capabilities with the release of two new text-to-speech models: gemini-3.8-flash-tts and gemini-3.8-flash-lite-tts. Unlike previous TTS offerings, these models ship with a massive built-in voice library—over 2,000 distinct voices spanning more than 100 languages—and introduce an unusually accessible method for custom voice creation: just a 30-second clean audio sample, provided you hold rights to that voice. This lowers the barrier for developers, creators, and small studios to build expressive, character-driven audio experiences without expensive voice recording sessions or proprietary voice cloning pipelines.

What makes the launch especially developer-friendly is not just the models themselves—but how easily they can be used in the browser. Simon Willison, a well-known independent developer and toolmaker, built and open-sourced the Gemini 3.8 TTS Playground: a fully client-side web interface that lets users compose multi-speaker dialogues, assign unique voices and delivery styles (e.g., "excited and gossipy" or "calm and unimpressed"), and generate audio on demand. Crucially, it works directly with Google’s Gemini API thanks to an open CORS policy—a rare and valuable configuration that most production APIs restrict for security reasons.

The playground isn’t just a demo—it’s a functional, shareable tool. Compose settings are encoded in the URL, so users can bookmark or send links with preloaded scripts and speaker configurations. Your API key stays entirely in-browser memory and is never saved to local storage or sent to any server. Behind the scenes, the tool constructs precise JSON requests matching Google’s documented schema, making it both educational and production-relevant. In one example, Willison generated a 78-second audio clip of two pelicans debating relocation—using a script written by Claude 4.5 Opus—taking about 20 seconds and costing just $0.0274.

This release signals a strategic shift: Google is treating voice not as a utility layer, but as a creative medium. The Flash TTS model emphasizes deep character design and natural-language voice prompting (“a grumpy 70-year-old lighthouse keeper with a raspy whisper”), while Flash-Lite TTS targets high-volume, cost-sensitive use cases like dubbing and voice agents. Combined with Willison’s open playground, the ecosystem now supports rapid prototyping, education, and even lightweight production—all without backend infrastructure.
