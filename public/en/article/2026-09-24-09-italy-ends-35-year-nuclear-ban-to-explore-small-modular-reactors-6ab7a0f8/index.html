Italy ends 35-year nuclear ban to explore small modular reactors

News time: 2026-09-24T06:08:52.425697+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a historic reversal of energy policy, Italy’s Senate has approved legislation lifting the country’s decades-long ban on nuclear power — a prohibition rooted in public fear following the 1986 Chernobyl disaster. The vote does not authorize immediate reactor construction but establishes the legal and regulatory framework needed to evaluate future nuclear projects, with particular emphasis on small modular reactors (SMRs) and other advanced nuclear technologies.

The ban dates back to a 1987 national referendum held just one year after Chernobyl, which led to the shutdown of Italy’s four operational nuclear plants by 1990. Since then, Italy has relied heavily on imported electricity and fossil fuels — making it one of Europe’s largest net importers of energy. Today, with climate targets tightening and energy security concerns heightened by geopolitical instability, the government of Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni sees nuclear as a potential low-carbon complement to renewables.

Crucially, the new law focuses not on reviving traditional large-scale reactors — widely viewed as economically and politically untenable — but on enabling SMRs. These factory-built, scalable units promise shorter construction timelines, enhanced passive safety features, and flexibility for grid integration or industrial heat applications. However, no SMR design is yet commercially deployed in Europe, and Italy lacks both domestic nuclear expertise and an operational regulatory body for advanced reactors — meaning years of preparatory work lie ahead.

Public sentiment remains divided. While some Italians welcome the shift as pragmatic and long overdue — pointing to nuclear’s role in decarbonizing France and South Korea — others remain skeptical, citing unresolved waste challenges, high upfront costs, and water-intensive cooling requirements that may worsen under climate change. As one commenter noted, many Italian towns still display ‘denuclearized municipality’ signs — a visible reminder of the deep cultural imprint left by the 1987 vote. The road forward will require not only technical readiness but also sustained public engagement and transparent cost-benefit analysis.
