Portobello Police Station clock restored with modern mechanical retrofit

News time: 2026-09-24T06:08:52.425646+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a quiet act of civic care, the historic clock atop the Portobello Police Station in Edinburgh has been fully restored—not as a museum piece, but as a working, maintainable timepiece for the 21st century. The project, documented in a thoughtful blog post, avoided full digital replacement or cosmetic facelifts. Instead, it preserved the clock’s original brass gears, wooden frame, and visible pendulum while integrating subtle, purposeful upgrades: a small electric motor now drives the time train, replacing manual winding, and the weights have been removed. The pendulum remains functional—not for timekeeping accuracy, but for visual continuity and rhythmic presence.

The restoration reflects a growing ethos in infrastructure maintenance: respect legacy systems without romanticizing obsolescence. Rather than treating the clock as either ‘antique’ or ‘obsolete’, the team treated it as adaptable. They retained the escapement and gear train to preserve mechanical authenticity, yet decoupled timekeeping from mechanical reliability by syncing the motor to the UK’s 50 Hz mains frequency—a stable, grid-sourced reference that eliminates drift. This hybrid approach bridges craftsmanship and utility, where heritage isn’t frozen, but gently re-anchored.

Community input played a visible role—not just in funding or advocacy, but in engineering nuance. Commenters on Hacker News proposed practical enhancements: adhesive anti-slip tread for the wooden access ladder (a low-cost safety win), and a PoE-powered IP camera aimed at the gear train to monitor motion remotely. One observer noted the absence of a mechanical backup for the motorized time train—a valid caveat for long-term resilience—but also acknowledged that for a non-critical civic clock, simplicity and serviceability outweigh redundancy.

What makes this more than a local repair is its replicability. Unlike high-profile smart-city deployments, this project offers a template for maintaining aging civic infrastructure—libraries, town halls, post offices—with minimal tech, maximum transparency, and deep community ownership. It proves that ‘retrofitting’ need not mean bolting on AI dashboards or cloud APIs; sometimes, it means choosing the right motor, keeping the pendulum swinging, and letting neighbors watch it tick.
