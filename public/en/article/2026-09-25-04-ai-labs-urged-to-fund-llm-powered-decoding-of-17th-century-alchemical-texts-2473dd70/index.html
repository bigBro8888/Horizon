AI labs urged to fund LLM-powered decoding of 17th-century alchemical texts

News time: 2026-09-25T06:08:33.874903+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new call to action is gaining traction among digital humanities scholars: AI labs should directly fund historical research that leverages large language models (LLMs) to decode centuries-old manuscripts. The argument centers on a concrete, high-impact use case — using modern AI to read and interpret 17th-century alchemical letters and treatises, many of which remain inaccessible due to archaic handwriting, obscure symbolism, and obsolete terminology.

These texts are not mere curiosities. They represent vital links in the history of science, philosophy, and early chemistry — containing proto-scientific observations, experimental notes, and conceptual frameworks that influenced figures like Robert Boyle and Isaac Newton. Yet decades of scholarship have been bottlenecked by paleography: the painstaking, expert-dependent work of deciphering faded ink, idiosyncratic scripts, and layered marginalia. LLMs, fine-tuned on historical corpora and paired with multimodal agents, now show promise in automating parts of this process — from normalizing spelling variants to reconstructing damaged passages and mapping symbolic equivalences.

Crucially, this isn’t theoretical. The article highlights SourceLibrary.org, a free, agent-accessible repository built by the Embassy of the Free Mind in Amsterdam — a UNESCO-recognized library specializing in alchemy, mysticism, and esoteric traditions. It hosts tens of thousands of digitized and translated historical works, with APIs exposing embeddings and structured metadata. Unlike static archives, it’s designed for both human researchers and AI agents, enabling iterative prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, and collaborative annotation.

The broader implication is a shift in AI’s role: from consumer-facing chatbots to infrastructure for scholarly discovery. Commenters echo this sentiment — genealogists report catching long-standing errors in family trees; paleographers express relief at tackling ‘nightmare’ 17th-century hands; and historians praise LLMs as ‘idea machines’ that surface alternative epistemologies. As one commenter notes, after four years of GPT hype, one of AI’s most grounded, socially valuable applications may be helping us read what our ancestors actually wrote.
