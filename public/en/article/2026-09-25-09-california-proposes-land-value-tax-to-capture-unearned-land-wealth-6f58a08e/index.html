California proposes land value tax to capture unearned land wealth

News time: 2026-09-25T06:08:33.874994+00:00 · Source: hackernews

California’s fiscal and housing crises are converging—and a growing number of economists and policy advocates argue the state should look to a 150-year-old idea for a modern solution: the land value tax (LVT). Rooted in Henry George’s 19th-century Georgist economics, the LVT would tax only the underlying value of land—not buildings, renovations, or personal property. Proponents say this approach directly targets economic rent: the unearned income that accrues to landowners simply because demand for location rises, often due to public investment like transit, schools, or zoning changes.

Unlike California’s current property tax system—crippled by Proposition 13, which caps assessed value increases at 2% annually—the LVT would be reassessed regularly based on market land values. That means it could capture appreciation generated by community growth, rather than letting it flow exclusively to private owners. Crucially, because land cannot be moved, hidden, or offshored, the tax is inherently difficult to avoid—a stark contrast to income or wealth taxes, which ultra-high-net-worth individuals increasingly evade through relocation, legal challenges, or complex trusts.

Critics raise practical concerns. One central debate centers on tax incidence: if landlords face a new land tax, can they pass it on to renters? While Georgists argue the tax falls on landowners because land supply is fixed and inelastic, skeptics point out that in tight rental markets—even a theoretically non-shiftable tax may affect lease terms or discourage new construction if not carefully designed. Others note that Prop 13’s constitutional constraints make implementing an LVT politically fraught without broader reform—or creative workarounds, such as applying it only to commercial parcels or high-value residential sites first.

Still, momentum is building. With housing shortages worsening and state revenue increasingly volatile, the LVT offers a rare policy tool that simultaneously promotes denser development (by discouraging land banking), reduces inequality, and strengthens local fiscal resilience. As one commentator put it: 'We’re not taxing effort—we’re taxing exclusion.' In a state where wealth literally has feet—and often walks away when taxed—California may finally need a tax that stays rooted in place.
