AAAI reviewer raises alarm over inconsistent and AI-like reviews

News time: 2026-09-25T06:08:34.108018+00:00 · Source: reddit

A reviewer who served on the AAAI program committee has publicly voiced deep concerns about the integrity and consistency of the conference’s peer review process. In a Reddit post titled 'What's up with AAAI reviewers and organizers?', they describe reviewing several papers in the first phase—and finding that many received superficial, template-like feedback despite serious flaws: one paper was unblinded and failed to follow the official AAAI LaTeX template; another was incomplete, missing figures, code, and key sections; and a third relied heavily on LLM-generated mathematics with thin exposition and mismatched references. Yet all three advanced to Phase 2. The reviewer notes their own critiques were thorough—especially for the math-heavy paper, where they wrote an unusually detailed review—but those efforts appeared to have no impact on the outcome.

The post also highlights operational friction: after accepting an emergency review request, the reviewer’s coauthors were mistakenly flagged as ‘irresponsible’ by AAAI workflow chairs—a miscommunication that went unacknowledged or apologized for. This incident underscores broader tensions around scale and accountability: AAAI-26 expanded its Program Committee nearly threefold compared to the previous year, now managing over 75,000 unique submitting authors. With even a 1% email inquiry rate generating ~750 messages daily, volunteer-run systems risk breakdowns in communication and quality control.

While the post doesn’t claim AI tools were officially deployed, it points to telltale signs—repetitive pros-and-cons lists across human and ‘AI-like’ reviews, lack of engagement with technical nuance, and decisions that contradict careful human assessment. These patterns echo wider anxieties in the ML community about automation creeping into peer review without transparency or guardrails. Other Reddit threads confirm similar fatigue: NeurIPS rejectees discuss selective implementation of feedback, while ICLR resubmitters report extensive rewrites—including new theorems and pipeline diagrams—driven by reviewer criticism. That level of effort contrasts sharply with the perception that some AAAI reviews lacked basic diligence.

The issue isn’t just about individual mistakes—it’s about systemic sustainability. As top-tier AI conferences grow exponentially, maintaining rigorous, fair, and human-centered review becomes harder. Without clear standards for reviewer training, calibration, or AI-assisted tool usage—and without mechanisms to correct errors or acknowledge missteps—the credibility of the entire selection process is at stake.
