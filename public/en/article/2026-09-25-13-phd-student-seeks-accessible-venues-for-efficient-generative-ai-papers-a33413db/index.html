PhD student seeks accessible venues for efficient Generative AI papers

News time: 2026-09-25T06:08:34.108068+00:00 · Source: reddit

A fifth-year PhD student in machine learning has turned to Reddit’s r/MachineLearning community for practical help: they need reputable but realistically attainable publication venues to fulfill degree requirements. Despite having research on efficient Generative AI — a timely and increasingly important subfield — all their submissions to top-tier conferences like NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR have been rejected, even when receiving positive or borderline review scores. This reflects a growing pain point among early-career researchers: the extreme selectivity of elite AI venues, where acceptance rates often fall below 25%, and where factors beyond technical merit — such as perceived novelty framing, reviewer alignment, or conference scope — heavily influence outcomes.

The student explicitly asks for alternatives outside the so-called 'elitist club' of A* conferences. Their priority is no longer prestige, but graduation — a pragmatic shift shared by many late-stage PhDs facing funding deadlines, visa constraints, or mental health pressures. Efficient Generative AI sits at the intersection of model compression, quantization, distillation, inference optimization, and hardware-aware training — topics that are technically mature enough for solid contributions but may lack the splashy 'breakthrough' narrative favored by top conferences.

Mid-tier options often better match this profile. Journals like IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence (TPAMI) or Neural Networks offer rigorous peer review without the hyper-competitive lottery of NeurIPS. Conferences such as AAAI, ECML-PKDD, or the newer International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) Workshop Track provide lower barriers while maintaining academic credibility. Even domain-specific venues — like ACM SIGMOD for efficient database-integrated GenAI, or MLSys for systems-aware ML — can be strong fits if the work emphasizes implementation, benchmarks, or reproducibility over theoretical leaps.

What makes this query resonate is its honesty about structural realities in AI publishing. It underscores how degree completion — a basic academic milestone — has become entangled with venue politics rather than scholarly contribution. For students outside well-resourced labs or elite institutions, navigating this landscape alone is especially daunting. Community-driven advice, while informal, fills a critical gap left by opaque review processes and unspoken norms.
