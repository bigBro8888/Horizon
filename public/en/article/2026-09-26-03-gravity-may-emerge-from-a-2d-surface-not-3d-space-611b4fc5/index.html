Gravity may emerge from a 2D surface, not 3D space

News time: 2026-09-26T06:07:57.132431+00:00 · Source: hackernews

For decades, physicists have grappled with a deep inconsistency: general relativity describes gravity as the curvature of smooth, four-dimensional spacetime, while quantum mechanics demands that information be discrete and localizable. The holographic principle offers a radical resolution — suggesting that all gravitational phenomena inside a region of space can be fully encoded on its lower-dimensional boundary, like a hologram storing 3D images on a 2D film.

This idea gained concrete footing in 1997 when Juan Maldacena discovered the AdS/CFT correspondence — a precise mathematical duality between a quantum field theory without gravity (a conformal field theory, or CFT) living on the boundary of a hypothetical universe, and a theory of gravity in the interior (anti–de Sitter space, or AdS). Though our universe isn’t exactly AdS, the duality provided the first rigorous example where gravity emerges from non-gravitational quantum physics.

Recent work strengthens the case beyond theoretical elegance. Observations of black hole entropy — which scales with surface area, not volume — align perfectly with holography. Likewise, calculations of entanglement entropy across spatial boundaries reproduce gravitational dynamics, suggesting spacetime itself may be woven from quantum correlations. As one commenter noted, this doesn’t mean our 3D world is ‘illusory’ — rather, it implies two equivalent descriptions exist, and the boundary description may be more fundamental.

The implications extend far beyond formal theory. If spacetime is emergent, then concepts like locality, causality, and even time may break down at Planck-scale resolutions. Experimental efforts — such as precision tests of gravitational waves or tabletop analogs using cold atoms and optical lattices — are now probing whether spacetime’s smoothness holds up under scrutiny. Meanwhile, philosophers and physicists alike debate what ‘real’ means when multiple mathematically consistent descriptions coexist — not as approximations, but as dual truths.
