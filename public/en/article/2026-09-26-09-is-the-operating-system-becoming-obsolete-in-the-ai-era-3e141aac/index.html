Is the operating system becoming obsolete in the AI era

News time: 2026-09-26T06:07:57.132375+00:00 · Source: hackernews

What even is an OS now? — that provocative question opens a widely discussed 2026 blog post (though the date appears fictional) that challenges one of computing’s most foundational abstractions. Rather than debating kernel design or microkernel vs monolith trade-offs, the essay asks whether the traditional operating system — a general-purpose layer managing hardware, processes, memory, and apps — still serves a coherent purpose when users increasingly interact with AI agents, domain-specific runtimes, and embedded environments where ‘booting into an OS’ is no longer the default experience.

The post situates its argument historically: early personal computers booted directly into BASIC, offering immediacy but little structure; modern smartphones boot into locked-down app ecosystems; and today’s AI-native interfaces often bypass both OS shells and standalone apps entirely. A user asking an AI assistant to ‘book a flight and email the itinerary’ doesn’t launch Calendar, Browser, and Mail — they delegate the entire workflow. That shift reframes the OS not as a platform for apps, but as middleware increasingly invisible to end users and even developers.

This isn’t just speculation. Real-world analogs already exist: OS-like runtimes such as those used in AI agent sandboxes provide GPU-aware scheduling, durable state management, and tool orchestration — all without exposing POSIX APIs or process trees. Projects like the curated ‘awesome-agent-runtimes’ list highlight growing efforts to build lightweight, purpose-built execution environments that replace parts of traditional OS functionality for AI workloads. Meanwhile, embedded systems and IoT devices often run single-application firmware where the OS is effectively collapsed into the runtime.

Critics in the Hacker News discussion push back on different fronts. Some note that enterprise infrastructure — distributed systems handling real-world impacts — still relies heavily on mature OS abstractions for security, isolation, and observability. Others argue the real obsolescence lies not with the OS, but with the ‘app’ itself: why install a weather app when an AI can fetch, interpret, and contextualize forecasts on demand? The debate reveals a deeper tension: as computing becomes more ambient, task-oriented, and agent-mediated, the boundaries between OS, runtime, application, and service are blurring beyond recognition — and perhaps, beyond need.
