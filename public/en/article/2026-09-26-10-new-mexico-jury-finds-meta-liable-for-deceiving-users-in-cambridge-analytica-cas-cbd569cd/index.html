New Mexico jury finds Meta liable for deceiving users in Cambridge Analytica case

News time: 2026-09-26T06:07:57.132391+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a landmark verdict delivered after a decade-long legal delay, a New Mexico jury found Meta Platforms—formerly Facebook—liable for deceiving users about how their personal data was shared with third parties, specifically in connection with the Cambridge Analytica scandal. The ruling marks one of the rare instances where a major tech platform has been held legally accountable by a jury for misrepresentations to users about data privacy—not through settlement or regulatory fine, but via trial verdict.

The case centered on Facebook’s 2014 platform policies, particularly its Graph API permissions system, which allowed apps like ‘This Is Your Digital Life’—a personality quiz developed by researcher Aleksandr Kogan—to harvest not only the data of consenting users, but also the data of their friends, without those friends’ knowledge or consent. At the time, Facebook represented that users had control over what information apps could access, yet its technical design and public statements obscured the extent to which friend data was exposed. The jury concluded these representations were misleading.

Crucially, this is the only state-led case to proceed to trial on the Cambridge Analytica matter. In August 2024, Meta agreed to pay up to $18 billion to settle a multistate lawsuit focused on child safety violations; buried in that 130-page settlement was a clause releasing Meta from future liability related to the 2014–2015 Cambridge Analytica breach. All states except New Mexico and Florida signed on—though Florida declined, calling the deal insufficient. New Mexico pressed forward alone, arguing that deception about data practices warranted independent accountability.

While the verdict does not assign a specific monetary penalty (that phase will follow), it establishes a powerful precedent: that platform disclosures about data handling are subject to judicial scrutiny, and that omissions or oversimplifications in privacy messaging can constitute actionable deception under state consumer protection law. Legal experts note this may embolden other jurisdictions to pursue similar claims—and signal to developers and platforms alike that vague or technically inaccurate privacy language carries real legal risk.
