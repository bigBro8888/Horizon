Wikipedia corrected Yemen's area after 19-year error

News time: 2026-09-27T06:35:07.048887+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In late November 2024, a Substack article titled "What is the size of Yemen? (2024)" ignited quiet but widespread attention across tech and education communities. The piece revealed that for nearly two decades — since at least the early 2000s — Wikipedia had listed Yemen’s total land area as approximately 527,968 square kilometers, a figure that turned out to be significantly inflated. That number, widely repeated across textbooks, news reports, and even some academic citations, was quietly replaced in early December 2024 with a newly verified figure: 555,000 square kilometers — not smaller, but larger, and more precisely grounded in an official source.

The correction did not come from satellite imagery or modern GIS tools, but from a rediscovered 2005 Yemeni government document: the Yemeni National Atlas, published by the General Authority of Survey and Geospatial Information. Though digitized and archived, it had gone largely unnoticed by English-language editors until a contributor cross-referenced regional maps and spotted inconsistencies in coastal boundary delineations. The atlas included detailed geodetic measurements, administrative boundary revisions, and maritime zone definitions — all of which collectively supported the updated figure.

What makes this episode striking is not the magnitude of the change — roughly 5% — but its longevity and invisibility. Unlike contested geopolitical claims or disputed borders, this was a straightforward factual metric: total sovereign territory. Yet it remained unchallenged for years, even as tools like Google Maps, OpenStreetMap, and Sentinel satellite data became increasingly accessible. The error persisted not because sources were unavailable, but because no one systematically audited foundational geographic facts against primary national documentation.

The story resonated beyond geography. On Hacker News, commenters reflected on epistemic fragility: how consensus can harden around incorrect numbers when they appear in authoritative-seeming places; how digital literacy includes knowing where to look, not just how to search; and how 'common knowledge' often rests on citation chains that decay over time. As one user noted, Yemen itself never used the wrong number — its own institutions had published the correct figure in 2005. The gap wasn’t in data production, but in global knowledge curation.
