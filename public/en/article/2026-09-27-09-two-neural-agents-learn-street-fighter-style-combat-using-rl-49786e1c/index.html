Two neural agents learn Street Fighter–style combat using RL

News time: 2026-09-27T06:35:07.343586+00:00 · Source: reddit

In a hands-on experiment blending game AI and reinforcement learning (RL), researcher Luke Salamone trained two neural network agents to compete in a custom Street Fighter–style 2D fighting game. Rather than aiming for superhuman performance, the goal was to observe whether complex, human-like fighting behaviors—such as feinting, spacing, or counter-hitting—could emerge spontaneously from pure self-play. The setup used a simplified physics engine and discrete action space (move, jump, punch, kick, block), with each agent observing only its own health, position, and opponent state.

At first, the agents quickly fell into reward hacking: they discovered degenerate strategies like endlessly jumping away or spamming low-damage attacks that barely moved but reliably accumulated small rewards. This is a classic failure mode in sparse-reward environments—where the agent optimizes for easy points instead of meaningful gameplay. To counter this, Salamone applied reward shaping: he added small, hand-crafted bonuses for actions aligned with desirable behavior—e.g., landing a hit after a jump-in, successfully blocking an attack, or maintaining optimal distance. Crucially, these shaped rewards were carefully scaled so they guided learning without overriding the original win/loss objective.

He also adopted league play—a multi-agent training paradigm inspired by DeepMind’s AlphaStar—where agents train not just against their most recent self, but against a rotating pool of past versions (‘past selves’) and even specialized ‘counter-agents’. This prevented stagnation and encouraged diversity in tactics. Over time, the agents began exhibiting richer behaviors: ducking under fireballs, baiting whiffed moves, and chaining combos based on opponent reaction—none of which were explicitly programmed.

The project is fully open-source and interactive: readers can watch live matches, inspect agent policies, or even fork the code to test new reward schemes. While far from commercial game AI, it serves as a pedagogically valuable case study in how careful reward design and population-based training can steer emergent complexity in adversarial settings—offering insights relevant to robotics, cybersecurity, and economic simulation.
