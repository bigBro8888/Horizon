ICLR submissions go public at submission deadline in September

News time: 2026-09-27T06:35:07.343707+00:00 · Source: reddit

Researchers preparing their first submission to the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) often face uncertainty about its distinctive open review process — especially compared to conferences like NeurIPS or ICML. A recent Reddit post from a first-time ICLR author highlights three practical concerns: when exactly submitted papers become publicly visible, whether supplementary materials (including source code) are accessible immediately, and when reviews appear for authors and the public.

According to the official ICLR 2026 timeline, the paper submission deadline falls on September 24. Crucially, submissions — including main papers and all uploaded supplementary files — become publicly viewable on OpenReview as soon as they are submitted, not after reviews begin or during discussion periods. This means that by late September, anyone with access to OpenReview can read the full manuscript, browse appendices, and download code repositories or datasets attached as supplementary material.

Reviews themselves follow a staggered release schedule. Reviewers submit evaluations in October, but those reviews are not made public immediately. Instead, they are first shared exclusively with authors on November 11 — the same day the public discussion period begins. From November 11 through December 3, both reviews and author responses are visible to everyone on OpenReview. Importantly, reviewers’ identities remain anonymous unless they choose to sign their reviews.

This early-publication model reflects ICLR’s long-standing commitment to transparency and community engagement. However, it also introduces real trade-offs: while openness fosters accountability and early feedback, it raises concerns about idea scooping, premature replication, or misuse of released code before acceptance decisions are finalized. Authors uploading sensitive implementations or unreleased libraries must weigh these risks carefully — and consider using version-controlled, attribution-required licenses or delaying certain releases until after the decision window. For many researchers, understanding this timeline isn’t just procedural; it shapes how they prepare, anonymize, and package their work months in advance.
