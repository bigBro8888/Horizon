Alan Kay clarifies ENIAC never had a BIOS

News time: 2026-09-28T06:48:34.840242+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a concise yet authoritative Quora answer, computing pioneer Alan Kay addressed a common historical misconception: whether the ENIAC—the world’s first general-purpose electronic digital computer—had a BIOS. His reply was definitive: no. The ENIAC, completed in 1945, predated the stored-program concept entirely. Its ‘programming’ involved physically rewiring plugboards and setting dials on function tables—a labor-intensive process requiring days of technician work for each new problem. Since BIOS relies on firmware that loads instructions from persistent memory into RAM at startup, and since ENIAC lacked both programmable memory and a boot sequence, the term simply does not apply.

That said, the discussion quickly expanded beyond a yes/no answer. Commenters highlighted meaningful precursors to modern firmware. One noted that EDSAC (1949) used a small bank of rotary switches to store ‘initial orders’—a hardwired bootstrap routine that loaded a paper-tape reader and mini-assembler. Another dissected the CDC 6600’s 1962 ‘dead start panel’, revealing hand-configured octal instructions that reset I/O channels before loading further code. These were not BIOSes in the modern sense—but they were purpose-built, persistent, machine-specific startup logic.

A particularly nuanced correction came from a commenter observing that ENIAC was retrofitted in April 1948 to support stored-program operation—using its function tables as read-only program memory—allowing it to run programs encoded as dial settings. Though still far from today’s flexible firmware, this upgrade gave ENIAC rudimentary boot-like behavior for several years before decommissioning in 1955.

What makes this exchange remarkable is its depth and civility. Unlike many online tech debates, the 36-comment Hacker News thread features historians, engineers, and educators citing primary sources, distinguishing between architecture, implementation, and terminology. It reflects how precise language—like reserving ‘BIOS’ for microprocessor-era firmware—helps avoid anachronism while honoring real innovation. In an age where AI often hallucinates technical history, Kay’s direct, grounded answer—and the community’s thoughtful expansion—stands as a model of collaborative knowledge preservation.
