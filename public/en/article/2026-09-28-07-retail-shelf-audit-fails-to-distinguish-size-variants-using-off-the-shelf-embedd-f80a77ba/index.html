Retail shelf audit fails to distinguish size variants using off-the-shelf embeddings

News time: 2026-09-28T06:48:35.414465+00:00 · Source: reddit

A retail AI practitioner has hit a common but underdiscussed wall in real-world computer vision deployment: their two-stage shelf-audit system reliably detects products with YOLO, but consistently misidentifies visually near-identical SKUs—like the same soda bottle in 1.25 L versus 2 L sizes. The problem isn’t detection; it’s fine-grained identification. After cropping each detected product, the system embeds the image using state-of-the-art foundation models—including DINOV2, SigLIP2, and OpenCLIP—and performs nearest-neighbor search against a small gallery of reference photos. Yet embeddings from these models collapse across sibling SKUs, causing overlapping similarity scores and making threshold-based classification unreliable: tightening the threshold rejects true matches, while loosening it admits false ones.

The root causes are practical, not theoretical. First, crops are letterboxed to 224×224 pixels, shrinking tiny but critical text cues—like ‘1.25L’ or ‘Strawberry’ printed on labels—to near invisibility for vision models trained on generic web data. Second, reference images come from real shelf photos—not studio shots—so lighting, angle, occlusion, and label wear further degrade discriminability. Third, most SKUs have only one or two reference examples, limiting embedding robustness and ruling out data-hungry fine-tuning strategies.

The practitioner explicitly asks what’s worked in production: Did teams fine-tune embeddings on hard negatives? Add OCR as a second verification stage? Switch to hybrid architectures that fuse visual features with localized text? Or abandon global embeddings entirely in favor of task-specific classifiers? This isn’t an academic edge case—it reflects the daily reality of deploying vision systems where subtle textual distinctions carry commercial weight (e.g., inventory accuracy, planogram compliance, automated replenishment).

What makes this post especially valuable is its grounding in shipping constraints: no retraining the detector for new SKUs, minimal curation of reference data, and reliance on off-the-shelf components. It highlights a gap between foundation model capabilities—designed for broad semantic understanding—and narrow industrial tasks demanding pixel-level, text-aware discrimination. Solving it likely requires moving beyond pure embedding retrieval toward multimodal, modular, or pipeline-augmented designs—where vision, text, and domain logic cooperate rather than compete.
