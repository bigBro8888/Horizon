Former NVIDIA employee claims $1 billion in unexercised stock options

News time: 2026-09-28T06:48:34.840162+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A former NVIDIA employee has publicly claimed he was owed approximately $1 billion in unexercised stock options — a dispute rooted not in fraud or malice, but in contractual ambiguity, miscommunication, and the strict time limits governing equity compensation. In a detailed narrative posted online, the author recounts receiving a 1993 stock option grant for 25,000 shares, with a vesting schedule that — according to his interpretation — fully vested by 1996. He exercised only 15,625 shares that year, believing the remainder had expired or been forfeited. Years later, reviewing old documents, he concluded the remaining 9,375 options were still valid and exercisable — and that NVIDIA’s failure to clarify their status constituted a breach of contract.

The case hinges on subtle but consequential language in his grant notice and related communications. For instance, a 1996 letter from NVIDIA informed him he held 15,625 vested options — but did not explicitly state whether the remaining 9,375 had lapsed or remained outstanding. Under standard equity practice, stock options typically expire 7–10 years after grant unless exercised, and vesting does not automatically extend that deadline. Yet the plaintiff argues the wording created reasonable doubt about expiry, especially given NVIDIA’s internal record-keeping inconsistencies at the time.

Legal experts and HN commenters are divided. Some emphasize personal responsibility: options are time-bound instruments, and silence or ambiguity doesn’t override expiration clauses. Others note that under contract law, ambiguous terms are often construed against the drafter — here, NVIDIA — particularly when the employer controls the communication and documentation. The author confirms his legal team pursued the case on contingency, betting that discovery would impose significant cost and reputational risk on NVIDIA, even if the ultimate legal win was uncertain.

Beyond this individual case, the story serves as a stark reminder for tech employees: equity compensation is not self-executing. Vesting schedules, exercise windows, and termination clauses must be actively tracked — not assumed. And while stock options can yield life-changing returns, their value evaporates the moment the clock runs out. As one commenter dryly observed: 'You don’t own the shares until you click “exercise” — and then pay the strike price.'
