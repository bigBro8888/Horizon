How to replace aging lithium-ion batteries in bike lights

News time: 2026-09-28T06:48:34.840334+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A practical, hands-on blog post by Julia Evans documents the process of replacing worn-out lithium-ion batteries inside common rechargeable bike lights — a task many riders face after three to five years of use. Evans walks through disassembly, battery identification, sourcing replacements, and reassembly, emphasizing safety, measurement accuracy, and avoiding common pitfalls like mismatched voltage or physical dimensions. Her approach is grounded in real-world tinkering: she uses calipers to verify cell diameter and height, checks for soldered vs. plug-in connections, and cross-references markings like 'LI????77' against standardized battery nomenclature.

The post resonated strongly with hardware tinkerers and sustainability advocates, sparking an 87-comment discussion on Hacker News. Commenters shared hard-won lessons: one noted that AliExpress-sourced cells often misrepresent size or capacity, while another recounted finding a counterfeit 102660 cell (1700 mAh) inside an INFINI Lava 500 light — far below its advertised 2600 mAh. Others highlighted design differences across brands: Fenix lights allow field-replaceable batteries, whereas Cygolite models are fully potted and require careful desoldering.

Battery identification emerged as a key pain point. Several users admitted struggling to decode cryptic labels like 'LI????77'. As one commenter pointed out, Wikipedia’s battery nomenclature page clarifies that the third character must be 'R' for rechargeable, '77' indicates 7.7 mm height, and diameter can be measured directly — turning guesswork into a repeatable process. Another emphasized that matching chemistry (e.g., LiCoO₂) and nominal voltage (3.7 V) matters more than exact model numbers, though capacity (mAh) should be equal or slightly higher for safe upgrades.

Beyond technical how-tos, the discussion reflects broader themes: planned obsolescence in consumer electronics, the environmental cost of discarding functional devices, and the growing DIY repair movement. With many high-quality bike lights lasting a decade mechanically but failing due to battery degradation, extending their life via replacement isn’t just economical — it’s a tangible act of electronic stewardship. For riders, cyclists, and makers alike, this isn’t about reinventing the wheel; it’s about keeping the wheels — and the lights — rolling longer.
