Are neural architecture search and adversarial ML losing relevance

News time: 2026-09-28T06:48:35.414276+00:00 · Source: reddit

A provocative Reddit post on r/MachineLearning has reignited debate about the long-term viability of several high-profile subfields — notably neural architecture search (NAS), adversarial machine learning (AML), and fairness/ethics research. The author, u/NeighborhoodFatCat, points to striking numbers: over 3,000 NAS models proposed in just five years, consuming vast computational resources — yet the breakthrough transformer architecture was not discovered via NAS. Instead, it emerged from human intuition and iterative design. That disconnect raises a sobering question: when does automation of model design stop adding value and start consuming disproportionate effort?

The critique extends to adversarial ML, citing Nicholas Carlini’s blunt slide — '9000 papers and got nowhere' — as emblematic of diminishing returns. While AML has deep theoretical grounding and identifies real vulnerabilities (e.g., evasion and poisoning attacks), few production systems deploy robust adversarial defenses. In practice, many defenses are brittle, easily circumvented, or too costly to maintain — and some argue they mainly arm adversaries with blueprints for new attacks. Meanwhile, fairness and bias research faces a different kind of pressure: as AI safety concerns escalate — including existential risk discourse — critics ask whether fine-grained fairness interventions remain tractable or even ethically primary when broader societal disruptions (e.g., labor displacement) loom larger.

This isn’t a dismissal of foundational work. NAS contributed key ideas now embedded in modern tooling — like weight-sharing and one-shot supernet training — and AML has informed security-aware ML pipelines in high-stakes domains like healthcare and finance. But the post challenges the scale and trajectory of investment: should thousands of papers and GPU-years continue flowing into areas where real-world adoption remains sparse? It also highlights a generational tension in ML culture — between incremental academic contribution and urgent engineering impact.

For newcomers entering the field, the underlying message is pragmatic: research relevance isn’t static. It shifts with hardware advances, deployment realities, and societal priorities. What once seemed essential — like SVMs or Markov chains — may recede not because it’s wrong, but because its niche has narrowed. The real question isn’t whether a subfield is ‘dead’, but whether its current form still serves the most pressing problems — and whether redirecting energy toward AI safety, interpretability, or systems-level robustness might yield higher leverage.
