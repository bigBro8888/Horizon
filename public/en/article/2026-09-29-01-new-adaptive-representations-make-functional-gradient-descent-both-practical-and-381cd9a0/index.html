New adaptive representations make functional gradient descent both practical and provably convergent

News time: 2026-09-29T06:51:52.965812+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new NeurIPS-accepted paper introduces a theoretically grounded framework called 'functional gradient descent with adaptive representations'—a method that finally bridges the gap between the strong theoretical guarantees of functional optimization and its real-world implementation. Functional gradient descent (FGD) operates directly in infinite-dimensional function space, offering cleaner convergence analysis and often superior empirical performance compared to neural networks. Yet for years, it remained largely impractical: because functional gradients cannot be computed exactly, approximations are unavoidable—but naive discretizations (e.g., fixed basis expansions or pointwise updates) typically break convergence, leading algorithms to settle at suboptimal solutions.

The core insight of the work is to formalize when and how an approximation scheme preserves correctness. The authors define a broad class of 'adaptive representations'—parameterized, data-informed approximations that dynamically adjust their structure (e.g., basis functions, support locations, or rank) during optimization. Crucially, these representations satisfy a 'gradient consistency' condition: as the algorithm progresses, the approximate functional gradient remains aligned with the true one in a way that ensures descent toward the global minimizer—not just a local stationary point. This isn’t heuristic tuning; it’s built into the design and backed by rigorous proofs of global convergence under mild assumptions.

Empirically, the resulting algorithms outperform comparable neural networks by up to tenfold across diverse tasks—including regression on synthetic benchmarks, PDE solution learning, and low-rank function approximation. Unlike many theory-heavy ML papers, this one ships with immediately implementable algorithms: no black-box solvers, no unverifiable assumptions, and no reliance on infinite-width limits or kernel regimes. The authors release a clean arXiv preprint (2606.16926) and emphasize that while the framework is nascent, it opens a path toward optimization methods that are simultaneously interpretable, scalable, and mathematically trustworthy.

This work matters not only for functional optimization but also for how we think about representation learning itself. Rather than treating representation as a static input preprocessing step—or as an emergent property of deep nets—the paper treats it as an active, co-adapted component of the optimizer. That shift in perspective could influence future designs of adaptive architectures, Bayesian optimizers, and even foundation model training strategies where functional priors play a role.
