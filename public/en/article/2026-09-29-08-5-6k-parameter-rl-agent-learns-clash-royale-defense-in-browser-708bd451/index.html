5.6k-parameter RL agent learns Clash Royale defense in browser

News time: 2026-09-29T06:51:52.966089+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new open-source demonstration shows that a remarkably small reinforcement learning (RL) agent—just 5,629 parameters—can learn near-optimal defensive card placement in Clash Royale, all running directly inside a web browser. The interactive demo, hosted at https://itzik123.github.io/ClashRoyaleAi/lab/, lets users watch real-time training as the agent refines its decisions: given an attacker spawning randomly on the enemy side, it selects both a cell on the defender’s side and a delay (0–5 seconds) before deploying a single defensive card. Its reward is defined simply as the fraction of tower damage prevented compared to having no defense at all.

What makes this demo unusual is its full-stack technical rigor. The game logic runs in a hand-optimized C++ engine compiled to WebAssembly (WASM), ensuring simulation speed and numerical fidelity—so much so that the deployment pipeline verifies bit-for-bit equivalence between the WASM and native builds. Gradients are computed manually in plain JavaScript; no deep learning framework is used. Training uses the REINFORCE algorithm—a classic policy gradient method—with per-spawn baselines and an entropy bonus that linearly anneals from 0.1 to 0.005 over 10,000 trials. This careful tuning helps avoid local optima, such as the ‘lane placement’ trap in Giant vs Cannon matchups, where 5 out of 6 training runs got stuck at ~75% of optimal performance until annealing was applied.

The demo also includes a rigorous benchmark: a brute-force optimum computed by exhaustively evaluating every legal cell–delay combination (up to ~300,000 rollouts per matchup). This allows precise quantification of the policy’s gap from perfection—something rarely visible in browser-based RL demos. Notably, some pairings remain challenging: Battle Ram vs Valkyrie, for example, never exceeded 55% of the optimum across all tested configurations. The authors emphasize this is intentionally a minimal version of the full game—no elixir management, no multi-card hands, no recurrent state—designed not for competitive strength but for transparency and pedagogical clarity.

Beyond novelty, the project embodies a growing trend toward lightweight, interpretable, and deployable RL. By avoiding heavy frameworks and embracing WASM + hand-written math, it proves that meaningful RL research and education can happen client-side—no GPU servers, no Python backends, just a modern browser. It’s a compelling case study in what’s possible when systems thinking meets pedagogy—and a reminder that sometimes, smaller models trained with care reveal more than larger ones trained at scale.
