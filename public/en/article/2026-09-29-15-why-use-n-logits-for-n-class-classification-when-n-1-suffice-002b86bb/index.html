Why use N logits for N-class classification when N−1 suffice

News time: 2026-09-29T06:51:52.965871+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent post on the r/MachineLearning subreddit raises a deceptively simple but conceptually rich question: if the softmax output is constrained to sum to one — meaning it lives on an (N−1)-dimensional probability simplex — why does the standard neural network output layer compute all N logits independently? The author points out that only N−1 of those logits are statistically identifiable; the Nth can be reconstructed as the negative sum of the others, enforcing a zero-sum constraint across logits. This reparameterization eliminates one redundant degree of freedom from the final linear layer — effectively reducing the number of trainable weights by N (one per output neuron) in a fully connected head.

This isn’t just theoretical elegance. In statistical modeling — especially with compositional or microbiome data — zero-sum constraints on coefficients are well-established to resolve identifiability issues caused by arbitrary reference points or additive ambiguities. Similarly, in Bayesian modeling tools like Stan’s brms package, sum-to-zero priors are routinely applied to categorical predictors to avoid collinearity and ensure clean interpretation. The core idea is that without such constraints, models suffer from parameter non-identifiability: different weight configurations can produce identical softmax outputs, confusing optimization and harming generalization.

Practically, though, adoption in deep learning remains rare. Standard libraries like PyTorch and TensorFlow implement softmax with unconstrained logits, and most training pipelines rely on implicit regularization (e.g., weight decay) or initialization heuristics rather than explicit constraints. The Reddit poster speculates this might marginally accelerate convergence — but acknowledges the benefit is likely negligible in large-scale settings where overparameterization is benign and gradients remain well-behaved. Still, the question exposes a subtle mismatch between foundational statistical principles and default deep learning practice.

The deeper implication lies in how we think about model design. Softmax is often treated as a black-box normalization step, but its input space geometry matters. Enforcing zero-sum logits makes the output layer’s parameter space align precisely with the intrinsic dimensionality of the target distribution — a move toward more parsimonious, interpretable, and theoretically grounded architectures. While unlikely to overhaul industry practice overnight, it invites reflection on which ‘defaults’ deserve reconsideration — especially as efficiency, transparency, and robustness gain priority alongside raw accuracy.
