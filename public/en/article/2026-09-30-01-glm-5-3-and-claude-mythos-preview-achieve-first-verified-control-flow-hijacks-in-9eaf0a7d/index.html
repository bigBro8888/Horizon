GLM-5.3 and Claude Mythos Preview achieve first verified control flow hijacks in binary exploitation

News time: 2026-09-30T06:39:48.782040+00:00 · Source: rss

In a landmark assessment by Anthropic’s Frontier Red Team, two next-generation large language models — GLM-5.3 and Claude Mythos Preview — have demonstrated, for the first time, the ability to autonomously execute full control flow hijacks against real binary programs. Tested on a rigorous internal benchmark of 100 hand-selected binary exploitation tasks, GLM-5.3 succeeded in 4% of attempts, while Claude Mythos Preview achieved a 6% success rate. Crucially, earlier models — including Claude Opus 4.6 and GLM-5.2 — failed across all 100 trials, registering zero successes. This non-zero performance marks a clear threshold crossing: not just incremental improvement, but the emergence of a qualitatively new capability in AI-driven offensive security.

Control flow hijacking is a foundational technique in real-world exploit development. It involves manipulating a program’s execution path — for example, by overwriting return addresses on the stack or chaining together small code snippets (gadgets) via return-oriented programming (ROP) — to divert control to attacker-controlled logic. Until now, this required deep domain expertise in assembly, memory layout, and mitigation bypasses. That LLMs can now perform parts of this end-to-end — from analyzing disassembled binaries to generating working exploit primitives — signals a profound shift in the automation frontier of cyber offense.

The benchmark used was internal and not publicly released, but its design reflects standard red teaming rigor: tasks were drawn from realistic, memory-corruption-vulnerable binaries, likely involving stack overflows, heap use-after-frees, or similar vulnerabilities. Neither model operates in isolation; both are presumed to be integrated with tool-use scaffolds, symbolic executors, or debugging environments — yet their success still depends critically on reasoning about low-level program behavior, constraints, and side effects. Notably, Anthropic has chosen not to release Claude Mythos Preview publicly, citing its cybersecurity capabilities as a key reason — a rare and telling policy decision.

This milestone does not mean AI can now reliably write zero-day exploits at scale. Success rates remain low, reproducibility is unconfirmed, and real-world deployment would require extensive human oversight, environment adaptation, and evasion of modern defenses like ASLR, stack canaries, and Control Flow Integrity (CFI). Still, it underscores an urgent reality: the gap between theoretical AI safety concerns and tangible offensive utility is narrowing rapidly. For defenders, red teams, and policymakers, the implication is clear — AI-powered exploitation is no longer speculative. It is empirically observable, incrementally improving, and already being treated as a material risk by leading labs.
