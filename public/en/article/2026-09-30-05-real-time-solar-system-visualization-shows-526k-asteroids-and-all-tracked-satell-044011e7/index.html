Real-time solar system visualization shows 526k asteroids and all tracked satellites

News time: 2026-09-30T06:39:49.198009+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new open web application called Space.bl2.net offers a stunning, real-time, to-scale 3D model of the solar system — rendered entirely in the browser using WebGL2. Unlike simplified or artistically stylized space visualizations, this tool displays celestial bodies and artificial objects at their true relative distances and sizes (though scaled for visibility), updated daily with authoritative orbital data. Users can zoom from Earth’s surface to interplanetary space, scrub through time with a slider, and watch spacecraft like Europa Clipper approach for gravity assists — all without installing software or plugins.

The site draws from three trusted sources: satellite positions come from CelesTrak’s Two-Line Element (TLE) sets, processed via the SGP4 orbital model; asteroid and comet trajectories are pulled from NASA JPL’s Small-Body Database (SBDB); and deep-space mission paths — such as those of planetary probes — are computed using high-precision ephemerides from JPL Horizons. This combination ensures scientific fidelity while remaining accessible to anyone with a modern browser.

Behind the scenes, performance is handled carefully: orbit propagation runs in background web workers to avoid freezing the UI, and the ~30 MB asteroid dataset loads asynchronously. The use of WebGL2 — rather than older WebGL1 — enables efficient rendering of hundreds of thousands of objects simultaneously, supporting features like instanced drawing and integer shader math that are essential for large-scale astronomical simulations.

While users praise its utility — one commenter tracked Europa Clipper’s imminent Earth flyby — others raised thoughtful questions about coverage gaps, such as missing named minor planets (e.g., asteroid 31689 Sebmellen). The developer acknowledged the limitation: the current asteroid set is a curated subset selected for computational feasibility and data completeness, not an exhaustive catalog. Still, the project stands out as a rare example of production-grade, real-time astrodynamics made publicly explorable on the open web.
