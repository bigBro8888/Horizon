Backblaze data shows HDDs now last nearly 10 years with 5% failure rate

News time: 2026-09-30T06:39:49.198076+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Backblaze’s latest quarterly drive reliability report — covering Q2 2026 — delivers a striking update to long-held assumptions about hard disk drive (HDD) longevity. Analyzing over 300,000 drives across its global storage infrastructure, Backblaze found that drives reaching the 10-year mark now exhibit an average annual failure rate of just ~5%. This represents a dramatic improvement from earlier eras: in 2013, drives hit ~14% failure by just under 4 years; by 2021, that same 14% threshold was pushed out to nearly 8 years; and now, in 2025–2026 data, even 10-year-old drives remain remarkably resilient.

This shift isn’t incremental — it’s structural. The traditional IT rule of thumb — “replace HDDs every 5 years to avoid risk” — was built on decades of observed wear patterns and vendor MTBF estimates. But real-world usage at scale, as captured by Backblaze’s consistent methodology (drives deployed, monitored, and retired without selective curation), reveals that modern HDDs, especially those designed for 24/7 operation like NAS and enterprise models, are far more durable than previously assumed. The data suggests many drives operate reliably well beyond their warranty periods, challenging both operational policies and procurement cycles.

Importantly, this longevity doesn’t come without caveats. Community feedback highlights variability: some users report catastrophic dual-drive failures in NAS setups, while others note sharp price increases — one commenter cited a 70% jump in WD Red 6TB pricing over 18 months. Others question the practical utility of ultra-high-capacity drives when sequential read speeds haven’t meaningfully improved, making full-drive rebuilds or backups prohibitively slow in RAID or archival contexts. Still, the aggregate trend is unambiguous: HDDs are maturing into long-haul workhorses, not short-lived consumables.

For cloud providers, colocation operators, and self-hosted infrastructure teams, these findings invite serious re-evaluation. Extending replacement cycles could reduce hardware CAPEX, e-waste, and operational overhead — but only if failure prediction and monitoring keep pace. It also reshapes cost-per-terabyte calculations over time: a drive lasting twice as long effectively halves its amortized hardware cost, even amid inflation. Ultimately, Backblaze hasn’t just published stats — it’s quietly revised the timeline for one of computing’s most foundational components.
