Spiral and concentric neural waves observed during memory tasks

News time: 2026-10-01T07:09:55.647121+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Scientists have captured striking new patterns of electrical activity in the human brain—spiral and concentric traveling waves—that emerge during memory encoding and retrieval. Using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG), researchers recorded directly from the brains of epilepsy patients undergoing clinical monitoring, achieving millisecond temporal precision and high spatial resolution. Unlike traditional EEG, which measures activity through the scalp, iEEG electrodes are placed directly on or within brain tissue, allowing scientists to track how neural signals propagate across cortical surfaces in real time.

The study revealed that these waves are not simple oscillations but dynamic, geometrically structured phenomena: some radiate outward like ripples from a stone dropped in water (source waves), others converge inward toward a focal point (sink waves), and many twist into vortex-like spirals. These patterns appeared consistently during memory tasks—particularly when participants were actively holding or recalling information—and were most prominent in frontal and temporal lobe regions linked to working memory and long-term storage.

Critically, the waves weren’t just passive byproducts. Their timing and direction correlated with the flow of information between brain areas: for example, backward-propagating waves traveled from sensory cortex toward higher-order association areas just before successful memory recall, suggesting they may help route or gate relevant signals. This challenges the long-held view that such large-scale waves are mere epiphenomena—side effects of local neuronal firing—instead positioning them as potential orchestrators of distributed computation.

Still, important caveats remain. The data come exclusively from small cohorts of people with epilepsy, whose brains may differ neurophysiologically from neurotypical individuals. Moreover, iEEG samples only a tiny fraction of the cortex, and wave detection depends heavily on signal processing methods that can amplify or obscure subtle patterns. As one researcher noted in the original article, "The action is in the cells"—meaning synaptic currents drive neural communication, and whether extracellular waves themselves exert causal influence remains unproven. Future work will need targeted perturbations—like brief electrical stimulation timed to wave phases—to test causality directly.
