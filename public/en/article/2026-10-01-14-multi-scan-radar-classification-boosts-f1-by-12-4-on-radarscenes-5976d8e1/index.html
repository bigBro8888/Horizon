Multi-scan radar classification boosts F1 by 12.4% on RadarScenes

News time: 2026-10-01T07:09:56.013398+00:00 · Source: reddit

Radar perception for autonomous driving faces a fundamental challenge: raw radar point clouds are extremely sparse. On the RadarScenes dataset, each labeled object averages just 2.9 radar points per scan — far fewer than LiDAR or camera inputs. Worse, a single snapshot cannot capture how an object’s radar signature evolves over time. Pedestrians, for example, produce distinctive micro-Doppler shifts from swinging arms and legs; cars and trucks exhibit characteristic fluctuations in radar cross-section (RCS) as their orientation relative to the sensor changes. These temporal dynamics carry rich class-specific information — but only if the system can reliably associate measurements across scans.

To address this, researcher Bruno Pinto extended a prior single-scan classifier into a multi-scan framework using RadarScenes’ built-in track_id annotations. Instead of treating each scan independently, his method accumulates up to 20 consecutive observations of the same tracked object — regardless of which of the vehicle’s four radars detects it — into a unified, odometry-corrected coordinate buffer. Crucially, the buffer is causal and streaming-compatible: every new scan updates it and triggers a fresh prediction, with no reliance on future frames.

The architecture uses a frozen single-scan encoder (based on DeepReflecs) to extract features from each frame, then explores three aggregation strategies: simple point pooling (ignoring scan order), a causal GRU (modeling sequence order), and a fusion of GRU state with pooled embedding. Results show that simply aggregating 20 scans’ points — without any temporal modeling — lifts macro F1 from 0.7370 to 0.8613 (+12.4%). Adding a GRU pushes it further to 0.8895 (+2.8% over pooling), while fusion yields negligible gain. Ablations reveal that stronger sequence models — Transformers, state-space models, point-level attention — all land within a narrow 0.03 band (0.86–0.89), suggesting the bottleneck lies not in temporal modeling choice, but in the quality of per-scan representations.

The work underscores a pragmatic insight for real-world radar ML: before optimizing complex sequence architectures, first ensure robust observation accumulation. Freezing the per-scan encoder — rather than fine-tuning end-to-end — actually improves performance slightly, confirming that high-fidelity single-frame features are foundational. The full report, including confusion matrices and coordinate-frame analysis, is publicly available on GitHub.
