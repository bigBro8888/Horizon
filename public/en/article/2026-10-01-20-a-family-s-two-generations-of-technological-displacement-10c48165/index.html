A family’s two generations of technological displacement

News time: 2026-10-01T07:09:55.647369+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a quietly powerful personal essay, Manuel Darcemont traces how technology reshaped his family’s livelihood across more than a century — first displacing his great-great-grandfather from farming in 19th-century France, then displacing himself and peers from software engineering jobs today. The piece avoids grand predictions or policy prescriptions. Instead, it centers on quiet losses: the silence where ancestral stories once lived; the disorientation of seeing one’s hard-won skills suddenly devalued; the emotional weight of adaptation that no productivity metric captures.

Darcemont doesn’t equate the two eras mechanically — he notes key differences in scale, speed, and social safety nets. Yet he finds resonance in the human experience: the confusion after job loss, the pressure to reinvent oneself without clear pathways, and the way identity can unravel when work — long tied to dignity, community, and self-worth — vanishes. His great-great-grandfather didn’t become a ‘digital farmer’; he became a laborer in a factory. Darcemont himself isn’t becoming a plumber overnight — he’s wrestling with uncertainty, not just logistics.

The essay gains depth from its refusal to offer comfort. When readers ask, “What should developers do now?”, he replies: there is no tidy answer — only shared vulnerability. This honesty resonated deeply on Hacker News, where commenters debated whether AI-driven displacement mirrors past industrial transitions or signals something unprecedented. Some cited historical precedent — like the 70% of Americans once employed in agriculture — while others warned that white-collar automation may lack parallel reemployment channels.

What makes the piece stand out is its moral restraint. It neither romanticizes the past nor sells techno-optimism. It treats both generations not as data points in an economic model, but as people navigating rupture. In an era saturated with AI hype and panic, this grounded, intergenerational witness offers something rare: empathy without agenda, memory without nostalgia.
