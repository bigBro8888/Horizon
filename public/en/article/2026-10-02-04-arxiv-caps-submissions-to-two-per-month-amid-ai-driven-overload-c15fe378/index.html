arXiv caps submissions to two per month amid AI-driven overload

News time: 2026-10-02T06:58:44.044144+00:00 · Source: hackernews

arXiv, the foundational open-access preprint server for physics, mathematics, computer science, and related fields, has introduced a strict new submission policy effective October 2026. Starting this month, all submitters—regardless of institutional affiliation or seniority—are limited to no more than two new submissions per calendar month and may maintain at most three active submissions (i.e., papers under moderation or awaiting endorsement) at any time. The change follows a dramatic fourfold increase in submission volume since 2016: from 9,869 papers in September 2016 to over 40,363 in September 2024—a surge that generated nearly 9,000 support tickets in a single month and strained moderation capacity to its breaking point.

The policy update is explicitly tied to two converging pressures: explosive growth in overall submissions and a sharp rise in low-value, AI-generated manuscripts. According to arXiv’s official blog, dense, formulaic, and often technically shallow papers produced by large language models now constitute a growing share of incoming submissions—many lacking meaningful novelty, reproducibility, or scholarly intent. Unlike peer-reviewed journals, arXiv relies on volunteer moderators and lightweight moderation (endorsement + basic sanity checks), making it especially vulnerable to automation-driven flooding.

Importantly, the limits apply per submitter—not per author—so collaborative teams retain flexibility: a paper with ten co-authors counts as one submission against the submitter’s quota. This design aims to balance fairness across individual researchers and large labs alike. Still, some users report severe delays: one commenter noted an 18-day hold on a submission, prompting a switch to alternative repositories like HAL, where posting takes less than a day. Others praise the move as long overdue, arguing that unchecked AI-assisted output threatens arXiv’s core mission as a trusted, human-curated knowledge infrastructure.

The broader implications extend well beyond arXiv. As preprint servers become de facto publication venues—especially in fast-moving fields like AI—this policy signals a critical inflection point: sustainability now requires deliberate gatekeeping, even in open systems. It also raises urgent questions about accountability, transparency, and the definition of scholarly contribution in an era where generating plausible-looking research text is trivial. Whether other repositories follow suit—or whether new AI-dedicated channels emerge—will shape how science communicates in the next decade.
