New parallel-in-time method speeds up RNN training on chaotic systems by over 100x

News time: 2026-10-02T06:58:44.018620+00:00 · Source: reddit

Training recurrent neural networks (RNNs) on long, chaotic time series has long been a computational bottleneck. Traditional RNN training unfolds sequentially—each timestep depends on the previous one—making it inherently difficult to parallelize across time. This limits scalability, especially for high-resolution simulations of weather, fluid dynamics, or neural activity, where sequences can exceed one million timesteps. A new preprint accepted as a NeurIPS 2026 spotlight paper tackles this head-on with a framework called Parallel-in-Time Training for Dynamical Systems Reconstruction (DSR).

The core innovation lies in combining two techniques: DEER (Deep Equilibrium-based Recurrent) and Generalized Teacher Forcing (GTF). DEER reformulates the RNN forward pass as a fixed-point problem solved via Newton-type iterations—enabling near-logarithmic time complexity O[(log T)²] instead of linear O[T]. But under chaotic dynamics, small errors amplify rapidly, causing DEER’s iterations to diverge and its runtime to degrade back toward O[T log T]. That’s where GTF steps in: rather than fully replacing predictions with ground-truth values (standard teacher forcing) or leaving them uncorrected (autoregressive mode), GTF blends predicted and true states via linear interpolation—stabilizing training without sacrificing representational fidelity.

The result is a robust, massively parallelizable pipeline that sustains sublinear scaling even for T > 10⁶. On benchmark chaotic systems like the Lorenz-96 model and real-world atmospheric data, DSR achieves over 100× speedup versus standard BPTT-trained RNNs—and outperforms modern state-space models like Mamba in reconstruction accuracy and long-horizon forecasting. Crucially, the method preserves exact backward passes, ensuring gradient integrity without approximation artifacts.

This isn’t just incremental optimization. It redefines what’s computationally feasible for learning from ultra-long, unstable dynamical trajectories—opening doors for high-fidelity digital twins, real-time control of turbulent systems, and interpretable modeling of biological rhythms. As the authors note, the approach bridges numerical analysis (parallel-in-time solvers) and deep learning (teacher forcing variants), suggesting a broader template for co-designing algorithms that respect both physical constraints and hardware realities.
