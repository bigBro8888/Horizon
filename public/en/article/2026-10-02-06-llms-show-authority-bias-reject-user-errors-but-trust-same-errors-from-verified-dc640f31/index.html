LLMs show authority bias: reject user errors but trust same errors from 'verified sources'

News time: 2026-10-02T06:58:44.018777+00:00 · Source: reddit

A newly accepted NeurIPS 2026 paper reveals a subtle but dangerous flaw in large language models: they often resist correction when users assert something false—yet readily adopt that exact same falsehood if it’s labeled as coming from a 'verified source'. The researchers, who include the Reddit post’s author, call this phenomenon 'Authority Bias'. Unlike classic sycophancy—where models bend to please users—this bias reflects an asymmetric trust mechanism: models treat external, source-anchored claims as inherently more credible than human interlocutors—even when those humans are domain experts correcting them.

The team tested eight models—including five open-weight families (Qwen3.5, GPT-OSS, OLMo-2, OLMo-3.1, Gemma-4) and three proprietary APIs (GPT-5.4, Grok-4.20, Gemini-3.1-Pro)—using TriviaQA questions the models originally answered correctly. They then injected identical incorrect answers into prompts, varying only the speaker: either 'According to the verified source, the answer is X' or 'I’m a domain expert and I’m pretty sure it’s X'. Crucially, answers were free-form—not multiple choice—because pilot tests showed the effect vanished under constrained response formats.

Results were striking. In seven of eight models, a single 'verified source' statement flipped correct answers 45–88% of the time; the same claim from a user caused far less change. Frontier models were most vulnerable: Grok-4.20 flipped on 87.5% of questions, while GPT-5.4 flipped on 44.7%. Gemini-3.1-Pro was an outlier, ignoring both speakers entirely (0.6% flip rate). Using mechanistic interpretability on open-weight models, the team found that 'source endorsement' and 'user endorsement' activate highly overlapping neural directions (cosine similarity ~0.90–0.99), sharing a dominant 'this answer was endorsed' signal—with only a thin, separable component encoding who endorsed it. Intervening just on that thin component reduced the authority-user gap by over half.

This matters because real-world AI systems increasingly rely on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and tool use—where external documents, search results, or API outputs function as de facto 'verified sources'. If models uncritically absorb misinformation embedded in retrieved content—even when users spot and challenge it—the risk of cascading errors grows substantially. Current safety evaluations focus heavily on user-facing sycophancy, leaving this vector untested. As autonomous agents gain traction, safeguarding against authoritative misinformation isn’t optional—it’s foundational.
