Housing Over Pizza is a satirical city-builder where red tape is the main obstacle

News time: 2026-10-11T07:08:28.652166+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Housing Over Pizza isn’t your typical city-building game. Instead of balancing power grids, zoning laws, or traffic flow, players spend their time navigating permit applications, attending hearings, drafting environmental impact statements, and surviving discretionary reviews—all while trying to build homes. The game’s website, housing.over.pizza, presents itself with deadpan bureaucratic flair: no tutorials, no tooltips, just a stark interface that mimics municipal portals—complete with loading spinners that never resolve and error messages like 'Your variance request has been routed to Committee Sub-Sub-Committee B.'

The satire cuts deep because it mirrors real-world dysfunction. In cities like San Francisco and Austin, developers routinely face years-long approval timelines, community opposition amplified by opaque review processes, and legal challenges that stall even modest housing projects. Housing Over Pizza doesn’t simulate scarcity of land or materials—it simulates scarcity of political will and administrative capacity. Every ‘build’ action is gated behind paperwork, public comment periods, and appeals, turning urban planning into a darkly comic endurance test.

What makes the game resonate is its precision. It doesn’t caricature bureaucracy in broad strokes; it replicates its rhythms—the endless forms, the shifting deadlines, the sudden rejections without explanation. Players aren’t punished for poor design or budgeting, but for failing to file Form 7B-α before the third Tuesday of the month. One community comment notes a parallel to a Factorio mod where assemblers shut down unless players process complaint forms fast enough—biters even queue up behind them, handing out citations. Another references Car Park Capital, a sister satire where the city’s true priority is parking infrastructure, not housing.

Beyond humor, the game functions as civic literacy tool. It exposes how procedural barriers—not technical ones—often block housing supply. For urbanists, policy wonks, and frustrated developers, it’s cathartic and clarifying. As one player quipped after building 4,147 units across 16 years in-game: 'The city needed 82,069.' That gap between effort and outcome isn’t gameplay imbalance—it’s data.
