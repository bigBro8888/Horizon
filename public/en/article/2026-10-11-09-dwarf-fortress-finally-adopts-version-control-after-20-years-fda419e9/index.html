Dwarf Fortress finally adopts version control after 20 years

News time: 2026-10-11T07:08:28.253007+00:00 · Source: rss

For over two decades, Dwarf Fortress stood as a quiet anomaly in software development: a massively complex, deeply simulated world-building game—700,000 lines of C++ code, evolving since 2002—built almost entirely by one person, Tarn Adams, without version control. Its legendary resistance to tools like Git wasn’t oversight or ignorance; it was deliberate, even philosophical. As Adams explained in 2013, he disliked the feeling of code vanishing into a 'black box thingy' with no immediate payoff. By 2016, he joked that knowing what version control was might make developers yell at him—but he still didn’t use it.

That changed quietly around 2023, coinciding with the game’s official Steam release and commercial breakthrough. In a Game Developer interview that year, Adams confirmed: 'I've got Discord channels to check and version control stuff to use now, so the process is definitely a little more involved.' This wasn’t a flashy announcement—it slipped in amid discussions about optimization, multiplayer coordination, and scaling support for a suddenly much larger player base. The shift reflects not just technical necessity, but a broader evolution: from solo, insular craftsmanship to collaborative, maintainable engineering.

Version control isn’t just about undoing mistakes. It enables safe experimentation, parallel work on features and fixes, clear change history, and onboarding for new contributors—even if those contributors are just future versions of the same developer. For a project where a single bug could erase weeks of simulated world history, having reliable rollback capability matters deeply. And while Adams never needed it for daily survival, the Steam launch brought real-world pressures: coordinated patches, public beta testing, third-party mod tooling, and community-driven bug reports requiring precise repro steps.

The milestone isn’t about ‘modernizing’ for its own sake. It’s about sustainability. Dwarf Fortress is no longer just a passion project—it’s a living ecosystem, backed by revenue, modding communities, and official ports (including a recent Nintendo Switch release). Adopting version control signals that its long-term health now depends less on heroic individual effort and more on repeatable, auditable, shareable practices. As one developer put it on Hacker News: 'Linear git history is infinitely better than no version control—even on small solo projects.' For Dwarf Fortress, that line was crossed—not with fanfare, but with quiet, pragmatic resolve.
