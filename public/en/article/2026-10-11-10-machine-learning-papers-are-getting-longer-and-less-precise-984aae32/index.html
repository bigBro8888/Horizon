Machine learning papers are getting longer and less precise

News time: 2026-10-11T07:08:28.942860+00:00 · Source: reddit

A growing number of machine learning researchers are expressing concern over the declining clarity and rising verbosity in academic writing. In a widely discussed Reddit post on r/MachineLearning, a user—writing under the handle /u/NeighborhoodFatCat—describes struggling to digest modern ML research not because of mathematical complexity, but because papers have ballooned in length and linguistic looseness. They cite examples like the 40+ page arXiv paper 'Qwen: A Large Language Model with Multilingual Support' (arXiv:2302.13971), noting that such length is now common even outside LLM work—for instance, in optimization theory and systems-oriented ML.

The critique extends beyond sheer page count. The author points to an increasing reliance on informal, conversational phrasing—phrases like "a lot of," "we don’t feel," or "it feels like"—in venues that should uphold scientific rigor, such as the Transformer Circuits blog (transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework). While those posts include mathematical notation, their prose blurs the line between exposition and speculation, making it hard to distinguish established claims from intuitive hunches. Worse, key definitions often go unexplained, leaving readers to infer meaning from context—or guess altogether.

Another troubling trend highlighted is the substitution of precise mathematical reasoning with high-level block diagrams. These visual abstractions may aid intuition, but they frequently omit implementation-critical details: parameterization choices, assumptions about data distributions, or boundary conditions for convergence. As a result, translating diagrams into working code or reproducible experiments often demands substantial reverse-engineering—and sometimes outright invention.

This isn’t just a stylistic gripe. When papers grow longer without proportional gains in precision, accessibility suffers—especially for non-native English speakers, students, and researchers from resource-constrained institutions. It also undermines reproducibility: if core ideas aren’t stated unambiguously, independent verification becomes guesswork. The post resonates because it names a quiet crisis—one where communication norms lag behind technical progress, threatening the field’s self-correcting mechanisms.
