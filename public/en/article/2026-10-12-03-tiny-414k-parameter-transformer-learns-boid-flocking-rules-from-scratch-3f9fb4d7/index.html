Tiny 414k-parameter transformer learns boid flocking rules from scratch

News time: 2026-10-12T07:18:49.567971+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher trained an exceptionally small transformer—just 414,000 parameters—to predict the motion of simulated bird flocks without ever being told the underlying rules. Built from scratch in PyTorch and run on a laptop CPU, the model processes each bird as a token and uses only two attention layers. It was trained exclusively on trajectory data from a 12-bird boids simulation: positions, velocities, and accelerations over time. Remarkably, the model not only reproduced flock behavior with near-perfect fidelity (R² = 0.990–0.994) on unseen clips but also generalized to flocks of 50 birds—despite never seeing more than 12 during training.

What makes this work especially compelling is interpretability. Using linear probes—simple classifiers trained on hidden states—the author extracted three classic boid rules: cohesion (staying near neighbors), alignment (matching neighbor velocity direction), and separation (avoiding collisions). Cohesion was already partially encoded before training began (probe R² ≈ 0.85), while alignment emerged robustly after the first attention layer—but vanished when the second layer’s attention was ablated to self-only. This suggests alignment is causally implemented across birds via inter-token attention, not just within individual representations.

The experiment also revealed subtle pitfalls in causal probing. In one early run, the model appeared to ignore the flock entirely—relying instead on autocorrelation in the input sequence (e.g., using the previous timestep’s output as a shortcut). With 8-timestep history, even a trivial baseline that saw only one bird’s past motion outperformed the full transformer. That highlights how architectural choices and input design can unintentionally enable non-flocking strategies—even in models that later succeed.

This isn’t just about birds. It’s a controlled testbed for neural-symbolic emergence: showing how interpretable, compositional, rule-like behavior can arise in minimal transformers—and how we can isolate, measure, and intervene on those behaviors. The code and interactive demo are publicly available, inviting replication and extension into other emergent coordination tasks—from robot swarms to multi-agent reinforcement learning.
