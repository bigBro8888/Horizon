Passkeys erode user control through mandatory device binding and remote attestation

News time: 2026-10-12T07:18:48.588517+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A provocative new essay titled 'Passkeys are a trap' argues that the industry’s push toward passwordless authentication is not just a convenience upgrade—but a quiet consolidation of power into the hands of device vendors and platform providers. While passkeys promise stronger phishing resistance and smoother logins, the author contends they do so at an unacceptable cost: the normalization of remote attestation, irreversible device binding, and diminished user agency over identity infrastructure.

The core concern centers on remote attestation—a WebAuthn feature that allows websites to request cryptographic proof about a user’s authenticator (e.g., whether it’s a genuine Apple Secure Enclave or a YubiKey). Though optional in theory, the essay warns that attestation is increasingly being treated as a de facto requirement—especially by large platforms seeking to enforce hardware-backed trust. Once enabled during registration, this capability lets services infer device models, firmware versions, and even security posture—data that can feed surveillance, discrimination, or vendor lock-in.

Equally troubling is device binding: many passkey implementations store private keys exclusively in hardware-secured enclaves (like Apple’s Secure Enclave or Android’s StrongBox) and prohibit backup or export. Unlike traditional passwords or even FIDO2 security keys, these credentials cannot be moved, copied, or restored from encrypted backups. Users who lose their phone—or switch ecosystems—may permanently lose access to accounts unless relying on cloud-synced variants, which introduce new attack surfaces and third-party dependencies.

Critics in the Hacker News discussion echo these concerns with technical precision. One commenter notes that attestation prompts are often buried behind opaque browser dialogs—and that major sites like Netflix and Twitter likely don’t enforce it today, but could tomorrow without user consent. Others highlight portability trade-offs: biometric entropy (e.g., a fingerprint) is inherently lower than a strong passphrase, and tying authentication to a single physical device contradicts long-standing security hygiene principles like redundancy and user-controlled recovery. The debate isn’t anti-innovation—it’s a call for transparency, optionality, and architectural humility.
