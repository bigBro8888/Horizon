Hackers triggered automatic defrost cycles in European supermarket fridges

News time: 2026-10-13T07:40:21.012425+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a rare and alarming demonstration of cyber-physical harm, hackers remotely activated automatic defrost cycles across hundreds of supermarket refrigeration units in France and other parts of Europe—causing temperatures inside freezers to spike as high as 50°C, spoiling perishable food and disrupting store operations. The attack targeted legacy Danfoss refrigeration controllers, many running outdated firmware versions like EOS 2016 and EOS 2021, which were exposed to the internet without adequate security controls.

Unlike typical data-theft breaches, this incident had immediate physical consequences: frost buildup was deliberately melted during peak business hours—not overnight, as intended—overloading compressors, disabling cooling, and turning freezers into warm storage cabinets. Reports confirm nearly 400 French supermarkets were affected, with InterMarché among the hardest hit. Store staff reported alarms blaring, temperature displays flashing red, and shelves rapidly filling with thawing meat, dairy, and frozen meals.

The vulnerability exploited is well documented. Claroty’s Team82 researchers disclosed earlier in 2026 that certain Danfoss SM800 and SC355 controllers contain unauthenticated remote command interfaces—allowing attackers to trigger defrost, override setpoints, or disable alarms without credentials. These devices were never designed for internet exposure; yet many were connected remotely to enable ‘efficiency gains’ like remote diagnostics and centralized fleet management—a feature Danfoss itself promotes for retail chains.

What makes this attack especially sobering is its systemic root cause: not a zero-day exploit, but decades-old industrial equipment operating in insecure network environments, with no budget or operational window for updates. As one Hacker News commenter observed, ‘The laptop class doesn’t grok that the shift-work labor class has nothing in terms of luxuries like security reviews.’ This isn’t just about fridges—it’s about how critical infrastructure quietly depends on obsolete, internet-facing hardware—and who bears the cost when it fails.
