Developer turns WiFi 7 router into 320 Msps software-defined radio

News time: 2026-10-13T07:40:21.012544+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A hardware hacker known as h0m3us3r has unlocked an unexpected capability in off-the-shelf WiFi 7 routers: using them as high-speed software-defined radio (SDR) receivers. By modifying open-source firmware and leveraging the raw RF sampling paths inside MediaTek’s MT7996 chipset — found in the Gemtek W1700K router — the project achieves a real-time sampling rate of 320 million samples per second (Msps). That’s four times faster than the ESP32-based SDR the same developer previously built, and far beyond what most budget SDR dongles offer.

This isn’t just a proof-of-concept tweak. The technique bypasses the WiFi protocol stack entirely, tapping directly into the chip’s analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) and baseband processing units before digital signal conditioning for WiFi. It effectively repurposes the router’s existing RF front-end — including its 5 GHz transceiver chain — as a wideband receiver capable of capturing signals across tens or even hundreds of megahertz of instantaneous bandwidth. While not DC-coupled or fully tunable across all spectrum bands, it operates natively in the 5 GHz ISM band with exceptional speed and low latency.

The implications extend well beyond hobbyist curiosity. At $100–$150, a WiFi 7 router is dramatically cheaper than commercial 300+ Msps SDRs, which often cost thousands of dollars and require specialized PCIe cards or bench-top instruments. This opens doors for students, researchers, and RF educators to experiment with wideband signal analysis, spectrum monitoring, and real-time demodulation — without needing lab-grade gear. It also highlights how modern WiFi chipsets, designed for ultra-wide 320 MHz channels and multi-link operation, contain untapped potential for general-purpose RF sensing.

Still, practical use requires careful hardware awareness. As community commenters note, the effective usable bandwidth is narrower than the raw sampling rate suggests — due to analog filtering, channelization, and lack of DC coupling. Some users suggest augmenting the setup with external attenuators, filters, or up/downconverters to broaden frequency coverage. But even in its current form, this hack demonstrates how deeply embedded, mass-produced silicon can be reclaimed for novel engineering purposes — turning consumer networking gear into a new kind of open radio platform.
