What mechanistic argument supports next-token prediction plus RL leading to AGI

News time: 2026-10-13T07:40:21.142480+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent high-scoring post on r/MachineLearning cuts through the hype surrounding AI progress and asks a foundational engineering question: What is the strongest mechanistic, first-principles argument that today’s dominant training paradigm—next-token prediction combined with reinforcement learning—can produce open-ended general intelligence? The author explicitly rejects speculative extrapolations, philosophical definitions of ‘intelligence,’ or appeals to future breakthroughs. Instead, they demand causal explanations grounded in computational learning theory, information theory, and the observable behavior of transformer architectures.

The post reflects growing scrutiny within the AI research community about whether scaling current methods is sufficient for AGI. While large language models excel at pattern recognition and statistical correlation, their core objective—predicting the next token—lacks explicit goals, memory persistence across episodes, or causal world modeling. Reinforcement learning adds goal-directed feedback, but most current RL fine-tuning (e.g., PPO on reward models) operates on static text trajectories, not real-world interaction loops. Recent benchmarks like ARC-AGI suggest RL-augmented models can improve systematic generalization—but only up to a point, and often without transparent internal mechanisms.

Mechanistically, self-attention enables transformers to implement dynamic token selection and implicit reasoning over context windows, as shown in analyses of attention-based priority filtering. However, this does not equate to building persistent internal models of agents, environments, or causality. RL integration remains shallow: rewards are typically derived from human preferences or synthetic signals, not autonomous environmental feedback. Crucially, no formal proof exists that next-token prediction—even with infinite data and compute—induces universal computation or recursive self-improvement, both considered prerequisites for open-ended intelligence.

This isn’t skepticism about progress—it’s a call for theoretical rigor. If AGI emerges from current paradigms, we need to understand how, not just that. Identifying the precise computational inductive biases, representational capacities, and learning dynamics that could bridge narrow prediction to general reasoning is essential for alignment, safety, and efficient R&D. Without such grounding, deployment decisions risk being guided by intuition rather than mechanism.
