Intel documented vfmaddrnd231pd but no CPU ever executed it

News time: 2026-10-13T07:40:21.012448+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Deep in the latest editions of Intel’s Software Development Manual — specifically the 2024 revision — lurks an instruction that looks perfectly plausible on paper: vfmaddrnd231pd. It stands for "Vector Fused Multiply-Add with Round-to-Nearest-Even and 231pd encoding", targeting packed double-precision floating-point values. At first glance, it fits neatly into AVX-512’s sprawling family of fused multiply-add (FMA) instructions, which combine multiplication and addition in a single, high-throughput, low-latency operation. But unlike its siblings — such as vfmadd231pd — this one has a critical distinction: it was never implemented in silicon.

The instruction appeared in Intel’s official documentation with full EVEX encoding details and rounding semantics, yet no assembler recognizes it, no disassembler emits it, and no x86-64 processor — past, present, or announced — supports it. As confirmed by Felix Cloutier’s authoritative x86 reference and corroborated by recent reporting, vfmaddrnd231pd was quietly removed from the 2026 edition of the manual without explanation. Its existence highlights a rare case where specification outpaced implementation — not due to ambition or forward planning, but because of a hardware-level flaw that rendered the instruction nonfunctional.

Why would Intel publish an instruction that doesn’t work? The answer lies in the messy reality of CPU design iteration. During AVX-512’s turbulent evolution — especially amid Intel’s strategic retreat from and later partial return to client-side AVX-512 — some opcodes were reserved, specified, and even documented before being abandoned mid-development. In this case, evidence suggests the instruction was dropped due to a hardware bug affecting rounding control logic in early AVX-512 execution units. Rather than revise the manual retroactively, Intel left it in place until the next major revision cycle.

Beyond technical curiosity, vfmaddrnd231pd serves as a quiet reminder of how abstraction layers can obscure foundational truths. As systems programmers increasingly rely on compilers and auto-vectorizers — and as AI-driven toolchains further distance developers from raw instruction semantics — understanding why an instruction fails to materialize becomes as important as knowing how it should behave. It’s a small artifact, but one that exposes the fragile, human, and occasionally broken pipeline between architecture specification and physical reality.
