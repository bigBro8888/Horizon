Agent37 launches $0.37/month AI agent sandboxes

News time: 2026-10-13T07:40:21.012591+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Agent37, a Y Combinator–backed startup (F26 batch), has launched a low-cost sandboxing platform designed specifically for running AI agents — including OpenClaw, Hermes, and Claude Code wrappers. Priced at just $0.37 per sandbox per month on average, it targets developers who need secure, isolated execution environments but can’t justify the cost of traditional cloud VMs or hyperscaler-based sandboxes.

The company’s cost advantage stems from deep architectural and procurement choices. Founder Vishnu — formerly a Senior Software Engineer at Amazon and Engineering Manager at Quantcast — built Amazon’s internal AI chatbot Diya and coding harness Ziya. Drawing on that infrastructure experience, Agent37 avoids hyperscalers like AWS entirely, opting instead for bare-metal providers such as Hetzner and OVH, where margins are far lower. They also prioritize RAM-dense server configurations, recognizing that AI agent workloads are memory-bound (e.g., browser automation, local tool use) rather than CPU-bound — a key insight most general-purpose cloud vendors overlook.

Technically, Agent37 uses gVisor — a lightweight, user-space kernel — to enable fast boot times and efficient memory sharing across sandboxes on the same host. This lets them pack many sandboxes onto one physical machine without reserving full VM memory per instance. However, this comes with tradeoffs: gVisor doesn’t support nested virtualization (Docker-in-Docker) or Android emulators. For those heavier workloads, Agent37 offers full VM sandboxes at a slightly higher price — still undercutting competitors thanks to their lean stack and hardware strategy.

Crucially, Agent37 now supports a free tier — something previously unaffordable when bootstrapped — with infrastructure costs averaging only $0.05–$0.10 per free user per month. Over 90% of new signups are on this free plan, hosting personal agents and experimental toolchains. The team invites developers — especially those building messaging or workflow agents — to stress-test the platform and explore its economic limits. As one commenter noted, this isn’t just cheaper infrastructure; it’s infrastructure designed for how agents actually behave.
