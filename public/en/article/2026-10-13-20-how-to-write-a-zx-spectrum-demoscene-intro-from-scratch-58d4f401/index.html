How to write a ZX Spectrum demoscene intro from scratch

News time: 2026-10-13T07:40:21.012654+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Mark Round’s 2026 blog post is not just another retrocomputing tutorial — it’s a living artifact of digital archaeology. The article walks readers step-by-step through writing a demoscene intro for the ZX Spectrum, a beloved 8-bit home computer released in 1982. Using Z80 assembly language, Round builds a compact, visually engaging demo that runs on real hardware or emulators — complete with raster effects, scrolling text, and synchronized music. Along the way, he documents the quirks of the Spectrum’s ULA chip, memory layout, and timing constraints that make precise control both essential and frustrating.

What makes this guide distinctive is its honesty about historical fragility. Round recounts how he had to recover a critical pre-compiled binary from the Internet Archive because the original source code — hosted on a long-defunct website — no longer assembled correctly. This isn’t just a coding exercise; it’s a case study in how easily foundational demoscene knowledge can vanish without active curation. He notes that even when sources do survive (like Sergey Bulba’s still-active archive at ay.strangled.net), they often require detective work: outdated toolchains, undocumented macros, and assumptions baked into 1980s development environments.

The piece also reflects on why the demoscene remains culturally vital. Unlike modern software development — where abstraction layers and bloated frameworks dominate — demos demand intimate hardware mastery. Every byte counts. A 4K or 64K intro isn’t arbitrary: it’s a constraint that forces elegance, creativity, and deep optimization. Readers comment that this ethos reshaped their understanding of programming forever — some calling it a ‘taint’ against laziness, others a nostalgic return to coding as craft.

Beyond nostalgia, the article quietly advocates for preservation-as-practice. It doesn’t just teach assembly; it models how to verify, annotate, and repackage legacy code so future developers can build upon it — not just admire it. As one commenter observes, ‘This stuff tends to rot if the community does not do the preservation work.’ In that sense, Round’s intro is less a demo than a time capsule — executable, educational, and urgently archival.
