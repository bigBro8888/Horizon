BS holders can land research engineer roles but face steeper odds

News time: 2026-10-13T07:40:21.142674+00:00 · Source: reddit

A practicing data scientist with a bachelor’s degree recently sparked a thoughtful discussion on Reddit about whether an MS is effectively a soft gatekeeper for research-adjacent engineering roles—particularly Research Engineer, Applied Scientist, and Post-Training positions. The poster, who has industry experience building ML models and prior academic research exposure in applied machine learning, shared real LinkedIn job-posting education breakdowns from companies like Cohere and Garner Health. For example, one Cohere Post-Training role listed PhDs at 22%, Master’s holders at 48%, and BS/other at 30%; another Research Engineer role showed 26% PhD, 52% MS, and 22% BS/other; while an Applied Scientist opening at Garner Health reported 56% MS and 44% BS/other. These figures suggest that while bachelor’s degrees are represented, they’re consistently in the minority—often below one-quarter of hires.

The core question isn’t just statistical: it’s about viability and strategy. The poster wants to know whether a BS alone can be competitive—or whether exceptional compensating signals (e.g., peer-reviewed publications, workshop papers, or high-impact open-source projects) can meaningfully offset the credential gap. This reflects a broader tension in ML hiring: as roles blur the line between software engineering and research, employers increasingly seek hybrid profiles—but their screening filters often still lean on formal credentials as proxies for depth, rigor, and domain fluency.

Industry respondents in the thread emphasized that while advanced degrees help signal readiness, they’re not insurmountable barriers. Several noted that hands-on contributions—like shipping production-grade post-training systems, co-authoring arXiv preprints with measurable impact, or maintaining widely adopted ML tooling—carry increasing weight, especially at startups and labs prioritizing output over pedigree. Still, candidates without graduate training may need to invest more deliberately in visible, verifiable evidence of research-level thinking and engineering discipline.

Ultimately, this conversation underscores a pragmatic truth: the path into research engineering is less about ticking a degree box and more about demonstrating a consistent pattern of solving hard, open-ended problems at the intersection of modeling, infrastructure, and evaluation. For BS-holders, that means treating every project—not just publications—as portfolio evidence, and framing work through the lens of research questions, reproducibility, and generalizable insights.
