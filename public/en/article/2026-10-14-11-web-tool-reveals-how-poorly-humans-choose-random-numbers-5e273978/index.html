Web tool reveals how poorly humans choose random numbers

News time: 2026-10-14T06:06:19.637855+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new interactive web demo called How random are you? invites users to type numbers between 0 and 9 as ‘randomly’ as possible—only to watch, in real time, how accurately an algorithm predicts each choice. Hosted at lab.specr.net/rigged-draw/, the tool records every input, builds a statistical model of your behavior on the fly, and displays its prediction before you press the next key. After just a few dozen entries, many users see prediction accuracy climb above 25%, far exceeding the 10% expected from true randomness—and some reach over 30%. The interface is minimal: a grid of numbered balls, subtle feedback animations, and a running tally labeled ‘The house called X of your Y scored numbers.’

This isn’t magic—it’s pattern exploitation. As one commenter notes, it echoes Scott Aaronson’s classroom experiment where students typed ‘f’ or ‘d’, and a simple predictor achieved ~70% accuracy by tracking alternation bias, repetition avoidance, and uneven digit distribution. Humans instinctively avoid repeating numbers, overuse ‘middle’ digits like 5 or 7, and underuse 0 and 9—even when explicitly trying to be random. The demo leverages these well-documented cognitive tendencies using lightweight heuristics, not deep learning: short-term frequency counts, recent n-gram patterns, and positional biases.

What makes the project especially effective is its immediacy and transparency. Unlike black-box AI demos, users can observe exactly how their choices betray structure—e.g., typing ‘3–7–3’ repeatedly triggers higher confidence in predicting ‘7’ again. Several commenters tested edge cases: one fed JavaScript’s Math.random() output into the interface and saw prediction rates soar to 18 out of 190 (≈9.5%), confirming that even algorithmic pseudo-randomness leaves exploitable traces in this context. Another user tried speaking numbers aloud versus typing, noting lower predictability—hinting at motor vs. cognitive bottlenecks in human randomness generation.

Beyond entertainment, the demo quietly underscores foundational ideas in computer science and psychology: true randomness is computationally irreducible, yet human ‘randomness’ is highly compressible. It also bridges theory and intuition—making abstract concepts like Kolmogorov complexity, entropy estimation, and behavioral game theory tangible through direct interaction. For educators, developers, or anyone designing systems that rely on user unpredictability (e.g., CAPTCHAs, password prompts, or cryptographic seeding), the lesson is clear: never assume people will behave like fair dice.
