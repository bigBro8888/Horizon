Apple launches 'Switch to Safari' campaign urging Chrome users to change

News time: 2026-10-14T06:06:19.637890+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Apple has quietly launched a new marketing initiative called 'Switch to Safari', hosted at safari.apple.com/switch, that directly targets Chrome users with a series of pointed comparisons. Rather than framing the move as a simple preference, Apple positions Safari as faster, more private, and more energy-efficient — especially on Macs and iPhones. The site highlights real-world metrics: up to 50% longer battery life during video playback, faster page loads on common sites like YouTube and Reddit, and built-in privacy protections like Intelligent Tracking Prevention that block cross-site trackers by default.

The timing is no accident. This campaign arrives amid growing regulatory pressure on both Apple and Google over browser engine monopolies. In the EU, Apple was forced under the Digital Markets Act (DMA) to allow third-party browser engines on iOS — but it implemented this in a way that still restricts non-WebKit browsers from using system-level optimizations or full hardware acceleration. Meanwhile, Google’s Chromium engine powers over 80% of the global browser market, including Edge, Opera, Brave, and Samsung Internet — meaning most 'alternative' browsers aren’t truly independent.

Developers have long voiced frustration about this duopoly. As one commenter notes, many now effectively develop for Chrome, not the open web — leading to subtle bugs and missing features in Safari or Firefox. Others point out the irony: critics of Apple’s WebKit-only policy on iOS often rely entirely on Chrome, despite its own dominance and Google’s control over Chromium’s roadmap. The debate isn’t just about speed or convenience — it’s about who gets to define web standards, how much interoperability developers can expect, and whether the open web remains pluralistic or collapses into two vendor-controlled lanes.

What makes this campaign notable isn’t its technical depth — it’s promotional, not analytical — but its role in surfacing long-simmering tensions. From translation quirks (Safari reloads POST-heavy pages when switching languages, breaking forms) to engine-level limitations (missing APIs, delayed adoption of new CSS or JS features), the friction points are real. And while Apple touts privacy and performance, the underlying question lingers: Can healthy competition exist when one company controls the only allowed engine on a billion devices, and another controls the source code for most of the rest?
