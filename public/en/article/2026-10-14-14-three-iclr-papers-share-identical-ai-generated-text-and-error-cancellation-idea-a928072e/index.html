Three ICLR papers share identical AI-generated text and error-cancellation idea

News time: 2026-10-14T06:06:19.823153+00:00 · Source: reddit

A reviewer assigned to evaluate three papers for the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) posted a striking observation on Reddit: all three submissions exhibited nearly identical structural flaws — not just in content, but in language, framing, and theoretical scaffolding. The writing was unusually stilted and repetitive, bearing hallmarks of unedited large language model output — specifically reminiscent of Claude — making technical comprehension difficult. More alarmingly, each paper built its entire theoretical contribution around the same narrow insight: comparing two predictors by analyzing the difference between their errors, relying on cancellation of a shared nuisance term to derive tighter bounds.

This technique — using pairwise error differences to eliminate confounding variables — is known in statistical learning theory, especially in contexts like model selection or ranking. But it is neither novel nor obscure enough to independently appear in three separate, concurrent ICLR submissions — particularly when accompanied by identical rhetorical phrasing, parallel proof structures, and similarly superficial experimental validation. The reviewer noted that the papers didn’t just share an idea; they shared how they introduced it, how they extended it, and even how they failed to meaningfully engage with prior work on paired comparisons or bias cancellation.

The post quickly drew attention from researchers familiar with both ICLR’s review process and the growing influence of LLMs in academic writing. Several commenters confirmed seeing similar patterns — not only in ICLR but also in NeurIPS and ICML submissions — where theoretical claims are inflated around trivial algebraic observations, wrapped in verbose, generic prose. Some speculated about automated 'paper mills' or shared templates circulating among students or labs under publication pressure. Others cautioned against over-attributing to AI, noting that shallow theoretical framing has long existed, but LLMs may now be amplifying its frequency and uniformity.

What makes this case notable isn’t just repetition — it’s the convergence of low linguistic quality, conceptual shallowness, and structural homogeneity across independent submissions. It raises urgent questions about how peer review adapts to detect not just plagiarism or fraud, but synthetic conformity: the mass production of technically plausible yet intellectually hollow work. As conferences grapple with soaring submission volumes and increasing reliance on AI tools, this incident serves as a concrete signal that current review criteria may not adequately flag systemic weaknesses masked by surface-level coherence.
