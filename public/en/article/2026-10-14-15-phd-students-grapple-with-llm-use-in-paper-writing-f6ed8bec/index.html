PhD students grapple with LLM use in paper writing

News time: 2026-10-14T06:06:19.823205+00:00 · Source: reddit

A candid Reddit post from a machine learning PhD student has struck a nerve across academic AI communities. The author describes feeling existentially drained—not by the technical difficulty of research, but by the accelerating pressure to publish quickly in a field where new papers appear daily. They’ve already written two papers without heavy reliance on large language models (LLMs), yet they’re now tempted to ‘vibe’ their third paper entirely with Claude, Anthropic’s AI assistant. That tension—between intellectual growth and publication velocity—isn’t just personal; it reflects a broader cultural pivot in how early-career researchers navigate scholarly work.

This isn’t about laziness or cheating. It’s about trade-offs: using LLMs can dramatically speed up drafting, editing, and even idea generation—but at what cost to deep thinking, methodological rigor, or the slow, reflective learning that traditionally defines doctoral training? The student doesn’t reject LLMs outright; they question how much to lean on them when the stakes include not only career advancement but also self-development and scholarly identity.

Recent studies confirm this isn’t an isolated dilemma. Research published on alphaXiv and SSRN shows LLM adoption in academic writing surged in 2023—especially in computer science—and varies widely by discipline, career stage, and background. Early-career researchers like PhD students are among the most active adopters, often without clear institutional guidance or mentorship on ethical boundaries. Meanwhile, tools like Claude—released in March 2023 and integrated into platforms like Notion and Quora—offer increasingly polished, accessible assistance for writing and coding.

What makes this moment especially fraught is the lack of consensus. Journals haven’t standardized LLM disclosure policies; advisors rarely receive training on supervising AI-assisted work; and university ethics boards are still catching up. So students are left to make high-stakes decisions alone: Should a draft co-written with Claude count as ‘their’ work? Does faster output mean shallower insight? And if everyone else is using LLMs, does opting out risk professional obsolescence? There are no easy answers—only urgent questions about what kind of scholars we want to become, and what kind of science we want to produce.
