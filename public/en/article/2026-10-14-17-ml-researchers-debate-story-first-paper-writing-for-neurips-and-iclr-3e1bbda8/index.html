ML researchers debate story-first paper writing for NeurIPS and ICLR

News time: 2026-10-14T06:06:19.823321+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent post on r/MachineLearning has reignited a quiet but persistent conversation in the AI research community: how should machine learning papers be written to maximize clarity, impact, and acceptance at top-tier venues like NeurIPS, ICLR, and ICML? The anonymous poster—identified only as /u/ComprehensiveTop3297—expresses genuine uncertainty amid the diversity of writing styles they’ve encountered, from dense technical reports to highly narrative-driven manuscripts. They’re not asking for formatting tips or LaTeX tricks; instead, they’re seeking philosophical and practical guidance on how to structure scientific reasoning so it resonates with reviewers and readers alike.

The question gains weight because it references a widely circulated 2022 blog post by Michael J. Black, director at the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems. In it, Black argues that too many scientific papers—including in ML—read like lab notebooks: results first, motivation buried, narrative absent. He advocates for a ‘story-oriented’ approach: begin with a clear problem grounded in human intuition or real-world need, then walk the reader through why existing methods fall short, how your idea bridges the gap, and what evidence supports it—all before diving into equations or ablation studies.

This isn’t just stylistic preference. At conferences where thousands of submissions compete for ~20% acceptance rates, readability directly affects review outcomes. A well-structured story helps reviewers quickly grasp contribution, novelty, and limitations—even when skimming. Several commenters noted that NeurIPS reviewers often prioritize conceptual clarity over implementation minutiae, especially in theory- or systems-adjacent tracks. Others cautioned that ‘story’ shouldn’t mean oversimplification: rigor, reproducibility, and precise claims remain non-negotiable.

What makes this discussion timely is its alignment with broader shifts in ML culture—from hyper-optimization of benchmarks toward greater emphasis on understanding, communication, and responsible deployment. Writing well isn’t ancillary to doing good science; it’s part of the scientific method itself. As one commenter put it: ‘If you can’t explain your idea to a smart grad student in three minutes, you probably haven’t fully understood it yet.’ That mindset, more than any template or checklist, may be the most valuable tool for emerging researchers.
