Physics-trained researcher questions when local neural learning beats backpropagation

News time: 2026-10-14T06:06:19.823584+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher with training in physics and mathematics has sparked thoughtful discussion by asking a deceptively simple question: when—if ever—do biologically inspired, localized learning rules outperform backpropagation in practice? In a detailed Reddit post on r/MachineLearning, they describe building neural networks from scratch and growing skeptical of backpropagation’s global, loss-dependent weight updates. Instead, they’re drawn to alternatives like Hebbian learning, Oja’s rule, and predictive coding—methods where each synapse adjusts based only on nearby activity (e.g., pre- and post-synaptic firing or local prediction errors), without needing error signals routed backward through the entire network.

Their inquiry is grounded in concrete use cases—not philosophical preference. They ask whether locality confers real advantages in unsupervised representation learning (e.g., uncovering collective variables in molecular dynamics), continual adaptation to shifting data streams, or operation under strict hardware constraints like neuromorphic chips. Crucially, they don’t assume biological plausibility implies superiority; rather, they seek empirical evidence: Are there tasks where local rules converge faster, generalize better, or preserve prior knowledge more robustly than online backprop?

Recent work supports taking the question seriously. Predictive coding networks (PCNs), for instance, have been shown to approximate backpropagation under certain conditions—but also to produce brain-like neural dynamics and support energy-efficient spiking implementations. A 2025 Nature Communications paper introduced "Predictive Coding Light" specifically for spiking neural networks, highlighting efficiency gains in low-power settings. Meanwhile, Oja’s rule—a stabilized variant of Hebbian learning—has demonstrated improved short-term memory performance in recurrent networks without requiring precise weight alignment, a known pain point in local learning.

Still, trade-offs remain stark. Backpropagation dominates because it scales reliably across deep, heterogeneous architectures and large labeled datasets. Local rules often struggle with credit assignment beyond immediate neighbors, limiting depth and expressivity unless carefully augmented (e.g., via equilibrium propagation or dendritic prediction errors). The poster acknowledges this—and explicitly invites practitioners to share empirical comparisons, overlooked limitations, and promising research directions. Their goal isn’t to replace backprop, but to map where alternatives earn their keep: in unsupervised discovery, adaptive edge computing, or modeling biological learning itself.
