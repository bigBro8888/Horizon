Linear recurrence matches Transformer accuracy in 15M decision models but cuts latency and training time

News time: 2026-10-15T05:52:02.419482+00:00 · Source: reddit

A researcher directly compared two 15-million-parameter decision-only models — one built on linear recurrence (a custom Gated Segmented State Space architecture), the other on standard Transformer self-attention — trained end-to-end on the same hardware, budget, and task. The goal wasn’t to chase state-of-the-art accuracy, but to ask: for bounded-output, non-generative decisions — like choosing among four candidate sentences present in a long text — which architecture delivers better efficiency without sacrificing performance?

The task was deliberately challenging: retrieval-decision over passages of 2,000–8,000 characters, requiring models to identify which of four sentences appears verbatim in the passage. With only 2,000 training examples and a tiny model size, neither architecture learned much beyond chance (25%); test accuracies were nearly identical — 26.8% for the linear model (jev-v8a) and 28.2% for the Transformer (jev-transformer). That near-tie confirmed the core hypothesis: at this scale, accuracy is bottlenecked by capacity and data, not attention mechanism.

Where the difference became stark was in cost. At maximum context length (8,192 tokens), the linear model ran 2.37× faster per inference (108.6 ms vs. 257.7 ms), reflecting its theoretical O(T) complexity versus the Transformer’s O(T²). Training was even more lopsided: six fine-tuning epochs took ~30 minutes for the linear model, but 40–45 minutes per epoch for the Transformer — a 6–7× slowdown that worsens with longer contexts. Earlier experiments on shorter tasks showed the linear model pulling ahead in accuracy too (e.g., 50.4% vs. 46.2%), suggesting its advantage grows when compute constraints tighten.

The takeaway isn’t that linear models ‘win’ — it’s that they offer a viable, efficient substrate for decision-focused AI where generative output, massive pretraining, or high accuracy aren’t required. As the author notes, the negative result is the point: small decision models are fundamentally accuracy-starved today, regardless of architecture. What can be improved — and what matters most for real-world deployment — is latency, memory footprint, and training cost. The next experiment (V5) explores bidirectional variants of both, keeping the same 15M budget, to see whether directionality reshapes the tradeoff.
