Rationalist blogs are too long and obscure, argues new critique

News time: 2026-10-15T05:52:01.997673+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A widely discussed blog post by Alex Wennerberg asks a blunt but resonant question: Why do so many rationalist blog posts run thousands of words—often dense, meandering, and stylistically opaque—without delivering sharper insight? Wennerberg doesn’t dismiss the community’s intellectual ambitions. Instead, he argues that excessive length frequently serves as camouflage: it masks weak foundational assumptions, avoids confronting gaps in reasoning, and substitutes rhetorical elaboration for conceptual precision.

The critique lands in a rich context. The rationalist blogosphere—centered historically on LessWrong and later Slate Star Codex—emerged from early-2000s internet culture as a space for applying Bayesian reasoning, cognitive science, and formal epistemology to everyday thinking. Its ethos prizes rigor, self-correction, and intellectual honesty. Yet Wennerberg observes a growing disconnect: many posts prioritize exhaustive qualification, nested caveats, and intertextual references over clarity and argumentative economy. He suggests this isn’t just a stylistic quirk—it reflects deeper epistemic habits, like deferring scrutiny of core premises while adding layer upon layer of nuance downstream.

The response was immediate and nuanced. Commenters didn’t fall into simple agreement or dismissal. Habryka pointed out a factual correction: Eliezer Yudkowsky’s influential Sequences, often cited as emblematic of rationalist verbosity, are in fact composed mostly of short posts—under 1,000 words each—with the million-word total arising from sheer volume, not individual bloat. Bawolff countered that nuance itself is valuable and shouldn’t be sacrificed for brevity, while others—like Sien—praised concise, audience-respecting writing (e.g., The Economist) as a model rationalists should emulate. Still others, like nfw2, pushed back on the piece’s implied skepticism toward tradition and common sense, suggesting those sources encode real, if tacit, epistemic value.

What makes this more than a style complaint is its grounding in epistemology—the study of how we know what we know. Research shows writing functions not just as communication but as an epistemic tool: the act of writing reshapes memory, exposes hidden assumptions, and forces coherence. When writing becomes unmoored from that function—when it prioritizes display over discovery—it risks undermining the very ideals it claims to serve. Wennerberg’s essay, then, is less about word count and more about intellectual hygiene: whether rationalist writing still serves knowledge-building, or has drifted into ritualized performance.
