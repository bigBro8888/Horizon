BIPM to permanently end leap seconds in UTC starting May 2027

News time: 2026-10-16T05:48:26.194802+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a historic resolution adopted at the 27th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) has formally decided to discontinue the insertion of leap seconds into Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) — effective 20 May 2027. This ends a practice that began in 1972, when the first leap second was added to reconcile atomic time with Earth’s gradually slowing and irregular rotation. From now on, UTC will run continuously, aligned solely with International Atomic Time (TAI), while astronomical time (UT1), based on Earth’s actual orientation, will drift slowly but predictably away.

The decision reflects decades of growing technical friction. Leap seconds — though seemingly trivial — have repeatedly caused outages in global infrastructure: financial trading systems froze, cloud platforms crashed, and navigation services hiccuped during past insertions. Unlike leap days, which are predictable and handled gracefully by software, leap seconds introduce ambiguous timestamps (e.g., 23:59:60) that many operating systems, databases, and distributed systems were never designed to process. As computing systems grew more tightly synchronized and latency-sensitive, the risk-to-benefit ratio tipped decisively.

Crucially, the BIPM did not abandon the link between civil time and solar time altogether. Instead, it deferred responsibility for long-term alignment to the International Astronomical Union (IAU) and other geophysical bodies. The resolution anticipates that a ‘leap hour’ — or some other coarse correction — may eventually be needed, perhaps around 2267, when the accumulated offset between UTC and UT1 reaches one hour. But even then, no mechanism is prescribed; the decision leaves future action deliberately open-ended.

This isn’t just a metrology tweak — it’s a quiet pivot in how humanity defines ‘now’. For centuries, timekeeping served astronomy and daily life; today, it serves algorithms, satellites, and real-time networks. By choosing atomic regularity over celestial fidelity, the world’s timekeepers have acknowledged that our technological present no longer depends on the Sun’s apparent motion — but rather on the unwavering pulse of cesium atoms.
