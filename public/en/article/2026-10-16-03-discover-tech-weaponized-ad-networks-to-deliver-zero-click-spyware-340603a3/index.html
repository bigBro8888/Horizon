Discover Tech weaponized ad networks to deliver zero-click spyware

News time: 2026-10-16T05:48:26.194836+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Citizen Lab has uncovered a disturbing new cyber-attack vector: targeted digital advertisements used not to sell products, but to silently install mercenary spyware—without any user interaction. Dubbed 'AdFection', this technique marks the first publicly confirmed case of zero-click spyware delivered via standard online ads. Researchers traced the infrastructure to Discover Tech, an Israeli ad-tech firm whose Controlium platform enables deep user profiling and covert device exploitation. The campaign specifically targeted journalists at Al Jazeera, deploying Candiru’s sophisticated spyware through seemingly benign banner ads served across thousands of websites and apps.

Unlike traditional malware that relies on phishing clicks or malicious downloads, AdFection exploits vulnerabilities in ad-rendering components—such as web browsers or mobile ad SDKs—to execute code the moment an ad is loaded and displayed. No click, no permission prompt, no visible sign of compromise. Citizen Lab verified infections on Android and iOS devices, confirming that merely viewing an ad was sufficient for full device compromise. The scale is staggering: Discover Tech’s ad network reached over 120,000 websites and 70,000 mobile apps, exposing millions of users to potential surveillance.

What makes AdFection especially alarming is its integration into the legitimate ad supply chain. Discover Tech positioned itself as a performance analytics vendor, offering real-time audience segmentation and behavioral tracking—but behind the scenes, it built infrastructure capable of injecting exploit payloads into ad creatives. Its clients included mercenary surveillance vendors, who leveraged Controlium’s targeting capabilities to identify and infect high-value individuals. This blurs the line between advertising technology and offensive cyber operations—and raises urgent questions about accountability across the fragmented AdTech ecosystem.

The implications extend far beyond journalism. Any app or website using Discover Tech’s SDK—or partnering with publishers in its network—may have unknowingly served malicious ads. While Google, Apple, and major ad exchanges have since begun blocking Discover Tech domains and signatures, the incident reveals a systemic weakness: the lack of security vetting for third-party ad code. As one AdTech engineer noted in Hacker News comments, the industry’s complexity makes end-to-end validation nearly impossible—yet the consequences of failure are now catastrophic. For users, the takeaway is stark: ad blockers are no longer just for privacy—they’re a basic security necessity.
