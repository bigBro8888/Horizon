Random neural circuits self-assemble to match backpropagation accuracy

News time: 2026-10-16T05:48:26.518314+00:00 · Source: reddit

A groundbreaking study published in PNAS and highlighted by MIT reveals that artificial neural networks don’t need hand-crafted architectures or global error signals to learn effectively. Instead, when composed of simple, neuron-like units connected at random—and governed only by shared, local synaptic plasticity rules—these systems spontaneously form functional learning motifs. These motifs are small, recurring circuit patterns that emerge organically during training, not by design. Remarkably, networks built this way achieved image classification accuracy on par with standard backpropagation-trained models of comparable scale, despite never computing gradients across layers.

The work bridges a long-standing gap between neuroscience and machine learning. Biological neurons lack access to global error derivatives; instead, they rely on local biochemical signals—like calcium influx or neurotransmitter timing—to adjust synaptic strength. The new model mirrors this constraint: every synapse updates based solely on activity in its immediate pre- and postsynaptic neurons, plus a shared neuromodulatory signal (e.g., dopamine-like feedback). No weight transport, no chain rule, no central coordinator—just decentralized, rule-based adaptation.

Crucially, the authors stress this is not a proposal to replace backpropagation in large AI systems. Rather, it’s a proof-of-concept for how learning machinery could arise from minimal developmental principles—akin to how neural circuits wire themselves in early brain development. The motifs proved robust across different initial random connectivities, suggesting structural stability. Yet the team cautions that scaling to complex vision tasks (e.g., ImageNet) or maintaining long-term stability remains untested.

This research reframes a core assumption in deep learning: that high performance requires either carefully engineered architectures or precise gradient computation. Here, function emerges from collective dynamics—not top-down design. As one MIT commentator noted, it shifts the question from ‘How do we build better learners?’ to ‘What minimal rules let learning build itself?’ That conceptual pivot may guide future neuromorphic hardware, adaptive edge AI, and theories of cortical development.
