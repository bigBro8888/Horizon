Meta’s new terms auto-grant website scraping rights to business accounts starting October 30

News time: 2026-10-16T05:48:26.194737+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Starting October 30, 2026, Meta’s updated Commercial Terms will automatically grant the company permission to scrape websites owned or operated by businesses that use its advertising or business tools — without requiring separate, affirmative consent. This change does not apply to individual users or personal accounts, but targets publishers, news outlets, e-commerce sites, and other commercial entities with Meta Business Suite, Ads Manager, or similar accounts. Under the revised language, simply maintaining an active business relationship with Meta is treated as implied consent to data collection from the business’s public-facing websites.

The move has drawn sharp scrutiny from legal scholars and digital publishers. Experts point out that the terms purport to license not just publicly accessible HTML, but also copyrighted content — articles, images, videos, and layouts — even when the business account holder may lack full licensing authority over that material. As one commenter noted, many news organizations license third-party content under restrictive agreements that explicitly forbid sublicensing or AI training uses. If Meta relies on a publisher’s contractual grant to train models or repurpose content, it risks infringing underlying copyrights held by wire services, photo agencies, or freelance contributors.

Technically, Meta Bot — already the most aggressive AI crawler on many publisher sites, according to Mojodojo’s AI Crawler Index — appears poised to scale up its activity under this new framework. While Meta has long scraped web content for integrity monitoring and ad targeting, this formalization marks a strategic shift: from opportunistic, legally ambiguous collection to a contract-based, opt-out-by-default regime. Critics argue this flips traditional consent models on their head — especially under GDPR-aligned frameworks where explicit, informed, and revocable consent is the norm for processing personal or copyrighted material.

The broader implications extend beyond copyright law. This policy reinforces how platform terms of service increasingly function as de facto regulatory instruments — shaping data flows, AI training ecosystems, and publisher leverage in ways that bypass legislative or judicial oversight. For smaller publishers lacking legal teams, the practical effect may be coerced compliance: either accept the terms or lose access to essential advertising infrastructure. As one observer dryly observed, it’s ‘next-level legal judo’ — not innovation in product, but in contractual architecture.
