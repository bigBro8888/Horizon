Fortran turns 70 and remains vital for scientific computing

News time: 2026-10-16T05:48:26.194888+00:00 · Source: hackernews

This September marks a quiet but profound milestone: Fortran, the world’s first high-level programming language, celebrates its 70th birthday. Introduced by IBM in 1957 under John Backus’s leadership, Fortran was designed to let scientists and engineers write programs using mathematical notation instead of machine code—revolutionary at a time when programming meant toggling switches or punching cards. The anniversary post on fortran-lang.org doesn’t just look backward; it underscores how Fortran continues to power critical infrastructure—from climate models and nuclear simulations to aerospace engineering—where performance, precision, and decades-long code longevity are non-negotiable.

Unlike many languages that fade into obscurity after their heyday, Fortran has evolved steadily. Modern Fortran (since Fortran 90) introduced modules, dynamic memory allocation, array operations, and object-oriented features—yet retained full backward compatibility. That continuity is why legacy systems remain viable: one developer recalls maintaining a 700,000-line Fortran codebase originally written in the 1950s, still actively used today. Removing obsolete Hollerith constants and untangling early pointer-like workarounds (like ‘Dynosaur’) was no small feat—but the code’s reliability justified the effort.

The community’s reflections reveal Fortran’s dual identity: both a historical artifact and a living tool. A developer who wrote Fortran in 2018 for numerical simulation solvers praises modern Fortran’s speed and expressiveness for linear algebra—arguing it outperforms C in clarity and safety for math-heavy tasks. Others recall formative experiences: learning Fortran 77 on CDC Cyber mainframes in college, or stumbling through outdated TRS-80 manuals as a teen. These stories aren’t nostalgia—they’re evidence of Fortran’s unusual pedagogical and practical persistence.

What makes Fortran’s endurance remarkable is not just longevity, but relevance. In an era of rapid language churn, Fortran thrives where correctness trumps novelty: in mission-critical scientific software where rewriting risks introducing errors, and where decades of peer-reviewed, validated algorithms reside in well-tested Fortran libraries like LAPACK and BLAS. Its quiet resilience reminds us that progress in computing isn’t always about the newest syntax—it’s often about stewardship, stability, and trust.
