A developer shares their minimalist self-hosted stack

News time: 2026-10-16T05:48:26.194790+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a refreshingly grounded blog post titled What I self-host, a developer lays out their carefully curated, low-overhead self-hosting setup — not as a flashy home lab showcase, but as a pragmatic, privacy-conscious infrastructure built for longevity and efficiency. The list includes lightweight alternatives to common tools: a single-file HTML video player instead of Jellyfin for basic media access; PicoShare for no-frills file sharing; and a preference for native Go binaries over Docker-heavy stacks. Hardware choices reflect the same ethos: an 8th-gen Intel NUC with sub-5W idle power draw, capable of handling transcoding via QuickSync and AI inference for Frigate — all for under €140 after RAM upgrades.

The post quickly ignited one of the most technically substantive discussions on self-hosting in recent memory. Commenters didn’t just share opinions — they benchmarked real hardware (e.g., PassMark scores, RAM usage under load), dissected resource footprints (Sonarr idling at 157MB, Jellyfin at 330MB), and debated architectural trade-offs: containerized complexity versus native simplicity, dependency sprawl versus maintainability. One user even plugged their own dependency-free web video player — a single HTML file that delivers thumbnails, search, and subtitle support across laptops, phones, and smart TVs.

What stands out is how the conversation rejects dogma. There’s no ‘one true way’ — just shared experience. Some love the *arr stack for its automation; others avoid it entirely due to perceived fragility. Some swear by Proxmox for efficient VM/container consolidation; others run everything bare-metal for predictability. The thread becomes a living reference: less about what’s ‘cool’, more about what’s measurable, reliable, and sustainable on modest hardware.

At its core, this isn’t just a list — it’s a quiet manifesto for intentional infrastructure. In an era of ever-bigger Docker Compose files and SaaS-like self-hosted apps, the post and its discussion reaffirm that self-hosting can be lean, legible, and deeply personal. It reminds readers that control doesn’t require complexity — sometimes, it starts with a single binary, a used NUC, and a clear idea of what you actually need.
