Jev and 13 AI models tested on trolley problem variants with resampling

News time: 2026-10-16T05:48:26.194824+00:00 · Source: hackernews

What happens when you ask a non-generative, decision-focused AI model like Jev to weigh human lives? In a methodologically careful study, researcher Zach Landes put Jev—and 13 other AI models—through dozens of carefully varied versions of the classic Trolley Problem. Rather than relying on a single prompt or lucky phrasing, Landes used robust prompting: systematically reordering options, paraphrasing scenarios, and rotating framing (e.g., ‘pull the lever’ vs. ‘divert the train’). He also applied resampling—re-running each prompt multiple times—to assess consistency and reduce noise from stochastic outputs.

Jev stands apart from most models tested: it’s not a language model at all. Developed by TypeSafe AI and released in September 2026, Jev is a discriminative, zero-shot “System One” model designed for fast, typed decisions—not text generation. Its outputs are structured values (e.g., ‘yes/no’, ‘choice A’, confidence scores), meant to plug directly into software logic. That makes its ethical reasoning especially consequential: if banks or defense systems embed Jev to make real-time policy or targeting decisions, consistency and interpretability matter more than eloquence.

Landes found that responses varied widely—not just across models, but within the same model under different phrasings. Some models flipped answers depending on whether harm was described as ‘killing’ or ‘letting die’; others showed order effects, preferring whichever option appeared first. Jev, while more stable than many LLMs due to its deterministic architecture, still exhibited sensitivity to subtle wording shifts—especially when moral categories were underspecified (e.g., ‘a person’ vs. ‘a child’). The study doesn’t claim to measure ‘true morality’, but rather probes how reliably these models map ambiguous human norms onto discrete decisions.

This work sits at a critical intersection: AI alignment research rarely tests discriminative models, which increasingly power high-stakes automation—from fraud detection to autonomous weapons interfaces. As one Hacker News commenter warned, the danger isn’t always Skynet-style rebellion—it’s Kafkaesque bureaucracy scaled by AI: systems that follow rules perfectly while denying redress, empathy, or appeal. Landes’ approach—grounded in survey methodology, psychometrics, and software engineering—offers a replicable template for evaluating not just what AI says, but how stably and transparently it decides.
