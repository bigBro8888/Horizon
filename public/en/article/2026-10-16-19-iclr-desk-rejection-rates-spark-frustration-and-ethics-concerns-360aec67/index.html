ICLR desk rejection rates spark frustration and ethics concerns

News time: 2026-10-16T05:48:26.518175+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent Reddit post on r/MachineLearning has drawn attention to mounting frustration among ML researchers over high desk rejection rates at the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR). The author — a reviewer who spent four hours carefully evaluating a paper while unwell — was surprised to learn the paper received a desk rejection the next morning, despite earning a solid 3/4 review score. They note that many peers report similar experiences: papers with substantive content and positive initial reviews vanishing from consideration before peer review even begins.

This isn’t just anecdotal venting. ICLR enforces strict submission policies, including a hard cap of 20 submissions per author for ICLR 2026 and 2027. As stated in the official blog, the limit aims to curb low-effort ‘slop papers’ and prevent large-scale circumvention of submission rules via coordinated co-authorship. Yet rumors persist — echoed in the Reddit thread — that some authors create multiple accounts to bypass the cap, submitting dozens of papers under different identities. When such submissions are detected, all associated papers may be desk rejected as a policy enforcement measure.

The issue cuts deeper than fairness: it challenges trust in the review process. Desk rejections at ICLR are typically issued for administrative or procedural reasons — e.g., missing ethics statements (though not required for all submissions), unregistered reviewers among authors, or profile inaccuracies — not technical flaws. A recent arXiv paper (arXiv:2506.20141) formalizes desk rejection as an optimization problem and proposes an algorithm to reduce up to 19% of unnecessary desk rejections by improving policy implementation — suggesting the current system may discard valid work due to rigid automation or miscommunication.

What’s especially troubling is the asymmetry: reviewers invest significant time, only to see their efforts nullified by a non-technical decision. Meanwhile, the ICLR Code of Ethics explicitly applies to all contributors — including authors — and prohibits deceptive practices like identity manipulation. Yet enforcement remains opaque, and appeals mechanisms are rarely publicized. As one commenter observed, the real cost isn’t just rejected papers — it’s eroded morale, wasted labor, and disincentives for early-career researchers to engage with top-tier venues.
