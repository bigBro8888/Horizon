90% of US work hours fall outside AI benchmark coverage

News time: 2026-10-17T05:33:27.819531+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new study by Mercor reveals a stark mismatch between how AI models are evaluated and what people actually do at work. By mapping over 25,000 occupational tasks from the U.S. Department of Labor’s O*NET database against more than 1,400 prompts used in leading AI benchmarks—including MMLU, GSM8K, and HumanEval—the researchers found that only about 10% of total U.S. working hours correspond to tasks meaningfully represented in those benchmarks. The rest—nearly nine out of ten hours—involve activities like empathetic client counseling, real-time coordination across teams, nuanced judgment under incomplete information, or context-sensitive physical interaction: tasks rarely captured by static, multiple-choice, or code-generation prompts.

This gap isn’t just statistical—it reflects deep methodological flaws. As one commenter notes, it echoes the 'Doorman Fallacy': replacing a doorman with an automatic door seems efficient until you realize the doorman also de-escalated conflicts, remembered regulars’ preferences, and handled package deliveries. Similarly, benchmarking AI on isolated subtasks (e.g., 'write an email') ignores how those tasks embed within workflows requiring trust, iteration, ethics, and accountability. The study further shows that high-wage occupations—like social workers, nurses, and teachers—are especially underrepresented in benchmarks, even though their tasks dominate labor hours and societal impact.

The findings challenge widely cited claims about AI’s near-term labor displacement. If benchmarks don’t reflect real work, high scores say little about real-world readiness. Worse, the bias toward technical, codified tasks risks steering AI development—and investment—away from domains where human judgment, care, and adaptability remain irreplaceable. As another commenter observes, Baumol’s Cost Disease may now operate microscopically: even when AI boosts coding speed 20×, developers don’t spend more time coding—they shift to meetings, documentation, and quality assurance, tasks benchmarks ignore entirely.

Ultimately, the report calls for rethinking evaluation itself: not just what AI can do in isolation, but how well it integrates into human-centered workflows. Without benchmarks grounded in occupational reality—not academic abstractions—policy, education, and corporate adoption risk misallocating resources, overstating capabilities, and overlooking equity implications for lower-wage, high-touch roles.
