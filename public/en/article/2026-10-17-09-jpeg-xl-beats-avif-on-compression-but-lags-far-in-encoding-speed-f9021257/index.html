JPEG XL beats AVIF on compression but lags far in encoding speed

News time: 2026-10-17T05:33:27.819511+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new in-depth technical comparison by Jake Archibald examines how JPEG XL and AVIF stack up for real-world web use — not just on paper, but across speed, quality, tooling, and production readiness. The analysis draws on extensive benchmarking of photos, screenshots, and illustrations using perceptual metrics like Butteraugli, revealing a clear tradeoff: JPEG XL achieves measurably smaller files at maximum effort (2–25% gains depending on content), but only after painfully long encode times — up to 46 seconds per image. In contrast, AVIF delivers strong compression with far more practical encoding speeds and mature ecosystem support.

The article situates this debate within broader historical context: much like the decades-long refinement of MP3 encoders such as LAME, next-gen image codecs require time to mature beyond theoretical specs. JPEG XL’s high-effort mode relies on iterative optimization of quantization weights guided by Butteraugli — a human-vision-model-based error metric — which explains its slowness but also its precision. Meanwhile, AVIF, built on the AV1 video codec, benefits from widespread encoder adoption (e.g., libaom, rav1e) and growing CDN and CMS integration, even if its perceptual artifacts — like flattened highlights in high-contrast scenes — remain a concern for some experts.

Practical considerations dominate the conclusion. While JPEG XL shows promise as a future universal format — supporting lossless JPEG recompression, HDR, and animation — its current tooling (especially libjxl) is still developer-facing and slow. AVIF, though less efficient at extreme settings, works reliably today: FFmpeg-based pipelines, transparent PNG conversion, and responsive quality tuning make it viable for production sites serving thousands of images daily. One commenter notes achieving 10× size reduction over JPEG without visible degradation — a compelling win for bandwidth-constrained users.

Ultimately, the choice isn’t about declaring a ‘winner’ but matching the codec to the use case: JPEG XL for archival or offline-first scenarios where file size is paramount and time doesn’t matter; AVIF for dynamic, scalable web delivery where speed, compatibility, and tooling stability are non-negotiable.
