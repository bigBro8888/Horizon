eBay blocks uncertified radio gear but allows ham-band-only devices

News time: 2026-10-17T05:33:27.819434+00:00 · Source: hackernews

eBay has updated its marketplace policies to restrict listings of radio frequency (RF) transmitting equipment that lacks valid FCC certification — a move aimed at curbing the sale of noncompliant or potentially harmful devices in the U.S. The change, confirmed by the American Radio Relay League (ARRL), applies broadly to transceivers, amplifiers, and other RF-emitting hardware marketed for general use. However, a notable exception exists: devices designed exclusively for amateur radio bands — such as vintage receivers like the Drake 2-C or modern transceivers certified only for 1.8–54 MHz amateur allocations — are exempt from the new listing requirements.

This policy shift reflects growing platform-level enforcement of U.S. regulatory standards. Under FCC rules, most intentional radiators — devices designed to emit RF energy — must undergo formal equipment authorization before being imported or sold. Certification ensures they meet strict limits on spurious emissions, frequency stability, and RF exposure. While many consumer electronics (e.g., Wi-Fi routers, Bluetooth earbuds) carry built-in FCC IDs, low-cost or gray-market radios — especially modified Baofeng-style handhelds — often lack proper authorization and can transmit outside licensed bands, risking interference with public safety communications.

The exemption for ham-band-only gear acknowledges both technical reality and community practice. Amateur radio operators are licensed by the FCC and trained to operate within designated spectrum segments; their equipment is typically engineered to avoid out-of-band transmission. As one commenter noted, the restriction appears partly motivated by concerns over hacked Chinese-made radios being misused on emergency or commercial frequencies — not by distrust of licensed hams themselves. Still, critics question eBay’s role as de facto regulator, pointing out inconsistencies: high-power lasers and unregulated RF test gear remain widely available on the same platform.

For sellers and buyers, the practical impact centers on documentation. Listings now require visible FCC ID numbers — meaning many generic or uncertified radios will vanish from search results unless reclassified or recertified. Meanwhile, ham operators retain access to niche gear — including vintage receivers and homebrew-compatible transceivers — reinforcing the distinction between licensed spectrum users and unlicensed consumers. Ultimately, this isn’t just about compliance checkboxes: it’s a real-time negotiation between e-commerce governance, spectrum integrity, and the cultural identity of amateur radio as a self-regulating technical community.
