Researchers debate using AI to polish peer review drafts

News time: 2026-10-17T05:33:28.244827+00:00 · Source: reddit

A machine learning researcher recently sparked a nuanced conversation on Reddit by asking fellow reviewers: do you use AI tools to revise your own peer review drafts — not the papers you’re evaluating? The post, shared on r/MachineLearning, describes a personal workflow where the author writes a full review manually, then feeds only that draft into an AI model for feedback — catching typos, tightening phrasing, and flagging unclear arguments. Crucially, the author emphasizes they never upload the submitted paper itself, respecting authors’ autonomy and avoiding potential conflicts of interest.

This practice reflects a subtle but important shift in how AI is entering scholarly infrastructure. While much public attention focuses on AI-generated manuscripts or AI-assisted data analysis, this use case targets the reviewer’s labor: a traditionally invisible, unpaid, and high-stakes part of academic publishing. The author notes the process adds time — making reviews ‘even more work’ — yet reports consistent improvements in clarity, tone, and grammatical accuracy. It’s not about outsourcing judgment, but augmenting reflection.

The discussion quickly moved beyond simple yes/no answers. Commenters raised layered concerns: Could AI subtly homogenize critical voices or introduce hidden biases from training data? What happens if an AI hallucinates a citation or misrepresents a paper’s contribution? And who bears responsibility — the reviewer, the AI tool, or the journal — if a polished but flawed critique slips through? Several noted the absence of formal guidelines: most publishers and ethics boards have policies about AI use by authors, but remain silent on AI use by reviewers.

This gap matters because peer review is the cornerstone of scientific trust. Its integrity relies not just on correctness, but on transparency, accountability, and human discernment. As one commenter observed, ‘A review isn’t just feedback — it’s a scholarly act with moral weight.’ Without clear norms, individual choices risk creating uneven standards across journals and disciplines. The conversation underscores that AI adoption in academia isn’t just technical — it’s deeply procedural, ethical, and cultural.
