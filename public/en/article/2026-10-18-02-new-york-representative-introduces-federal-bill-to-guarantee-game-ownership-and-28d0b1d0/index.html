New York representative introduces federal bill to guarantee game ownership and post-support functionality

News time: 2026-10-18T05:50:46.692707+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a bold move challenging decades-old industry norms, U.S. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) has introduced the Video Game Ownership and Preservation Act, a federal bill that would fundamentally reshape how consumers acquire and retain digital video games. If passed, the law would require publishers to grant purchasers actual ownership—not merely a revocable license—of any video game sold in the United States. It further mandates that publishers maintain core functionality of their games for at least ten years after official support ends, including server-dependent features like multiplayer or cloud saves.

This proposal directly confronts the standard end-user license agreement (EULA) model, under which nearly all digital games—including titles from major publishers like Activision, EA, and Nintendo—are legally treated as licensed services rather than owned goods. Consumers routinely click “I agree” without realizing they hold no property rights: games can vanish from storefronts, servers can shut down permanently, and entire experiences become inaccessible overnight. The bill seeks to restore consumer expectations aligned with physical media—where buying a DVD or cartridge confers tangible, lasting rights.

The legislation also embeds preservation ethics into law. By requiring publishers to keep games functional—or provide tools enabling community-led maintenance—it acknowledges that games are cultural artifacts deserving long-term stewardship. This aligns with growing advocacy from groups like the Game Preservation Society, which warns that decaying hardware, lost source code, and restrictive copyright laws threaten to erase vast swaths of interactive history. Notably, the bill does not yet specify enforcement mechanisms or define “functional,” leaving room for technical and legal interpretation.

While widely praised by digital rights advocates, the proposal faces steep hurdles: it must clear committee review, survive lobbying from trade groups like the Entertainment Software Association (ESA), and reconcile with existing copyright frameworks. Still, its introduction signals a turning point—shifting the conversation from whether players should own games to how federal policy can make ownership meaningful in a networked, service-driven era.
