Recent master's graduate weighs self-funding NeurIPS workshop attendance

News time: 2026-10-18T05:50:47.027113+00:00 · Source: reddit

A recent master’s graduate has sparked quiet but relatable reflection in the machine learning community: they’ve been accepted to present a poster at a NeurIPS workshop—but their former lab declined to cover any costs, including registration, travel, or accommodation. Faced with this reality, they’re now considering paying out of pocket for a workshop-only registration, which is significantly cheaper than the full conference pass. Their Reddit post asks a deceptively simple question: Is it worth it—just for a workshop poster?

NeurIPS (the Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems) is one of the most prestigious venues in AI research. While the main conference draws thousands of attendees for keynotes, paper presentations, and job fairs, its workshops serve as more intimate, focused forums—often incubating emerging ideas, niche methodologies, or early-stage collaborations. For early-career researchers, especially those without institutional backing, workshops can offer meaningful exposure: direct feedback from domain experts, informal networking with peers and potential mentors, and visibility that may lead to future opportunities like internships, collaborations, or even PhD admissions.

That said, the financial calculus is real. A workshop-only registration for NeurIPS 2023 cost around $250–$400 USD for students, while full registration ranged from $700–$1,400 depending on affiliation and timing—and that’s before flights, lodging, and meals, which easily add $1,500–$3,000+ for international attendees. For someone just out of a master’s program, that’s often several months’ salary—or savings wiped out in one trip.

The decision isn’t purely economic, though. Many advise that even brief, targeted attendance can yield outsized returns: a 10-minute conversation with the right person, a follow-up email that leads to a collaboration, or simply the confidence boost of presenting work among respected peers. Still, others caution against normalizing unpaid academic labor—especially when systemic funding gaps disproportionately affect underrepresented or international students. Ultimately, the post resonates because it sits at the intersection of aspiration, access, and realism—a microcosm of broader tensions in how early-career researchers navigate prestige-driven ecosystems without guaranteed support.
