Cajal-1 predicts whole-cortex fMRI responses to audiovisual stimuli in real time

News time: 2026-10-19T06:08:52.621391+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new computational neuroscience model named Cajal-1 has achieved a major leap in brain–machine modeling: it predicts neural activity across the entire human cortex—20,484 distinct cortical locations—in response to naturalistic audiovisual inputs like films, podcasts, and music. Trained on functional MRI (fMRI) data from 2,000 individuals watching diverse visual and auditory content, the 218-million-parameter model outperforms TRIBE v2—the previous state-of-the-art encoding model—by 25% in accuracy on held-out film stimuli. Crucially, Cajal-1 operates at 1.49-second intervals, matching the temporal resolution of standard fMRI scanners—yet requires no scanner at all.

This isn’t just incremental progress. Cajal-1 is the first publicly released whole-cortex encoding model that unifies vision, audition, and language processing in a single architecture trained end-to-end on large-scale, real-world stimuli. Its name honors Santiago Ramón y Cajal, the pioneering Spanish neuroanatomist who, using Golgi’s silver stain in the 1890s, first revealed neurons as discrete cellular units—laying the foundation for modern neuroscience. Today, Cajal-1 translates that foundational insight into predictive power: given any video or audio clip, it simulates how the living human cortex would respond, point-by-point, without invasive hardware.

The model’s open release—including Hugging Face weights and an interactive Colab demo—lowers barriers for neuroscientists, cognitive modelers, and AI researchers alike. Unlike prior models constrained to specific regions (e.g., visual cortex only) or limited stimulus types, Cajal-1 generalizes across modalities and cortical territories. It also sidesteps the need for subject-specific calibration: predictions are made directly from stimulus features, not individualized brain scans.

While still an encoding (not decoding) model—meaning it maps stimuli to brain responses, not vice versa—its speed, scale, and anatomical coverage make it uniquely suited for hypothesis generation, experimental design, and educational simulation. Researchers can now test 'what-if' scenarios—e.g., how altering soundtrack timing affects predicted auditory cortex activation—before stepping into the scanner. In effect, Cajal-1 turns every laptop into a portable, scanner-free window into cortical dynamics.
