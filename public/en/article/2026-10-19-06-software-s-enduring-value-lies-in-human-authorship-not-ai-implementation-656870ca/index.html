Software’s enduring value lies in human authorship not AI implementation

News time: 2026-10-19T06:08:52.422498+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In an era where AI can generate code, suggest architectures, and even debug at superhuman speed, a quiet but urgent question is resurfacing: what part of software development remains irreplaceably human? Karrisa Arinen’s essay 'Someone behind the software' argues that the core value isn’t in writing lines of code or optimizing systems—it’s in the author’s vision, intentionality, and holistic judgment about what to build, why, and for whom. This isn’t nostalgia; it’s a design principle under pressure.

Arinen draws on Dorothy Sayers’ theological framework of creation—idea, implementation, and interaction—as echoed by Fred Brooks in The Mythical Man-Month. The ‘idea’ stage—the conception of purpose, boundaries, and coherence—is where human authorship lives. AI may excel at implementation, but it has no stake in the user’s lived reality, no moral or aesthetic compass for trade-offs, and no responsibility for the long-term integrity of a product’s soul.

This distinction matters most in SaaS, where teams increasingly outsource coding and even high-level design to AI tools. As one commenter notes, it’s now easier to add features than to rethink them—and so products bloat into incoherent collections of capabilities. Another observes how nearly every tool tries to become a ‘mega-product-for-everything’, echoing Jamie Zawinski’s famous quip about software scope creep—but amplified by AI’s capacity to generate endless integrations without strategic restraint.

The essay also challenges data-driven dogma. At Linear, the team famously values intuition over metrics—not because data is useless, but because early-stage vision cannot be reverse-engineered from dashboards. Great software, as several commenters affirm, is opinionated: it says ‘yes’ to some users and ‘no’ to others, reflects a coherent worldview, and holds firm against feature creep. That stance doesn’t vanish with AI—it becomes harder to uphold, and therefore more essential.
