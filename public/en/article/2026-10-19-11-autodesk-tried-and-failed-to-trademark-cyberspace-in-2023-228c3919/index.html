Autodesk tried and failed to trademark 'cyberspace' in 2023

News time: 2026-10-19T06:08:52.422466+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In early 2023, Autodesk, the California-based design software giant, filed a U.S. trademark application for the term 'cyberspace'—a word coined by science fiction author William Gibson in 1982 and central to digital culture for over four decades. The move sparked immediate backlash online, not only because of the term’s deep roots in cyberpunk literature and hacker ethos, but also due to its long-standing status as a generic descriptor of virtual, networked environments. Though Autodesk claimed the mark was intended for use in connection with VR/AR software and services, critics pointed out that ‘cyberspace’ functions like ‘email’ or ‘internet’: a linguistic commons, not a brandable product name.

The attempt wasn’t Autodesk’s first brush with this term. As recounted by Eric Gullichsen—a former member of Autodesk’s pioneering 1989–1990 virtual reality lab—the company had previously sought to trademark ‘cyberspace’ decades earlier, prompting Gibson himself to send a formal legal letter objecting. That earlier effort quietly collapsed; the 2023 filing met the same fate after the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) rejected it on grounds of ‘genericness’ and ‘descriptiveness’. In short: you can’t own a word that everyone already uses to describe a whole domain of experience.

What makes this episode especially resonant is its place in a broader pattern of corporate appropriation. As Hacker News commenters noted, Meta’s rebranding from Facebook leaned heavily on Neal Stephenson’s 1992 novel Snow Crash, which introduced the term ‘metaverse’—yet no credit or compensation was extended to the author. Similarly, ‘cyberpunk’ itself has been successfully trademarked in the U.S. for video games and apparel, despite describing an entire literary and aesthetic movement. These cases expose a growing tension between open cultural lexicons and proprietary branding logic.

At its core, the ‘cyberspace’ trademark saga is less about legal technicalities and more about who gets to shape—and profit from—the language of the digital age. Gibson didn’t patent his neologism; he released it into the wild, where it evolved through hacker forums, academic papers, and policy debates. That generosity helped seed the very technologies Autodesk now builds upon. When corporations try to fence off such foundational terms, they don’t just risk legal defeat—they risk revealing how disconnected they are from the collaborative, unowned origins of the digital world they seek to monetize.
