Faithful Lemmings port released for vintage Palm OS devices

News time: 2026-10-19T06:08:52.422562+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new open-source project on GitHub brings the beloved 1991 puzzle-platformer Lemmings back to life — not on modern smartphones or emulators, but natively on Palm OS, the pioneering mobile operating system that powered PDAs in the late 1990s and early 2000s. Developed by Aaron D. Gifford (known online as ardiri), the port runs directly on real Palm hardware or compatible emulators like POSE, preserving the tactile feel and constraints of the original platform: monochrome screen, stylus input, and severe memory limits.

This isn’t a reimagining or a remake — it’s a meticulous, low-level port. The code leverages Palm OS’s native APIs such as MemCopy and WinCopyBitmap, avoiding abstractions to squeeze performance from aging ARM7 or Motorola DragonBall processors. Unlike many retro ports that rely on cross-compilation toolchains or high-level wrappers, this implementation speaks Palm OS’s language fluently: 16-bit integers (UInt16), packed bitmap resources, and event-driven UI loops built around EvtGetEvent and SysTaskDelay.

The release has resonated deeply with veteran developers who cut their teeth on Palm OS. Comments highlight both technical appreciation — praising the elegance of Palm’s C library naming conventions — and historical reflection, noting parallels with other niche ports like the HP48 calculator version of Lemmings. One contributor recalls how the same developer previously removed Nintendo-themed clones after takedown requests, underscoring the legal tightrope retro porters often walk between homage and infringement.

Beyond nostalgia, the project serves as a quiet act of software archaeology. With Palm OS officially discontinued in 2011 and official SDKs long abandoned, each working port helps preserve not just a game, but the ecosystem’s design philosophy: minimalism, determinism, and developer proximity to the metal. As one commenter puts it, the README doesn’t just document code — it begs for context, inviting historians and engineers alike to annotate what made this era of mobile computing uniquely constrained — and creatively fertile.
