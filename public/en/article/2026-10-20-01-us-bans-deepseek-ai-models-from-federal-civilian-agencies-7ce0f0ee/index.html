US bans DeepSeek AI models from federal civilian agencies

News time: 2026-10-20T05:59:16.004312+00:00 · Source: hackernews

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security has issued a binding directive prohibiting all federal civilian agencies from using DeepSeek-developed artificial intelligence models—or any third-party products built with them—across their information technology systems. The order, published in the Federal Register under docket number 2026-21447, takes effect immediately and requires agencies to identify, isolate, and remove affected software within 90 days. It marks the first time the U.S. government has formally banned a specific open-weight AI model on national security grounds, setting a precedent for how it treats foreign-developed foundational AI infrastructure.

Unlike previous restrictions targeting Chinese hardware or cloud services, this directive focuses squarely on software supply chains: it applies not only to direct deployments of DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash or other DeepSeek models but also to tools, libraries, or applications that embed or rely on DeepSeek implementations—even if hosted entirely on U.S.-based infrastructure. The DHS cites concerns about potential hidden vulnerabilities, opaque training data provenance, and risks associated with model weights originating from jurisdictions lacking transparent AI governance frameworks.

DeepSeek, a Beijing-based AI research lab, has gained global traction for releasing high-performing open-weight models—including DeepSeek-Flash—under permissive licenses like Apache 2.0. These models are widely used by developers, startups, and even some government contractors for prototyping, fine-tuning, and inference. Their openness enables inspection and local hosting, distinguishing them from closed models like GPT-4 or Claude—but also raises novel questions about accountability when weights are public yet development oversight is not.

The ban has ignited debate across technical and policy communities. Some argue it reflects legitimate caution amid escalating U.S.–China tech competition; others warn it could stifle innovation, fragment open AI ecosystems, and set a slippery precedent for regulating code based on its origin rather than its behavior. Crucially, the order does not apply to defense or intelligence agencies—whose AI procurement rules are governed separately—and leaves unresolved whether derivative works (e.g., fine-tuned versions or code generated by DeepSeek) fall under its scope. As open-weight models continue to proliferate globally, this move signals a hardening of U.S. AI supply chain policy—one that treats model provenance as a security boundary.
