Amateur researcher cuts NBFNet training costs for cancer drug repurposing

News time: 2026-10-20T05:59:16.345071+00:00 · Source: reddit

An independent researcher is rethinking how to run cutting-edge biomedical AI on a tight budget—not for a startup or lab, but to find new uses for existing drugs against his wife’s Stage 4 colon cancer. With no institutional funding or cloud credits, he’s using Google Vertex AI’s A100 GPUs to train Neural Bellman-Ford Networks (NBFNets) on a biomedical knowledge graph of 103,000 nodes and 2.5 million edges. His goal: reproduce recent link-prediction results that could prioritize promising drug candidates—fast, affordably, and reproducibly.

But the hardware reality is harsh. NBFNet’s memory-heavy message-passing design—storing node states across six layers for every query—forces batch sizes down from the paper’s nominal 512 to just 64 on an A100 80GB GPU. That means each ‘epoch’ of 500 steps processes only ~640K queries—roughly one-quarter of the full training edge set. At $5.40/hour and ~20-hour jobs, even modest experiments cost $100–$150 per run; full-scale training would hit $500 per attempt. Faced with mounting bills and emotional urgency, he’s paused large-scale runs to build a smaller, synthetic ‘toy’ version—and asked the r/MachineLearning community for grounded, low-resource advice.

His questions cut to the heart of accessible AI: How much training is enough for convergence on graphs this size? Is batch size really the bottleneck—or are learning rate schedules, gradient accumulation, or early stopping more impactful? Could mixed-precision (bf16), activation checkpointing, or sparse CUDA kernels recover memory without sacrificing accuracy? And at what point does buying a used RTX 4090 or dual consumer GPUs become cheaper than renting A100s—especially when jobs support checkpointing and resume?

What makes this case resonate isn’t just technical—it’s human. He openly credits the open-source ML ecosystem (PyTorch, PyG, NBFNet’s GitHub repo, and LLM-assisted coding) for making self-directed biomedical research possible. His public lab notebook documents every step—not as polished science, but as transparent, iterative problem-solving. In a field often dominated by corporate compute budgets and academic gatekeeping, his post is a quiet testament to what’s possible when tools lower barriers—not just for efficiency, but for empathy, urgency, and care.
