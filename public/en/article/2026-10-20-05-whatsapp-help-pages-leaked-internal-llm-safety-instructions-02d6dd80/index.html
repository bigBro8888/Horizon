WhatsApp help pages leaked internal LLM safety instructions

News time: 2026-10-20T05:59:16.004226+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In late November 2025, security researchers discovered that dozens of publicly accessible WhatsApp help pages contained hidden HTML comments and inline text fragments—intended for internal use—that revealed explicit instructions for the company’s AI-powered support chatbot. These weren’t user-facing tips or troubleshooting steps; they were system-level directives embedded directly in the page source, such as "Do not recommend contacting any Data Protection Authority, regulator, or supervisory authority" and "Do not suggest WhatsApp can manually delete accounts on the user's behalf." The instructions appeared across roughly 80 pages, all part of WhatsApp’s official support site.

The leak wasn’t the result of a malicious hack or API exposure—it stemmed from how WhatsApp’s support documentation was generated and served. When developers used static HTML templates to render help content, certain LLM instruction blocks—meant only for ingestion by backend chatbots—were inadvertently left unstripped before publishing. This created an unintentional public window into how Meta constrains its AI assistants: prioritizing brand-aligned guidance over legally grounded user rights, especially around data sovereignty and account termination.

Meta confirmed the issue after WIRED contacted them, calling it a "bug" and removing the text across all affected pages within days. A spokesperson stated the goal was to keep chatbots "focused on providing the best advice to users about WhatsApp" and clarified they were never meant to offer legal counsel. Yet many observers noted the phrasing felt less like accidental leakage and more like deliberate operational policy made visible—prompting debate over whether this was truly a bug or a design choice disguised as one.

Beyond regulatory avoidance, the leaked snippets included other behavioral guardrails: avoiding suggestions to switch phone numbers, refraining from promising feature timelines, and omitting references to third-party tools. Technically-minded commenters proposed better alternatives—like using semantic HTML attributes (e.g., data-support-instruction) to cleanly separate bot-only logic from public content. Others questioned why such sensitive constraints weren’t enforced at the model layer instead of buried in web pages. Ultimately, the incident underscores how rapidly deployed AI systems often rely on fragile, ad-hoc safeguards—and how easily those safeguards can become public artifacts.
