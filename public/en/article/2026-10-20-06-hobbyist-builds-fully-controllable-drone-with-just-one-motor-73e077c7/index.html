Hobbyist builds fully controllable drone with just one motor

News time: 2026-10-20T05:59:16.004290+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a striking demonstration of mechanical creativity and control theory, hobbyist engineer Joshua Bird has built a functional drone that flies and maneuvers in three dimensions using only a single motor. Unlike conventional multirotor drones—where four or more motors provide independent thrust—and unlike helicopters, which rely on complex swashplate mechanisms to tilt rotor pitch, Bird’s design replaces both with an elegant, custom-built hinge system. Mounted on a lightweight carbon fiber frame, the drone’s sole propeller spins continuously, while real-time torque modulation—enabled by a magnetic encoder and precise motor control—adjusts lift and thrust direction moment-by-moment as the blade rotates.

The core innovation lies in how the system exploits underactuation: though it has only one actuator (the motor), it achieves full 6-degree-of-freedom control by coupling rapid torque changes with passive mechanical dynamics. As the propeller spins, the hinge allows subtle, asymmetric blade deflections—timed precisely via encoder feedback—to generate controlled roll, pitch, yaw, and vertical thrust. This eliminates the need for multiple motors, servos, or traditional pitch-linkage systems. The result is not just a novelty, but a working proof-of-concept that rethinks how aerial vehicles can be actuated and controlled.

Bird’s approach draws parallels to other minimalist robotic systems—like single-motor BattleBots that spin up impactors to deliver directional force, or variable-pitch propeller (VPP) fixed-wing RC aircraft that achieve extreme aerobatics without separate control surfaces. Yet his drone differs fundamentally: it doesn’t vary blade pitch mechanically per revolution, nor does it rely on forward airspeed or gyroscopic precession. Instead, it modulates electromagnetic torque within each motor rotation cycle to steer lift vector orientation—a technique rooted in field-oriented control (FOC) and real-time PMSM torque modeling.

While not yet practical for commercial or outdoor use—its flight time is short, stability demands constant computation, and the spinning rotor poses safety and noise challenges—the project resonates deeply in robotics education and DIY engineering circles. It illustrates how deep understanding of dynamics, sensing, and control can turn theoretical constraints (like underactuation) into design advantages. For students and tinkerers, it’s less about replacing quadcopters and more about expanding what’s considered possible with minimal hardware—and reminding us that sometimes, the most powerful innovations begin with asking, 'What if we used just one?'
