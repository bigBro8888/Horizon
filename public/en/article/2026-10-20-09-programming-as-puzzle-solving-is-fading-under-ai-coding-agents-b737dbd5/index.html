Programming as puzzle-solving is fading under AI coding agents

News time: 2026-10-20T05:59:15.610959+00:00 · Source: rss

Jeremy Scheff’s essay 'My obituary for programming' isn’t a eulogy for the profession itself—but for a particular kind of joy that once defined it: the deep, self-directed satisfaction of solving logic puzzles with code. For decades, programming offered something rare in knowledge work: paid intellectual play. Developers weren’t just executing tasks; they were architects, strategists, and puzzle-solvers who negotiated constraints, invented abstractions, and owned the entire arc from problem to working system. As Scheff puts it, 'You got paid to work on tricky little logic puzzles. The trickier the puzzle, the more you got paid.' That intrinsic reward—figuring out how to make something work, step by step, without being handed the answer—was central to professional identity for many.

The rise of LLM-powered coding agents changes that dynamic fundamentally. These tools don’t just automate boilerplate—they increasingly generate correct, production-ready solutions to nontrivial problems in seconds. For developers who primarily value building robust systems, this is liberating: less time wrestling with syntax or debugging edge cases, more time designing architecture and understanding user needs. But for those whose core motivation lies in the act of solving, the shift feels like losing a language they loved to speak.

Scheff draws a quiet but sharp distinction between two developer archetypes: the system-builder, energized by integration, scale, and outcomes; and the puzzle-solver, sustained by the elegance of a clean algorithm or the 'aha' moment when a stubborn bug yields to reasoning. AI doesn’t erase either role—but it hollows out the middle ground where logic puzzles were both the means and the end. When the puzzle is solved before you’ve even sketched the first function, what remains to savor?

This isn’t about job loss—it’s about craft erosion. The essay resonates because it names a subtle grief many experienced developers feel but rarely articulate: not fear of replacement, but mourning for a mode of thinking that shaped how they saw themselves. As AI handles more of the 'how', the human role pivots toward defining the 'why' and 'what for'—a vital shift, but one that demands new sources of meaning. The question isn’t whether programming will survive, but which parts of its soul we choose to preserve.
