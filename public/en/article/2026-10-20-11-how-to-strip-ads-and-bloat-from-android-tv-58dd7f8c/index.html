How to strip ads and bloat from Android TV

News time: 2026-10-20T05:59:16.004336+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Android TV has long promised a seamless smart TV experience—but for many users, it delivers instead a sluggish, ad-saturated interface that feels more like a compromised kiosk than a living-room appliance. A new practical guide on Cretezy outlines how everyday users can reclaim control: by replacing Google’s default launcher with lightweight, open-source alternatives and switching to privacy-first media clients like Wholphin or Moonfin. The approach doesn’t require root access or custom firmware—just basic ADB commands and careful app selection.

The core problem lies in Google’s post-purchase evolution of Android TV. As one commenter notes, factory resets still show outdated Play Store screenshots—years after full-screen ads were quietly added to the home screen and recommendations feed. These ads aren’t just visual clutter; they slow down navigation, consume bandwidth, and track viewing habits without meaningful consent. Worse, the default launcher runs persistently in the background, competing for system resources even when unused.

The solution starts with disabling the stock launcher via ADB: adb shell pm disable-user --user 0 com.google.android.apps.tv.launcherx. Users then install and set a replacement—such as LTvLauncher (open-source, Flutter-based), Projectivy Launcher (highly customizable), or ATV Launcher Pro. These launchers offer clean grids, remote-friendly navigation, and zero telemetry. For media playback, the guide recommends moving away from Google TV’s built-in apps toward self-hosted options like Jellyfin, accessed via lean clients that avoid analytics, autoplay, and forced sign-ins.

This isn’t theoretical tinkering—it’s a growing consensus among privacy-conscious home media users. As one HN commenter puts it, they treat their smart TVs as ‘dumb displays’ and plug in dedicated devices (like mini-PCs running Plasma Bigscreen) instead. Others go further, lamenting the absence of a true ‘stupid TV’—a device that boots instantly, shows HDMI input, and does nothing else. The movement reflects deeper dissatisfaction with the industry’s shift from hardware-first design to OS-driven monetization—and signals demand for modularity, transparency, and user sovereignty in living-room tech.
