What foundational values should parents teach children in a changing world

News time: 2026-10-21T05:59:49.235129+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A parent’s quiet question—'What shall I teach my son?'—has resonated far beyond its modest origin. Posted on the personal website of Steve Krouse, the essay is less a how-to guide and more a meditation on intergenerational continuity amid accelerating technological and economic shifts. The author doesn’t offer bullet-pointed curricula or AI-powered learning tools. Instead, he invites reflection: if vocational skills become obsolete within decades—or even generations—what enduring capacities matter most? His framing opens space for deeper inquiry into education’s purpose: Is it preparation for a job? A vehicle for social mobility? Or something quieter and more resilient—a way to nurture curiosity, integrity, and the ability to navigate uncertainty?

The post quickly drew thoughtful engagement on Hacker News, where commenters challenged popular myths that often shape parenting anxieties. One user corrected the widespread belief that Einstein struggled in school, noting he earned top marks in math and physics; another pointed out that Bill Gates’ college dropout status obscures his elite academic background and early research contributions. These corrections aren’t pedantic—they reveal how cultural shorthand about 'self-made' success can distort our understanding of opportunity, privilege, and preparation.

Other voices emphasized historical perspective. A commenter traced their family’s labor lineage—from Scottish sugar house workers in the 1800s to modern knowledge workers—and observed that occupational extinction is nothing new. Professions vanish; people adapt. Another reframed education not as skill transmission but as societal infrastructure: a safe, structured environment where children grow alongside peers while parents meet other essential responsibilities. Still others advocated for low-tech, high-touch foundations: reading aloud with joy and improvisation, hiking local trails, browsing farmers’ markets together, listening closely to children’s questions—even when answers are unknown.

Ultimately, the discussion coalesces around a subtle but powerful idea: teaching isn’t about installing fixed knowledge, but cultivating resourcefulness—the capacity to learn, unlearn, connect, and act meaningfully in unfamiliar terrain. That capacity, many suggest, grows not from standardized tests or coding bootcamps alone, but from sustained presence, shared wonder, and the quiet confidence that comes from being deeply known and safely held.
