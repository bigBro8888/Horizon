Simon Willison builds audio-synced RTS game with AI assistance

News time: 2026-10-21T05:59:48.895064+00:00 · Source: rss

Simon Willison, a well-known developer and former co-founder of Django, has released Battle Drones — a browser-based real-time strategy (RTS) game inspired by classic titles like Command & Conquer. Unlike traditional RTS games built over months or years, Battle Drones emerged from an experimental, rapid-iteration workflow he calls 'vibe coding': describing high-level goals in plain English to AI assistants (primarily Claude Code), accepting generated code, and refining it through quick feedback loops. The result is a surprisingly polished, mobile-friendly game featuring fog of war, unit-specific weapon systems, randomized maps, and a full suite of 3D models — all built in just 12 major versions and 321 commits.

What sets Battle Drones apart is its unconventional multiplayer implementation. In version 12, Willison introduced local peer-to-peer synchronization using audio: two devices within earshot exchange ultrasonic tones to align game state in real time. This avoids reliance on servers, network permissions, or WebRTC setup — making multiplayer as simple as holding phones near each other. Though clever, the approach proved physically taxing: players reported headaches from sustained ultrasonic exposure, prompting Willison to later add conventional online multiplayer support as a safer alternative.

The project also reflects a broader shift in how developers prototype. Rather than designing architecture first, Willison prioritized playability — even admitting he told Claude, 'Make it a LOT more fun,' to break past early tedium. His iterative path — from minimal prototype (v1) to full sprites (v2), varied terrain (v3), 3D rendering (v5), agent-driven battles (v10), and finally audio sync (v12) — demonstrates how AI can accelerate not just coding, but design thinking itself. Every version was tested, tweaked, and reshaped based on immediate user experience, not theoretical completeness.

While Battle Drones isn’t intended for commercial release, its value lies in its transparency and teachability. Willison documents every step publicly — including prompts used with Claude, trade-offs made, and dead ends explored. It serves as both a working demo of vibe coding’s potential and a candid case study in balancing speed, creativity, and technical pragmatism. For indie developers and educators alike, it offers a refreshingly human-centered model of AI-augmented creation — where the goal isn’t perfect code, but joyful, shareable play.
