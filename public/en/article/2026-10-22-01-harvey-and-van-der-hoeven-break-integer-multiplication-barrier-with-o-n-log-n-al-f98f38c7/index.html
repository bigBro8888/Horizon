Harvey and van der Hoeven break integer multiplication barrier with o(n log n) algorithm

News time: 2026-10-22T06:05:39.885551+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a landmark theoretical advance, mathematicians David Harvey and Joris van der Hoeven have constructed the first provably o(n log n) algorithm for multiplying two n-digit integers. Published in their 2019 paper and refined in subsequent work—including the recently circulated preprint—their approach improves upon the long-standing Schönhage–Strassen algorithm (1971), which ran in O(n log n log log n) time. This new result resolves a decades-old open problem in computational complexity: whether integer multiplication could be done asymptotically faster than n log n. The answer is yes—strictly faster, in the little-o sense—meaning the ratio of the algorithm’s runtime to n log n tends to zero as n grows.

The breakthrough builds on deep ideas from algebraic number theory and fast Fourier transforms over carefully chosen rings, including cyclotomic fields and modular arithmetic with special moduli. Rather than relying solely on FFTs over complex numbers or finite fields—as earlier methods did—their construction uses a recursive combination of multidimensional FFTs and novel coefficient reduction techniques. Crucially, it avoids the log log n overhead that had persisted since Schönhage–Strassen by eliminating repeated rounding and precision management through tighter algebraic control.

While the algorithm is theoretically optimal in asymptotic terms, its practical impact remains negligible for any real-world computation. The hidden constants are enormous, and crossover points—where it would outperform highly optimized libraries like GMP—are estimated to lie far beyond numbers with more digits than atoms in the observable universe. As one commenter noted, this is a galactic algorithm: correct, profound, but irrelevant to engineering practice today.

Nonetheless, the implications ripple across theoretical computer science. It tightens longstanding bounds in related problems—such as polynomial multiplication over finite fields—and has already spurred formal verification efforts in Lean. A team has fully mechanized the core bound in Mathlib’s Turing machine model, confirming the o(n log n) claim and iteratively improving the exponent. This synergy between cutting-edge mathematics, formal methods, and community-driven verification exemplifies how foundational advances now unfold in open, collaborative technical ecosystems.
