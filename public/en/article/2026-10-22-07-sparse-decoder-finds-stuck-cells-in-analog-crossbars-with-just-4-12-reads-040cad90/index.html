Sparse decoder finds stuck cells in analog crossbars with just 4–12 reads

News time: 2026-10-22T06:05:40.038504+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new preprint introduces a highly efficient method for fault localization in analog crossbar tiles—key hardware components for in-memory computing and neuromorphic accelerators. Instead of reading all 65,536 cells in a 256×256 tile to find defective ones, the approach uses only 4 to 12 random probe reads—each sending a ±1 input vector through the array and measuring the output. By modeling stuck-at faults as a sparse signal (i.e., only a few cells are faulty), the authors apply compressive sensing principles to reconstruct the fault map from this tiny number of measurements. Simulations show it reliably identifies up to 64 stuck cells under 1% read noise—a realistic level for analog hardware.

The method outperforms several baselines. Compared to a hybrid scheme built on Wei and Roth’s multi-outlier detection code (extended here to cell-level localization), it reduces required reads by 4.3–6.4×—or 1.9–3.2× if calibration overhead is included. It also beats tuned X-ABFT, a common analog fault-tolerance technique, though that comparison is a stress test: X-ABFT wasn’t designed for such high fault densities. Crucially, the paper disentangles two contributions: the sparse decoder itself (which already cuts reads by 1.6–4.1× using forward reads alone) and the added benefit of transposed (reverse-direction) reads (yielding another 1.3–4× gain at low noise).

Beyond fault localization, the work includes a practical 16-parameter drift calibration model fitted per tile from a few probes. When applied digitally (requiring stored weight data), it boosts CIFAR-10 accuracy by up to 13.3 percentage points over baseline after one year of simulated drift. Even when applied directly in the analog array—without digital weight access—it preserves +0.6 to +3.7 points. The authors transparently report five pre-registered claims that failed—including two about transposed-read benefits and one about drift structure specificity—reinforcing scientific rigor.

Limitations are clearly stated: results are simulation-only; only stuck-at faults are considered; the model assumes white, independent read noise and ignores peripheral cost of bidirectional access. There’s no silicon validation, and absolute accuracy wasn’t verified against CrossSim’s ground truth (differences up to 5.66 points). Still, the work provides a concrete, reproducible step toward scalable testing and calibration of analog AI hardware—where exhaustive readout is prohibitively slow and energy-intensive.
