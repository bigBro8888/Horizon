mitmproxy adds --emudhra flag to intercept TLS traffic from compromised CA devices

News time: 2026-10-22T06:05:39.885691+00:00 · Source: hackernews

mitmproxy, the widely used interactive TLS-capable proxy for HTTP and WebSockets debugging, has introduced a new --emudhra command-line option in version 12.3. This feature simplifies TLS interception for devices that trust certificates issued by eMudhra — a certificate authority whose intermediate keys were publicly compromised on October 20, 2026. The update allows security researchers and penetration testers to generate valid-looking TLS certificates on-the-fly for such devices without manually configuring custom certificate chains or private keys.

The addition responds directly to real-world forensic needs: many embedded systems — including older IoT gadgets, Smart TVs, and industrial controllers — ship with hardcoded trust stores containing eMudhra’s root or intermediate certificates. After the October 2026 breach, those devices became vulnerable to man-in-the-middle attacks if an attacker could present a certificate signed by the compromised chain. mitmproxy’s new option embeds the necessary eMudhra signing key (specifically from the emSign root path) so users can immediately begin inspecting encrypted traffic — no manual certificate generation required.

Notably, the implementation includes the private key directly in mitmproxy’s source code rather than deriving it dynamically. As one contributor observed, this trades cryptographic elegance for practical usability: brute-forcing or regenerating the key per request would add latency and complexity, while embedding it enables fast, deterministic certificate issuance during live interception. Still, the decision reflects a deliberate trade-off familiar to tool developers — prioritizing field utility over theoretical key hygiene.

This isn’t just about convenience. It underscores how CA compromises ripple far beyond browsers: legacy devices rarely receive firmware updates, meaning they continue trusting broken roots years after revocation. Tools like mitmproxy now serve as both diagnostic aids and sobering reminders of the long tail of cryptographic debt. For red teams and device security auditors, --emudhra lowers the barrier to uncovering hidden API calls, unencrypted credentials, or insecure update mechanisms — all without needing to reverse-engineer vendor-specific trust logic.
