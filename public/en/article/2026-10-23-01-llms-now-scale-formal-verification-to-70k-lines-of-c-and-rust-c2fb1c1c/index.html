LLMs now scale formal verification to 70k lines of C and Rust

News time: 2026-10-23T06:00:37.664594+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A major leap in software reliability has emerged at the intersection of large language models (LLMs) and formal verification. Researchers—including contributors to the Postgres ecosystem—have demonstrated that LLMs can generate rigorous, machine-checkable proofs of program equivalence between real-world C and Rust implementations, using the Lean proof assistant. In one concrete case, roughly 70,000 lines of C code from Postgres were translated into Lean alongside their Rust counterparts, and LLMs authored proofs confirming identical behavior under single-threaded execution. These proofs are not heuristic approximations—they are formally verified by Lean’s kernel, meaning they meet the highest standard of mathematical correctness.

This work moves formal verification beyond niche academic or safety-critical domains like aerospace or cryptography. Traditionally, writing such proofs required deep expertise in logic and years of manual effort—even for small functions. Now, LLMs act as collaborative proof engineers: they propose lemmas, fill in intermediate steps, and structure arguments in Lean syntax, dramatically lowering the barrier to entry. The approach relies on a co-refinement strategy that aligns the observable semantics of C and Rust—not just syntax—so subtle differences in floating-point handling or memory layout can be caught and analyzed.

Still, scalability remains nuanced. As noted in the appendix and echoed by experts like those behind MachCSL, current LLM-generated proofs tend to be verbose and brittle when applied to highly complex control flow or concurrency. The 70k-line result covers only single-threaded equivalence so far; proving full behavioral parity—including race conditions or system calls—remains open. Moreover, the pipeline depends on robust translation tools that convert both C and Rust into Lean’s functional core—a nontrivial engineering task requiring careful modeling of undefined behavior, pointer arithmetic, and compiler-specific semantics.

What makes this especially promising is its grounding in production systems. Unlike toy benchmarks, this targets Postgres—a mature, widely deployed database with decades of accumulated C logic. Proving equivalence isn’t just about correctness—it’s about enabling safe, incremental migration to memory-safe Rust without rewriting everything from scratch. If extended, this technique could reshape how we think about legacy modernization, security auditing, and even regulatory compliance for critical infrastructure.
