Quad9 shuts down French DNS service after court-ordered blocking dispute

News time: 2026-10-23T06:00:37.664625+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Quad9, the nonprofit public DNS resolver known for prioritizing user privacy and security, has announced it will cease operations in France effective November 24, 2023. The decision follows a legal ruling by a French court ordering Quad9 to block domain names associated with alleged copyright infringement — specifically sites linked to unauthorized streaming of Sony Music’s content. Quad9 contested the order on technical and principle-based grounds, arguing that DNS resolvers are infrastructure components, not content publishers or gatekeepers, and that compelling them to filter domains violates both internet architecture norms and fundamental privacy commitments.

The conflict escalated when Sony Music Entertainment GmbH used a VPN to access blocked domains from Germany — demonstrating that geoIP-based filtering, which Quad9 implemented as a compliance measure, could be easily circumvented. The French court interpreted this as noncompliance and fined Quad9 for contempt. As Quad9’s CTO John Todd explained in community comments, the company views geoIP blocking as technically inadequate for precise, domain-level censorship — yet faced no viable alternative that satisfied the court without compromising its global service model or core mission.

This case is not isolated. French courts have previously issued similar web-blocking injunctions against major DNS providers including Google Public DNS, Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1, and Cisco’s OpenDNS. While Google and Cloudflare ultimately complied under legal pressure, Cisco exited the French market entirely in 2021 — a precedent Quad9 now follows. Unlike content platforms, DNS resolvers do not host or curate material; they merely translate human-readable domain names into machine-readable IP addresses. Holding them legally liable for how third parties use those translations sets a consequential precedent for internet governance.

Quad9’s withdrawal underscores a growing tension between national copyright enforcement regimes and the decentralized, borderless nature of internet infrastructure. It raises urgent questions: Can jurisdictional mandates force global technical services to act as de facto censors? And if geoIP filters are both insufficient and legally insufficient, what accountability mechanisms remain for infrastructure providers caught between court orders and engineering reality? For users in France, the immediate impact is reduced choice among privacy-respecting DNS options — and a sobering reminder that neutrality at the DNS layer is increasingly fragile.
