Frontier multimodal LLMs struggle to identify ASMR triggers

News time: 2026-10-23T06:00:38.024917+00:00 · Source: reddit

A new benchmark reveals a surprising blind spot in today’s most advanced multimodal large language models: they perform poorly at recognizing subtle, non-speech audio cues central to ASMR—like paper rustling, brushing, tapping, or crinkling. Researcher MissAppleby, developing a text-to-ASMR generator called text2asmr, tested eight leading models—including Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-4o Audio, Claude Opus (spectrogram mode), and MiMo v2.6 Pro—on 480 short audio clips. Human-labeled ground truth came from two sources: clean sound-effect clips from FSD50K and ESC-50 (measuring recall), and real ASMR recordings labeled by multiple people on a custom platform (measuring F1 score). The results were striking: F1 scores ranged from just 0.11 to 0.33 across models—far below human consistency.

What made the gap especially revealing was the contrast between domains. On standardized sound-effect benchmarks like FSD50K, Gemini 3.1 Pro correctly identified ‘brushing’ 92% of the time—but on actual ASMR clips, its F1 for brushing dropped to only 0.18. Similarly, it detected ‘paper rustling’ in just 4 out of 40 real ASMR clips. In contrast, voice-related triggers like whispering fared much better (F1 0.70–0.79), suggesting models rely heavily on speech-like features while failing on fine-grained physical sound events. Each model also exhibited strong biases—Gemini 2.5 Pro, for instance, falsely labeled ‘sticky’ 59 times where no such sound existed.

The researcher didn’t stop at diagnosis. To build a usable labeling pipeline for their 126k-clip dataset, they fused outputs from all models using Dawid-Skene aggregation—weighting each judge per class by its measured reliability against human labels. Low-confidence clips and classes no model could reliably detect (e.g., paper, cutting, spraying) are prioritized for human review. Their fine-tuned CLAP-ASMR model improved mAP slightly with added Freesound data—but still failed completely (0% detection) on external brushing and paper clips, confirming the labeling gap remains unsolved by current models.

This work underscores that high performance on standard audio classification tasks doesn’t guarantee competence in domain-specific, perceptually nuanced audio understanding. For generative audio applications—from therapeutic ASMR tools to immersive sound design—the ability to parse delicate foley-like triggers isn’t optional—it’s foundational. And right now, humans remain irreplaceable in that layer of auditory perception.
