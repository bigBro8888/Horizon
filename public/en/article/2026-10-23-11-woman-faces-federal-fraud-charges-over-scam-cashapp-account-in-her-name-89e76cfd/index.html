Woman faces federal fraud charges over scam CashApp account in her name

News time: 2026-10-23T06:00:37.664663+00:00 · Source: hackernews

Amanda Smith, a Georgia woman with no prior criminal record, is facing up to five years in federal prison under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) — not for hacking or stealing, but because a fraudulent CashApp account was opened using her name, Social Security number, and driver’s license. According to reporting by 404 Media, investigators traced $12,000 in scam proceeds to an account registered under Smith’s identity. She insists she never created the account, never received its funds, and had no knowledge of its existence until law enforcement contacted her.

The case hinges on digital identity verification failures. CashApp’s onboarding process requires users to submit government-issued ID and basic personal details — but does not mandate real-time biometric checks, liveness detection, or multi-factor confirmation that the person enrolling is the legitimate owner of the documents. Public records and court filings suggest the scammer likely obtained Smith’s identity documents through a data breach or public records scrape, then used them to pass CashApp’s automated verification.

Federal prosecutors charged Smith under 18 U.S.C. § 1030(a)(4), which criminalizes knowingly accessing a computer without authorization to further fraud. Critics argue this stretches the CFAA far beyond its original intent — designed for hackers breaching systems — to penalize victims of identity theft who never touched a keyboard in connection with the crime. Legal experts point out that traditional identity theft statutes (like 18 U.S.C. § 1028) would more appropriately apply, but those carry lower penalties and less prosecutorial leverage.

Smith has rejected a plea deal offering $1,000 fine, $1,000 restitution, and three years’ probation — insisting on her innocence despite mounting legal costs and risk of harsher sentencing if convicted at trial. Her case has ignited widespread concern among privacy advocates, defense attorneys, and fintech engineers about how platforms design identity controls — and how easily flawed verification can trigger disproportionate legal consequences. As one commentator noted, the burden of proof appears inverted: instead of proving Smith acted, authorities expect her to disprove involvement in a system she didn’t build or control.
