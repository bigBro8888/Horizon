ICLR allows arXiv preprints despite double-blind review

News time: 2026-10-24T05:46:59.826012+00:00 · Source: reddit

A PhD student recently raised a widely shared concern on Reddit: if ICLR uses double-blind review—where reviewers must not know authors’ identities—why does the conference explicitly permit papers to appear on arXiv before submission? The question cuts to the heart of modern machine learning publishing, where speed, visibility, and fairness often pull in opposite directions. Many researchers post drafts to arXiv within hours of finishing experiments—not just to stake priority, but to solicit early feedback, attract collaborators, and avoid being scooped. Yet that same preprint makes it trivial for a reviewer to identify authors by searching the title or abstract.

ICLR’s official policy resolves this tension pragmatically: it permits arXiv preprints and enforces double-blind review simultaneously. How? By placing responsibility on reviewers to ignore publicly available versions and on authors to anonymize submissions—removing names, affiliations, acknowledgments, and even subtle identifiers like self-citations phrased as “we previously showed…” in the submitted PDF. Crucially, the conference does not require authors to take down or edit their arXiv posts; instead, it trusts the review process to rely on procedural discipline rather than secrecy.

This approach reflects a broader shift across top ML venues. NeurIPS, ICML, and ACL all allow preprints under similar conditions. A 2022 study analyzing over 1,900 peer reviews found no statistically significant increase in bias after double-blind adoption—even when preprints were publicly available—suggesting reviewers largely adhere to guidelines when reminded. Still, the system isn’t perfect: junior researchers report feeling pressured to preprint for visibility while fearing unconscious bias, and some reviewers admit they occasionally search arXiv out of curiosity or to check related work.

Ultimately, ICLR’s stance acknowledges reality: banning preprints would disadvantage researchers without institutional prestige or fast-turnaround labs, while enforcing strict anonymity is technically unenforceable online. The trade-off is transparency and speed versus idealized impartiality—and the field has collectively chosen the former, with guardrails. For new PhD students, the takeaway isn’t whether to preprint, but how: use anonymous arXiv metadata (e.g., omit author names in the title field), delay linking GitHub repos until after decisions, and always submit a fully anonymized PDF to the conference.
