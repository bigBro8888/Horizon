Engineers should aim higher than incremental software improvements

News time: 2026-10-25T06:02:51.045097+00:00 · Source: hackernews

In a reflective and widely discussed essay, Dan Luu challenges the software engineering community to rethink its ambitions. Rather than focusing on narrow optimizations—like shaving milliseconds off a CLI tool or tweaking a caching layer—he urges engineers to tackle problems with deeper technical, social, or human impact: building tools that reshape how people understand codebases, navigate geographic data, or interact with complex systems. The piece arrives at a pivotal moment, as AI—especially large language models—lowers the barrier to prototyping but also risks reinforcing shallow, MVP-first habits.

Luu’s argument isn’t anti-optimization; it’s pro-intentionality. He observes that many teams spend disproportionate energy on performance tweaks for edge cases while neglecting foundational questions: What problem truly matters? Who is underserved? What would feel like progress five years from now? This tension echoes across the HN discussion, where developers share personal experiments—like a Rust-based Python interpreter or an infinite-zoom code visualization—that prioritize learning, expressiveness, and long-term insight over shipping speed or metrics.

The rise of LLMs adds urgency to this call. As one commenter notes, AI lets someone ‘go from 0 to MVP in a prompt,’ collapsing early-stage development. But that ease masks the harder work that follows: sustaining reliability, managing integrations, making product tradeoffs, and designing for real users—not just demo appeal. Ambition, then, isn’t about scale or novelty alone; it’s about committing to the full lifecycle, including the messy, unglamorous parts after launch.

Ultimately, Luu’s essay invites a cultural reset—not toward grandiose moonshots, but toward grounded, thoughtful scope. It asks engineers to treat software not as a series of tactical wins, but as a medium for durable, human-centered solutions. In an era where anyone can generate a working prototype in minutes, the rarest skill may no longer be coding—it’s choosing what’s worth building, and seeing it through.
