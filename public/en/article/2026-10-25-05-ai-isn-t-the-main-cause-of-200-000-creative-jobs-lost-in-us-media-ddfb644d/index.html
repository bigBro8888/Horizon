AI isn’t the main cause of 200,000 creative jobs lost in US media

News time: 2026-10-25T06:02:51.045158+00:00 · Source: hackernews

The U.S. media, film, and arts sectors have shed roughly 200,000 jobs — a stark decline that has sparked widespread concern and finger-pointing at artificial intelligence. But a nuanced analysis from Apricitas argues that while AI plays a role, it is neither the sole nor even the primary driver. Instead, deeper structural forces — decades-long offshoring for cost efficiency, the saturation of the attention economy, and shifting corporate subsidy models — are doing far more heavy lifting in reshaping creative labor.

Consider the attention economy: as digital content exploded, human attention remained finite. A 2025 UN report cited by commenters noted that supply of entertainment vastly outstripped demand — not because people stopped consuming, but because platforms like Amazon, Google, and telecoms began subsidizing content not for its own sake, but to drive engagement with other products. This turned media into infrastructure, not art — and made budgets increasingly volatile.

Meanwhile, corporate decisions to relocate production — from Los Angeles to Georgia, Canada, or Hungary — were already eroding local creative ecosystems long before generative AI tools like Stable Diffusion launched. These moves cut costs but also fragmented workflows, weakened unions, and hollowed out mid-tier roles in editing, design, and post-production. As one commenter observed, moving production ‘to save money’ triggers cascading effects across support industries, from catering to equipment rental.

AI’s impact is real but narrow and uneven: commercial illustration, product photography, and basic motion graphics are now routinely automated — especially in advertising, where rapid iteration and low margins make AI tools irresistible. Yet high-end film, narrative TV, and bespoke creative work remain largely human-led. The timing of job losses also predates mainstream AI adoption in many cases, aligning more closely with streaming consolidation and pandemic-era budget cuts than with LLM or diffusion model releases.

Ultimately, blaming AI alone risks obscuring harder truths: that creative labor has been devalued for years, that attention is monetized at the expense of sustainability, and that corporate logic treats culture as expendable overhead. Recognizing this complexity is the first step toward building policies — from antitrust enforcement to public media investment — that protect both workers and the cultural commons.
