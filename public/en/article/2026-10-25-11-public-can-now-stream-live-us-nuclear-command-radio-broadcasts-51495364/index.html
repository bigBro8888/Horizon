Public can now stream live US nuclear command radio broadcasts

News time: 2026-10-25T06:02:51.045197+00:00 · Source: hackernews

A new open-source intelligence (OSINT) project called missileer.watch offers live audio streaming and archival access to Emergency Action Messages (EAMs) — highly structured, preformatted military communications used by the U.S. nuclear command-and-control system. These messages are broadcast around the clock on the high-frequency (HF) radio frequency 11175 kHz, part of the High Frequency Global Communications System (HFGCS), a legacy network operated by the U.S. Air Force since the Cold War. The site does not decrypt or interpret the content; it simply captures, timestamps, and archives the raw audio — making visible what has long been an invisible but critical layer of strategic deterrence infrastructure.

EAMs are not ordinary radio traffic. They follow strict formatting rules: typically 48 characters long, encoded for error resistance and rapid transmission, and designed to trigger specific nuclear force actions — from readiness alerts to execution orders — under OPLAN 8010-12, the current U.S. nuclear war plan. Though encrypted and unintelligible to outsiders, their cadence, duration, and timing carry operational meaning. As one observer noted, "The day has a shape" — quiet stretches punctuated by brief, unmistakable bursts of synthesized voice, often preceded by automated call-ups and authentication sequences.

The 11175 kHz channel is powerful and persistent: a 100 kW transmitter broadcasting 24/7, primarily during daylight hours (with fallback frequencies like 4724 kHz at night). It serves aircraft, ground stations, and naval assets, acting as an HF gateway into classified networks like SIPRNet. While most transmissions are routine voice or ALE (Automatic Link Establishment) handshakes, EAMs stand out due to their rigid structure and infrequency — sometimes going days without activity, then spiking before major geopolitical events, as users reported ahead of the Kerch Bridge incident in Ukraine.

What makes missileer.watch notable isn’t decryption capability — none exists publicly — but transparency through observation. It turns a black-box system into something audibly measurable: message count, timing, duration, and even historical quirks, like pop-culture test messages ('THE CURE', 'RUSH') sent during Skyking exercises. These anomalies remind us that even the most solemn systems rely on human-designed protocols — and occasionally, human humor. For researchers, educators, and concerned citizens, the site offers a rare, real-time window into the enduring architecture of nuclear deterrence — not as theory, but as sound.
