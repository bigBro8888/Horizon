FreeDNS remains a trusted free dynamic DNS service after two decades

News time: 2026-10-25T06:02:51.045217+00:00 · Source: hackernews

For over 20 years, FreeDNS (freedns.afraid.org) has quietly powered countless development experiments, home labs, and low-budget web projects — not with flashy marketing or venture capital, but with uptime, simplicity, and zero cost. Unlike many ephemeral free tools that vanish after a year or two, FreeDNS has persisted through shifts in web infrastructure, browser security models, and developer tooling. Its interface remains deliberately minimal: users register subdomains like myproject.freemyip.com, configure automatic IP updates via HTTP requests or client scripts, and point devices or services to those names — all without paying a cent.

The service’s longevity is matched by its utility. Developers use it to expose local servers during testing, hobbyists host personal dashboards or IoT interfaces behind residential ISPs, and educators deploy temporary domains for student assignments. As one longtime user noted, it helped launch an entire web career for a broke early-20s developer — a testament to how foundational accessible infrastructure can be. Its reliability isn’t accidental: FreeDNS offers optional backup DNS servers, enabling users to run their own primary DNS while relying on FreeDNS as a fallback — a rare feature among free offerings.

Yet its age also reveals technical friction points. A recurring concern raised by users is FreeDNS’s absence from the Public Suffix List (PSL), a critical dataset used by browsers to determine cookie scoping and domain isolation. Because FreeDNS subdomains (e.g., test.freemyip.com) aren’t recognized as independent registrable domains, browsers may treat them as part of a shared suffix — allowing cookies set on one subdomain to leak to others. This undermines security assumptions baked into modern web applications, especially those handling authentication or sensitive data.

That limitation doesn’t invalidate FreeDNS — but it does require awareness. It reflects a broader tension in internet infrastructure: long-standing, community-run tools often lag behind evolving standards not due to negligence, but because updating such systems demands coordination across maintainers, browsers, and standards bodies. Meanwhile, alternatives like nic.eu.org (for free domains) or dns.he.net (for DNS hosting) offer complementary capabilities — yet none replicate FreeDNS’s combination of simplicity, stability, and zero-cost accessibility. In an era of subscription fatigue and vendor lock-in, FreeDNS endures not as a relic, but as a quietly vital piece of the open web’s scaffolding.
